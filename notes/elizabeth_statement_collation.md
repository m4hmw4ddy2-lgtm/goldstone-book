## THE HEADLINE FINDING

**There are three reprints, all abridged differently, and none of them is the original.**

Length of the statement body, from "During our five years" to "the most awful distress", counted the same
way in each:

| | words |
|---|---|
| Essex Weekly News | 1,309 |
| Western Daily Press | 1,075 |
| Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian | 804 |

**The Essex Weekly News is the fullest and is the copy text** until the original is found. The Western
Daily Press cuts one long block and marks the cut with an ellipsis. The Guardian cuts five passages and
marks nothing. Where two or three print the same passage they agree closely, and the differences are of the
kind compositors introduce.

## WHAT THE GUARDIAN CUTS, AND THE WESTERN DAILY PRESS KEEPS

Five passages, all in the Essex Weekly News, all absent from the Guardian. The Western Daily Press keeps
the first four.

1. **The church-going, and how she managed him.** "That was the only way in which he ever spoke of the
   burthen of the family. Before we had the first child, and right on till the birth of the second, we
   regularly went to Church. The second child was weakly, and the first was still only a baby, so that we
   could not leave the house together. For all his occasional absent ways he was very lively, and as I had
   all to do with the spending of the money, and did not let him know if there was any trouble about
   payments when there was a confinement or sickness in the house, I always found it very easy to turn his
   attention to something brighter."
2. **His solitude.** "He was a man who had few acquaintances except his own relatives and his
   fellow-workmen, and he was nearly always at home when not at work, except on the Sunday morning when he
   occasionally took a walk alone."
3. **The children and the concertina, and his fear of his own mind.** "The children were very fond of him,
   and never tired listening to the concertina. If he seemed worried, amusing the children, at which he was
   very good, or a remark from me would at once change his mood. He was painfully aware of how easy it was
   for him to get worried, and he has often said to me that it was a good job he had not much to think of,
   as if he had he believed it would turn his brain. Nothing that he ever said or did before the loss of
   the little ones gave me the least fear that he would take his own life or that of anyone else."
4. **Her answer to his worry.** "But then lots of people manage on less, and we did very well, and when I
   put this before him he ceased to be moody and absent."
5. **The longest cut, and the only one the Western Daily Press also drops:** the children's ages, the
   morning tea, the birth, and the doorway. "You see the eldest was 3 1/2, bright and healthy, but the
   next, 2 1/2, was sickly and could not walk, and the third, being only 16 months, I had practically two
   babies to mind when the twins were born. But for all this there was no change in his kindly ways to me
   and the children. He got up early in the morning of the day when the twins were born, and got me a cup
   of tea, as he often did before, and did every morning from their birth till their death. The thing that
   struck me most was that during all this time he never took any notice of the babies. They were born
   about half-past ten, and he immediately went out of the house, and did not return till after 12. He then
   came in the room to me, but did not speak. His appearance seemed changed, and his eyes were strange and
   wandering. He came home early on the day he attacked the poor things, and I asked him if he had told his
   foreman about the twins, as he had lived with him once, and we looked upon him as one of our own family,
   but he said he had not."

**That last passage carries the morning tea, the wandering eyes, and Elizabeth's own version of the
doorway question about Graves.** Of the three reprints it survives only in the Essex Weekly News, and the
Western Daily Press prints an ellipsis where it should be. Anything drawn from it must cite the Essex
Weekly News by name, not "her statement" generically.

## THREE CLAIMS IN THE FIRST VERSION OF THIS FILE THAT WERE WRONG

All three were artefacts of our own transcription of the Essex Weekly News, not differences between the
papers. Each has now been checked against the source image and against the Western Daily Press, and the
transcription has been corrected with a note at its foot.

1. **"any insanity on the family".** The earlier version of this file recorded "on" as the Essex Weekly
   News's reading and recommended keeping it because "the idiom is hers". The page reads **in**. So does
   the Western Daily Press. So does the Guardian. The phrase had been quoted as "on the family" in
   `notes/elizabeth_thread_presence_as_absence.md`, which is corrected.
2. **"He realises in so far as he believes he will die".** The earlier version concluded that the Essex
   Weekly News had "dropped two words" and the Guardian preserved them. It had not; we had. The page reads
   "He realises **his position** in so far as…"
3. **"the times I found him sitting thinking".** Recorded as an Essex Weekly News variant against the
   Guardian's "the times when I found him". The page has "when".

The general lesson is worth the line: a collation of two transcriptions is a collation of two
transcriptions. Where a variant looks substantive, go to the image before drawing anything from it.

## THE DATE — NOW AS FIRM AS IT CAN BE WITHOUT THE ORIGINAL

- Her Chelmsford visit was **Tuesday 18 September**. All three texts agree it was a Tuesday, and the
  Guardian's own news column independently reports "The prisoner was visited on Tuesday by the High
  Sheriff… and also by his wife, mother, and sister."
- **Two of the three reprints keep "yesterday (Tuesday)"** — the Western Daily Press and the Guardian. The
  Essex Weekly News, publishing on the Friday, normalised it to "on Tuesday last", which is what a weekly
  does; and it kept "yesterday" four sentences later, in "His sister and aunt accompanied me yesterday",
  where it forgot to.
- A paper writing "yesterday (Tuesday)" was published on **Wednesday 19 September 1883**.

**Conclusion: the statement was given on or just before Wednesday 19 September and first published that
day, in a London daily not yet identified.** The note logged on 22 September giving Thursday 20 September
as first publication is superseded; it rested on Grantham Robinson's "I have just read Mrs. Gouldstone's
letter in your paper this morning" in a letter dated the 20th, which is satisfied by his having read a
Wednesday paper, or a Thursday reprint, a day later.

## WILLIAM'S LETTER OF 15 SEPTEMBER — A WARNING THAT STANDS

The three papers print his letter with **different misspellings**, in exactly the places where the
letter's apparent illiteracy shows. Essex Weekly News against Guardian: Dreadful/Dreadfull, Happy/Happey,
Recive/Receive, than/then, Belive/Believe, Chappeling/Chapeling, shell/shall. The Western Daily Press
agrees with the Essex Weekly News on "shell" and with the Guardian on "Dreadfull", so it follows neither.

**The spelling in that letter is the compositor's, not reliably William's.** Three printings of one
holograph disagree, and the capitalisation differs throughout as well. If the letter is quoted in the book,
the prose must follow one named printing and say so, or regularise, and it must not rest any argument
about his literacy on how the words are spelled. What all three agree on is the substance, the phrasing
and the rhythm, which are the things that carry it.
