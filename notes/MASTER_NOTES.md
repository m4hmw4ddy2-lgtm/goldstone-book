## PART ONE: THE BOOK

### The hook
On the evening of 8 August 1883, a whitesmith named William Gouldstone came home early from work in London. His wife Elizabeth had just given birth to twins. He murdered all five of his children and told the arresting officer he was a happy man. He was wrong. Because of what he did that evening, one hundred and forty years later, a man named Richard Ferguson exists.

### Structure — confirmed
Prologue + 12 chapters + Coda, organised into six parts:

- **Prologue:** 8 August 1883, real time through Ada Hamilton's testimony. Ends on locked door.
- **Part One: Before** — Essex origins. William and Elizabeth's backgrounds. Great Sampford and Wethersfield. The migration to London. The deterioration.
- **Part Two: The Evening** — return to the prologue and complete it. The cistern. Charles. The locked door. The hammer. What Elizabeth said when the door opened.
- **Part Three: The Reckoning** — inquest, trial, medical debate, reprieve, Broadmoor, copycat murder.
- **Part Four: Elizabeth** — survival, the four missing years, Lambeth, Albert's birth, Whitstable, William Madams.
- **Part Five: Albert** — the Germany lie, the Navy, the wars, sixty-one years of fabrication.
- **Part Six: The Researcher** — the blank space on the birth certificate, the thread, the causal chain understood.
- **Coda** — William Gouldstone is not Rik's ancestor. He is the reason Rik exists. The Broadmoor files open in 2036.

### The central argument
If William Gouldstone does not murder his five children on 8 August 1883, Elizabeth remains in a functioning marriage. She does not disappear to Lambeth. She does not meet William Madams, or if she meets him she does not form a relationship with him. Albert is never born. Vera Georgina Goldstone is never born. Michael George Ferguson is never born. Rik is never born.

William Gouldstone is not Rik's ancestor. He is the reason Rik exists.

## PART TWO: THE PROLOGUE — FINAL TEXT

When William Gouldstone came home early on the evening of Wednesday 8 August 1883, Elizabeth was still upstairs in the back room, seven days delivered of twins and not yet recovered enough to leave her bed. She heard him enter, but it was Ada Hamilton, the monthly nurse, who went through to the kitchen and found him standing by the dresser. William was a whitesmith, and his usual hour of return was half past seven. It was only a quarter past five. Ada told him that he had frightened her. She had wondered who was there. He smiled but made no reply.

A little later he came to the bedside and stood there without speaking. Elizabeth asked how it was that he was home so early, and he replied that he had left when the manager went to dinner. It was an answer that belonged to an ordinary working day. Elizabeth had no reason to know it was false, but he had not been at the works since the Bank Holiday. When she asked whether he had told them at the shop that she was confined, he said simply, "No."

She was asking from the bed, seven days after giving birth to twins, with five children under four and no room in the household for a lost wage.

Frederick, sixteen months old, was on the bed. William took him up and carried him into the kitchen, where Charles and Herbert were. The rooms were close together, but from Elizabeth's bed the kitchen was out of sight, and beyond the kitchen was the scullery. Ada remained with Elizabeth and the twins. There was still work to do; she had yet to wash the second baby.

In the kitchen, when Ada next saw him, William was sitting on the fire guard, the three boys playing at his feet, turning and tying a piece of clothesline around and over his fingers. She asked whether he had had his tea, and when he said he had not, she told him it was ready. He made no reply.

In the scullery, Ada noticed that the perambulator had been moved from its usual place by the cistern, and a chair placed there instead. The cistern, running the length of the room, was fixed high up, close to the ceiling, beyond the reach of a small child standing on the floor. When she asked who had moved the chair, it was Charles who answered, "My daddy put it there." He was three and a half, old enough to notice a change in the room but too young to know that his answer had any importance.

Ada went back to the bedroom. Elizabeth had to be lifted while the bed was made. When William put his head in the doorway, she told him, "You can come in, Mr Gouldstone." At that he turned back towards the kitchen, mumbling something to himself which she could not understand.

Ada suggested to Elizabeth that she might leave the room to give William an opportunity, if he wished, of speaking to her privately, and went downstairs. The older boys were no longer where she could see them. From below, she heard him open the kitchen door and, later, go into the bedroom she had just left.

For a short while, Elizabeth was alone in the back room with William and the twins. As Ada reached the yard door, she heard screams of "Murder!" and knew immediately that the voice was Elizabeth's. She ran back upstairs; the bedroom door was locked from the inside. Elizabeth cried out for the door to be opened, that he was murdering her children. She was screaming from the room in which the two newborn babies lay.

*I wasn't looking for William Gouldstone. I was looking for someone else entirely, a great-grandfather I already had a name for. But this is the story I found instead, waiting underneath the one I thought I wanted. A murder that horrified a nation, and without which I would not exist.*

**Status: Settled.**

### Prologue decisions — current
- Opens on Elizabeth's physical state and Ada as the point-of-contact, not on William's premeditation directly — the reader arrives at the deception through Ada and Elizabeth, not through narratorial assertion.
- "He had left when the manager went to dinner" — paraphrase of William's answer, sidesteps the unresolved reading of the deposition/witness-statement wording around "situation" (see Section 3 below) without asserting a disputed reading in the prose.
- "Since the Bank Holiday" replaces an earlier "three days" framing that had incorrectly folded Monday 6 August (Bank Holiday, nobody was at work that day) into his irregular absence. The genuine anomaly is Tuesday 7th and Wednesday 8th only.
- Frederick's move from bed to kitchen now has an explicit transition ("William took him up and carried him into the kitchen"), closing a gap present in earlier drafts.
- Chair/cistern quote: "My daddy put it there" — CONFIRMED against coroner deposition, verbatim: *"I asked as to who had placed the chair there. William Gouldstone was within hearing. Charles Gouldstone replied 'My Daddy put it there.'"* The detail that William was within hearing but did not answer is sourced and sits in the confirmed facts file, but deliberately left out of the Prologue prose itself — too didactic, spells out what the reader can see for themselves in a three-year-old answering while his father stands there.
- "You can come in, Mr Gouldstone" — CONFIRMED against coroner deposition, verbatim: *"I said to him 'You can come in Mr Goldstone.'"* Note: deposition spells the name "Goldstone" in this instance. Editorial decision: normalised to "Gouldstone" in the Prologue prose for consistency with the rest of the book's spelling convention. This is a conscious silent correction inside a direct quotation — flagged here rather than left undocumented, per the project's verbatim-first principle. If a future editorial pass wants the deposition's own spelling preserved inside the quote marks, that's a one-word reversal.
- "Tying it" (the clothesline) — CONFIRMED against coroner deposition, verbatim: *"I noticed he had a piece of clothes line in his hand tying it. The piece produced is either the same or similar to it."* Not an inference; her word.
- Twins are male throughout. Em-dashes: none present. Four closing sentences in Rik's own voice, as required.

## PART THREE: THE CRIME — CONFIRMED FACTS

### The people in the house, 8 August 1883
- **William Gouldstone** (26), whitesmith, husband and father
- **Elizabeth Gouldstone** (28), seven days post-partum with twins
- **Charles Thomas Gouldstone** (3½), eldest boy — in kitchen
- **Herbert Ernest Gouldstone** (2½), second boy — in kitchen
- **Frederick Gouldstone** (16 months), third boy — in bedroom on the bed; taken to kitchen by William
- **Twin boys** (7 days old), unnamed in the record
- **Ada Hamilton**, monthly nurse, lodging on same floor as Gouldstones with husband William Hamilton (jeweller)
- **William Hamilton** (jeweller), Ada's husband — present in the building; gave no testimony at any hearing; invisible in all press reporting
- **Emma Clarke**, wife of **Charles Clarke** (carpenter — CONFIRMED; press accounts giving "James" are errors), head tenant of the house — in the garden

### The house — confirmed
**Address:** 8 Courtenay Place, St James's Street, Walthamstow. Also known as 2 Helen Cottages, Queens Road, West Walthamstow — same building, confirmed as one address not two locations.

**What it was:** One of **thirteen bay-fronted two-storey cottages** on the east side of Courtenay Place, facing St James's Street station platforms directly. Confirmed from OS 25" map (1891-5 revision, National Library of Scotland). Matching bay-fronted terrace on the High Street — same speculative development, mirror image. Both terraces built approximately 1881 by unknown speculative builder to exploit the railway. The IPN called it "one of the first of its row" — most likely at the southern, station end of the terrace. "Parallel with St James's Street Railway Station" — confirmed from map; the cottages faced directly onto the platforms.

**What stands there now:** Bus depot. Site fully demolished at unknown date.

**Internal layout — confirmed from testimony:**
- Ground floor: Clarke territory (probable but not confirmed in any source — Emma was in the garden)
- First floor: Gouldstone accommodation
  - Back room (bedroom — Ada's coroner deposition uses "back room," not "bedroom")
  - Passage connecting back room to kitchen ("came along the passage and looked into the room")
  - Kitchen (fire guard, where the boys played, where William sat — William slept here during Elizabeth's confinement)
  - Kitchen door to scullery/washhouse ("saw the prisoner coming in at the kitchen door from the washhouse")
  - Scullery/washhouse (cistern, perambulator, chair placed under cistern)
  - Landing with banisters
- Same floor: Ada and William Hamilton in the front room (Ada's coroner deposition says "second floor" — see Contradictions; all other evidence says same floor)

**The cistern:** In the scullery/washhouse. Six feet from the floor. Ten inches from the ceiling. Fourteen inches of water. Ran the full length of the room.

**William slept in the kitchen** during Elizabeth's confinement — confirmed, Ada's coroner deposition.

**William smiled at Ada** when she spoke to him on arrival — "only smiled but made no reply" — coroner deposition only; no other source.

### The sequence — confirmed
**Boys drowned first. Twins attacked second.** Confirmed by multiple independent sources.

1. William arrives 5:15pm. Had not been at work. Not at home during the day either.
2. Ada in bedroom washing second twin. Charles and Herbert in kitchen. Frederick on the bed.
3. William in kitchen with boys, cord in his fingers, turning it. Said he hadn't had tea.
4. William takes Frederick from the bed to the kitchen. All three boys now in kitchen.
5. Ada goes to scullery, sees chair under cistern, perambulator moved. Charles: "My daddy put it there."
6. Ada back to bedroom. William appears in doorway, mumbles, returns to kitchen. Ada offers to leave. Elizabeth agrees.
7. Ada goes downstairs toward the yard door.
8. Ada hears "Murder!" screamed from above. Runs back up. Bedroom door locked.
9. Ada leans over banisters, calls for help. Emma Clarke runs up from garden.
10. William opens the door himself. Clarke's confirmed words from him: "You can come in, it is all done."
11. Ada sees twins on pillow, bleeding from nose and mouth.
12. Ada goes to kitchen; meets William coming from scullery carrying Frederick's wet body.
13. Three boys in a row on kitchen floor. Dr Twining present.
14. Cheeseman arrives. William stooping over boys. "Good evening, policeman. I have done it now. I am happy and ready for the rope."
15. William taken to station.

### William's words — in sequence
- Smiled at Ada on arrival; made no reply (coroner deposition — unique detail)
- On opening bedroom door: see Contradictions — multiple versions in sworn documents. Emma Clarke confirmed to her: "You can come in, it is all done."
- To Cheeseman on walk to station: "When I took my money last week I thought of buying a revolver to do it with, but I altered my mind, because I thought it would make too much noise. I had a hard job with the two biggest, but the other little one I soon settled him. I thought it was getting too hot to have five children in about three years and a half, so I thought I would put a stop to it."
- At station: "I've done it like a man."
- Police cell, c.6:45pm: "I have had an extra drop of drink to accomplish the job; there's five of them gone to glory, and it is a good job."
- Police cell, c.7:30pm: "I am sorry for my wife and parents, but the children will be better off in heaven, if there is such a place; it is better than leaving them to the mercy of the world."
- Police cell, c.9pm: "I wish I had killed the little ones out of the way; I don't know whether I hit them once or twice; I have had this preying on my mind for a long time." — The constable corrected this in cross-examination. The word was *preying*. Not *playing*.

**Note on the cord:** Almost certainly a noose — PC Honey under cross-examination: "I saw the noose on the string which was loose round the child's neck." The cord was almost certainly brought for William himself, not the children. He went out that morning intending to drown himself (BMJ 6 Oct). He came home having failed.

**Note on the drink claim:** William said he had had an extra drop of drink. Three independent police witnesses (Cheeseman, Folkard, Wheatley) observed no traces of drink. His claim is his alone.

**Note on "preying":** The constable corrected this in cross-examination. The word was *preying*, not *playing*.

### William went out that morning intending to drown himself
**Source:** BMJ Medical News, 6 October 1883.

He failed. He came home with a noose in his pocket. The chair under the cistern — was it first intended for himself? This reframes the entire sequence. He came home having failed to die. He came home to his five children.

### The days before the murders
- Twins born 1 August 1883.
- William showed signs of change from that moment. Graves noticed immediately.
- Bank Holiday Monday 6 August: not at work.
- Tuesday 7 August: absent from work AND did not remain at home.
- Wednesday 8 August: absent from work AND did not remain at home during the day.
- Went out intending to drown himself. Failed. Came home. Murdered the children.

### William's family history of mental illness
- Mother Emily: multiple sustained episodes of severe depression; multiple suicide attempts including attempting to strangle herself — "grain" is the Essex word for strangle, confirmed by a juror at the Old Bailey when Thomas used the term.
- Maternal aunt Mrs Andrews: same condition — "despondency," suicidal inclinations, ideas of ruin impending. Attended by Dr Sunderland of Thaxted.
- Neither woman ever certified insane or confined.
- Second cousin William Gouldstone (a soldier): confined Brentwood Lunatic Asylum from 21 April 1880, died there after 16 months.
- Thomas's father's sister: wore a straitjacket for years.
- Great-uncle: committed suicide.
- BMJ: "a terrible disease which has scourged his family for three generations."

### William's family background
- Father **Thomas Gouldstone** baptised 1 December 1821, St Michael's, Great Sampford, Essex. Father Joseph Gouldstone, mother Ruth. Archive ref: D/P 289/1/13. Blacksmith employing three men (1861); General Smith (1891). Died Q3 1899, Saffron Walden registration district, age 77.
- Mother **Emily Willett** born 1834, Wimbish, Essex. Married Thomas 30 January 1851. Died June 1913, Essex, age 79.
- William's siblings: Robert, Emily, Thomas, Bennett, Flora, Hugh C. Brother **Bennett also worked at the Falkirk Ironworks**.
- William and Elizabeth lived with foreman William Graves for approximately the first three years of their marriage. The three older boys — Charles, Herbert, Frederick — were born in Graves's house.
- Marriage certificate (15 March 1879): William listed as Iron Worker, age 22. Address 88 Frampton Park Road. Father Thomas Goldstone, Smith. Elizabeth age 24, same address, father Thomas Stock (decd). Witnesses: Thomas and Emily Goldstone.

### William's workplace
The Falkirk Ironworks, Upper Thames Street, London (Kirkley Wharf, 67 Upper Thames Street). A large firm with fully 1,000 workmen. William had been there approximately five years. His foreman William Graves had known William since birth.

Partners included Howard John Kinnaird (probably Kennard — possible transcription error). Kinnaird confirmed: "The whole feeling of his fellow-workmen is that he is certainly insane."

### The trial — 14 September 1883
- Prosecuted: Poland and Montagu Williams
- Defended: Grain and Elliott
- Expert witness: Dr George Henry Savage (examined William once, for between a quarter and half an hour, the day before trial)
- Judge: Mr Justice Day (applied narrow McNaughten Rules; ridiculed second cousin evidence; publicly questioned Savage's bona fides from the bench)
- Jury: approximately fifteen minutes deliberation. Guilty on all five counts.
- Sentence: Death.

Examining doctors sent after sentence: **Dr Clarke of London and Dr Orange of Broadmoor** (Sunday Dispatch, 30 September 1883 — NOT Dr Gover as the BMJ speculated on 29 September). Both examined William for six or seven hours at Chelmsford. Orange entertained no doubt of his insanity.

**Reprieve:** 3 October 1883. Home Secretary Sir William Harcourt. William received the news "with deep gratitude." Committed to Broadmoor 15 October 1883.

**Broadmoor:** "An ideal patient. He worked in the various wards." Died 19 January 1935. 52 years confined. Age 78 (Evening Standard said 80 — approximation).

**Broadmoor files:** Sealed until 2036 (101 years after death). Confirmed to contain family correspondence.

**Home Office depositions:** The original depositions (reference A31638) were retained at the Home Office from 1883 until 2 December 1901, when they were returned to the Central Criminal Court. They sat at the Home Office for 18 years.

### The copycat murder — James Cole, Thornton Heath
Approximately 19 August 1883. Cole read about the Gouldstone case and murdered his three-year-old son that evening. Tried and executed. BMJ: "the execution of a lunatic is generally followed by a copious crop of lunatic homicides."

### Rev. Thomas Harrison Grantham Robinson (1837–1884)
First Vicar of St Saviour's, Walthamstow, within the parish of St James's. Born Ireland, July 1837. Educated King's College London and University of Dublin. Ordained to Whitechapel; Secretary to the Prayer Book and Homily Society; assistant curate St Augustine's Highbury under Rev. Gordon Calthorp. In 1865 offered the living of St James's by the then Vicar of Walthamstow. St Saviour's church built through the munificence of Mr R. Foster.

Conducted the funeral of the five Gouldstone children 13 August 1883. Almost certainly the "Vicar of the parish in which he lived" (BMJ 29 September 1883) whose statement to the Home Secretary was cited as one of the factors contributing to the reprieve.

Preached Sunday 16 March 1884; visited parish Monday; felt unwell Wednesday; died Friday 21 March 1884, age 46. Seven months after the murders. Five months after the reprieve. He buried the children, wrote the letter, and was dead before any of the outcomes were fully known.

Character from obituary (*Church Bells and Illustrated Church News*, 1884): "Large-hearted, good humoured, and kind to all." Respected by Nonconformists — several nonconformist ministers attended his funeral. The poor mourned him as "a departed leader and friend." The Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian: "the beau ideal of a gentleman and an Englishman."

He earns a paragraph in Part Three.

### The Trial of Lunatics Act 1883
Passed the same year as the murders. Permitted a verdict of guilty but insane, with committal to Broadmoor at Her Majesty's Pleasure. William was one of the first major cases tried in its shadow.

Four articles constitute the BMJ's complete coverage of the case, September-October 1883. All transcribed and in `/transcribed/` folder.

Orange and Gover to examine William (NOTE: the BMJ was wrong about Gover — it was Dr Clarke of London and Dr Orange, per Sunday Dispatch 30 September). ## PART SIX: ELIZABETH — THE FOUR MISSING YEARS

### Albert's birth — confirmed from primary source
**Albert Jennet Goldstone** born 19 June 1887, Workhouse Infirmary, Brook Street, Lambeth. Registered 20 June 1887 by **J Frost** (infirmary staff member — almost certainly a trained nurse; the Brook Street infirmary trained its own staff), present at the birth.

Elizabeth's address at the time of the birth: **2 John Street, Hercules Buildings, Lambeth.**

Elizabeth was **not a workhouse inmate.** She had her own address. She came to the infirmary as a pauper patient through the relieving officer system specifically to deliver. Admissions to the Brook Street infirmary were by means of the relieving officer — the stigma of pauperism attached, but she was not resident in the workhouse.

The infirmary on Brook Street (now Brook Drive) was approximately **half a mile** from her address at John Street. Not adjacent — she travelled to deliver.

### The address
2 John Street, Hercules Buildings, Lambeth. John Street ran parallel to the railway line, adjoining Hercules Terrace. In 1893 John Street, Short Street, and Hercules Terrace were merged and renamed **Newnham Terrace**. The street no longer exists — the site is now the **Park Plaza Hotel Waterloo**. The **William Blake plaque** at 23 Hercules Buildings (Blake lived there 1793-1821) is directly opposite the hotel, which corroborates the location.

### The infirmary
Brook Street (now Brook Drive), Lambeth. Built approximately 1876-1879. Separate institution from the adjacent Renfrew Road workhouse — separate building, separate entrance. Had a dedicated Lying-in Ward. Trained its own nurses. Admissions through the relieving officer. [Mrs Emma Brewer's account, 1890, workhouses.org.uk] Charlie Chaplin was briefly a workhouse (not infirmary) inmate in 1896 — nine years after Albert was born at the adjacent infirmary. Site largely demolished; former Master's House (1871) and water tower survive; Cinema Museum now in Master's House.

### Albert's name
- **Albert**: no family precedent identified
- **Jennet**: origin unknown. No family precedent found in Gouldstone, Stock, Suckling, or Crawley lines. Open question.
- **Goldston/Goldstone**: Elizabeth gave her married name (a version of Gouldstone), not Stock. The spelling "Goldston" on the birth certificate is the registrar's rendering — the significant fact is the choice of root name, not the precise spelling.

### The family from Whitstable
Elizabeth cohabited with William James Madams in Whitstable from approximately 1888. No marriage certificate found — Elizabeth could not legally marry while Gouldstone was alive in Broadmoor.

- **Lilian Florence Isabel Madams** born 7 April 1890, Whitstable
- **Eliza Jane Madams** born 16 September 1891, Whitstable — birth certificate lists Elizabeth as "Elizabeth Madams, late Goldstone formerly Stock" — all three identities in one document. Married Percy Coombs. Emigrated to New Zealand 29 June 1927, SS Ionic, with husband and seven children. Died 1 April 1976, Rangimarie Home, New Plymouth, aged 84.

William James Madams admitted to **Chartham** Asylum (not Chatham — Chartham, near Canterbury), Kent, 4 September 1901 (National Archives MH 94/37). Died 23 November 1901. Albert had already enlisted before Madams was admitted.

Elizabeth Stock died July 1906, registered Blean district, Kent (not a confirmed specific place — see Part Nine and dramatis_personae.md), aged 51. She did not live to see William Gouldstone die in 1935.

### Elizabeth's maternal line and passage to London — see dramatis_personae.md for full detail
Fully researched 14 July 2026 (see notes/session_update_2026-07-14.md and session_update_2026-07-14_part2.md for the full working record). Summary: Elizabeth was one of Jemima's two sets of children, not an only child — Jemima's first marriage (to John Ansell, 1833–1845) produced seven children, six of whom survived infancy, making Elizabeth's known half-siblings six rather than the single "Arthur" previously recorded. Elizabeth's own route into London domestic service (1871 census, Stoke Newington) runs alongside a fellow Wethersfield-born servant, Susan Newman, in the same household — the likely mechanism, though how Elizabeth reached William Gouldstone specifically (Stoke Newington to Hackney, 1871–1879) remains an open, unrecorded gap. Thomas Stock's own first marriage (to a "Sarah Hockley") could not be confirmed against a background of at least three namesake Thomas Stocks in the same parish, and has been deliberately left as a flagged, open uncertainty rather than pursued further — it is not load-bearing for the book's argument.
