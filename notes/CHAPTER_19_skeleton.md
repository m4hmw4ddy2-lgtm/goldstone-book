## FINDINGS

1. **The first Whitstable document is the 1891 census.** With "c. 1888" withdrawn and Lilian's certificate not held,
   nothing places Elizabeth in Whitstable before Sunday 5 April 1891. Between Chapter Eighteen's last line (20 June
   1887) and the census: nothing in the repo.
2. **Her names in this chapter, in order:** "Elizabeth do" [Madams], wife (1891 census); "Elizabeth Madams late
   Goldstone formerly Stock" (1891, her own registration); "the mother of the child", unnamed (1896); "Elizabeth do"
   [Madams], wife (census, Sunday 31 March 1901); "Elizabeth Goldstone, of Middle Wall" (Tuesday 2 April 1901, court and
   paper); "Elizabeth Madams", "Widow of William Madams" (1906, her daughter). Who gave the 1901 court the name
   "Goldstone" is not recorded. Inference only, not for prose: the paper ties her to "the boy in custody", who was
   charged as "Albert Goldstone", and the name may have come with him. The plan's "four names" (thread section) are
   now six, given by others except once.
3. **Albert's names, which move with hers:** "Albert Goldstone", Stepson (1891); "his son" in Madams's summons (1896);
   "Albert Madams" to the School Board (1898, 1899); "Albert do" [Madams], Son (census, 31 March 1901); "Albert
   Goldstone" in every 1901 court report (two days later). The 1896 "his son" is the reporter's wording of a summons in
   Madams's name: a third reading for Contradiction 88, not a document of the household's own.
4. **Two days in 1901 hold the whole question of names.** On Sunday the census enters the household as Madams, Albert
   as "Son". On Monday the mother goes to the police station for "the boy"; on Tuesday mother and son are both before
   magistrates as Goldstone. The record does this without any sentence of ours.
5. **"His father wanted 3s."** The first words in the record attributed to Albert, at second hand through Pout, two
   days after "his own boots ... given to him" and the day before the census. Who "his father" was is not said. Not
   for prose as fact; quotable as the Folkestone Chronicle's report of Pout's evidence (DECIDE C).
6. **The register's "E. Madams, Mother" and the court's "the mother of the child" (1896) are the only two places after
   1883 where she acts for a child.** The 1901 police station is a third, in the Canterbury Journal's sequence ("when
   she heard that he had been locked up she went"), and stays the paper's.
7. **Rooms.** Two in 1891 (four people); fewer than five in 1901 (five people; the page total box counts every
   schedule on the page as under five rooms). No exact number for 1901.
8. **Madams's death certificate has no family informant**: the asylum's clerk and steward registered it. Her death
   certificate's informant is her daughter. Nothing to say about either in our voice.
9. **Edwin Johnson** registered both Eliza Jane's birth (1891, from Elizabeth) and Elizabeth's death (1906, from
   Lilian). A small district; nothing to make of it.
10. **Things not to make anything of:** "Oct" struck through on Eliza Jane's certificate; the margin's "Four E.J.";

    from a ChatGPT session, that Madams had two earlier sons, George (1885) and Arthur (1880). Unsourced. Neither would
    have been eight in 1896, so it does not touch the identification of "his son aged eight" as Albert.
12. **The Warspite and Impregnable are two ships, seven months apart**, with nothing documented between (live_lists 4,
    item 45; DO NOT USE, live_lists 5). What the Warspite was is general knowledge without a source in the repo.
    UPDATED 10 October 2026 (Rik's lead, Wikipedia "HMS Warspite (1807)"; then two web pages, read the same day, nothing
    saved to the repo): **the 1807 Warspite is NOT Albert's ship.** She was lent to the Marine Society as a boys'
    training ship in 1862, moored on the Thames between Woolwich and Charlton, and was destroyed by fire on 3 January
    1876 (Wikipedia, citing Ballantyne 2001, Lavery 1983, Glasgow Herald 4 January 1876). The Warspite of 1901 was her
    replacement: the old two-decker launched as Waterloo (1833), renamed Conqueror (1862), renamed Warspite on 11
    August 1876 as the Marine Society's training ship off Woolwich, "for training boys for Royal Navy and Merchant
    Service" (1890 entry), "Moved to Greenhithe, Kent" in 1901 (month not given), burnt 20 January 1918 and replaced by
    the cruiser Hermione, renamed Warspite (pdavis.nl, ShowShip id 1268, a ship database; Children's Homes site,
    "Training Ship 'Warspite', Woolwich, Kent", for the 1918 fire and the Marine Society). STATUS: secondary web sources
    only; NOT for prose. Before any sentence about the ship: a Navy List of 1901 (lent ships) or the Marine Society's
    own records. Consequences: (a) in April 1901 she lay off Woolwich or at Greenhithe, which is not yet known; (b) the
    1890 entry's "Royal Navy and Merchant Service" fits a route to Impregnable but does not document Albert's (DO NOT
    USE stands); (c) the Children's Homes page's admission rules (ages 14 to 16 and a half, indentures, a fee) are for
    1911 and are not to be carried back to 1901; (d) the register lead for item 45: the Marine Society's archive (MSY), on loan to the
    National Maritime Museum, Greenwich, with registers of boys admitted 1889 to 1958; not online (CORRECTED 10
    October 2026; the Children's Homes page had pointed to the Society itself, 202 Lambeth Road). DECIDE F (a) unchanged:
    nothing on what the ship was.

## THE ELIZABETH THREAD

Part Four inherits the thread inverted (thread file, section 6): no one writes about her. In Chapter Nineteen that
changes twice, and both times in a register far from 1883's "the poor woman". In 1896 a reporter records her in court,
unnamed, pleading poverty: she speaks before a bench thirteen years after she was never called. In 1901 a court
paragraph describes her, once, drunk at a police station on the day her son is locked up. Between them stands the one
document in which she gives her own history: "Elizabeth Madams late Goldstone formerly Stock". The chapter's work is to
set these down in order, the papers named, and to let the names do what the thread file asks of them (section 6: "Let
the pronoun do it"). In our narration she is Elizabeth throughout; the documents call her what they call her. Nothing
of what she felt, chose, or knew. The echoes (her mother's "late Ansell formerly Suckling", Chapter One; "present at
the birth" in Chapter Eighteen against "present at death" here; the bench that heard her in 1896 against the inquest
that did not) are the record's, and are left for the reader: no sentence of ours points at any of them.
