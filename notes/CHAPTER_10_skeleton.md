## WHAT CHANGED IN THE FIRST REVISION

The first version of this skeleton was written from the notes. Every date and every claim in it has now been
checked against a named source. The sequence holds. Four things in it did not.

1. **The post-mortems are datable.** They were open in the first draft ("the 9th or the 10th"). Gould's
   coroner deposition, read from the manuscript image, gives Thursday 9 August, 3.30 p.m. for all five and a
   second sitting at 4.30 p.m. for the second infant. The Pall Mall Gazette of 9 August, an evening paper,
   puts the Stratford hearing that same **morning**. Contradiction 45.
2. **"Twelve men and a coroner" went on the view.** No source says twelve. The Daily Telegraph says the
   coroner went "with some of the jurymen". Contradiction 47. Corrected in the Elizabeth thread file too.
3. **The Daily Chronicle and the Dover Express are one text**, not two sources, for the Saturday hearing.
   The Chronicle of 13 August is the earlier printing and the one to name. Contradiction 46.
4. **The grave was not covered in flowers when the jury sat down.** The burial was at noon, the grave was
   filled in at three, and the flowers went on after that — both papers agree. The image the first draft
   called "the chapter" is not sourced in the form it was written. Contradiction 48, and see below.

## THE WEEK, PINNED TO SOURCES

- **Wednesday 8 August.** The murders. The arrest. Both twins dead by dawn. (Chapters Seven to Nine.)
- **Thursday 9 August, morning.** 8.30 a.m., charged with all five (Folkard). Stratford Petty Sessions,
  first appearance: "examined at the Stratford petty sessions this morning" (Pall Mall Gazette, 9 August).
  Before Mr Arthur Lister and Mr W. W. Glenny. Atkinson for the defence. Ada gives evidence and sobs through
  it; William "now and then smiled when certain actions of his were described by the witness" (Daily
  Telegraph, 10 August). Lister: **"Then the prisoner will be remanded till Saturday, and in the meantime
  further inquiries will be made."** Ada's JP witness statement is sworn this day.
- **Thursday 9 August, afternoon.** Gould makes the post-mortem examination of all five children at 3.30
  p.m., and of the second infant again at 4.30 (coroner deposition, manuscript image 0039).
- **Friday 10 August, afternoon.** The inquest opens **at the board room of the Walthamstow Cemetery**,
  before Mr Charles Carne Lewis, coroner for the southern division of Essex. Inspector Craggs watches for
  the Commissioners of Police. The jury first views the five bodies "in the mortuary close by". The coroner
  charges them to "dismiss from their minds anything and everything they might either have heard or read
  relative to the case". Three witnesses: Ada Hamilton, Emma Clarke, PC Cheeseman. Ada "repeated the
  evidence she gave on the previous day before the magistrates" — which independently confirms the Thursday
  hearing. **A juryman asks the question that produces Emma Clarke's "I have heard the mother say she wished
  the children dead"**; the coroner "did not think anything would turn on that". Adjourned. The coroner makes
  out the orders for the burials, then goes with **some of the jurymen** to view the house. Elizabeth is in
  the front room. Blyth is not among them. Ada's coroner deposition is sworn this day, the day after her
  witness statement.
- **Saturday 11 August, morning.** Second Stratford hearing. Two of his brothers visit him in the cells
  before it (IPN, 18 August, press-tier). The crowd at the wrong station; the half-mile walk; the hooting;
  Ada recalled and her deposition read over; the cord held up and William pointing to his throat; the hammer
  produced; Inspector Craggs asking a week because "The Treasury will take up the prosecution"; Atkinson
  deferring his cross-examination; remanded a week; the escape through the coffee shop and the crowd's short
  cut to the station. Daily Chronicle, 13 August (reprinted by the Dover Express a week later).
- **Sunday 12 August, morning.** Blyth at Elizabeth's bedside.
- **Monday 13 August, noon.** The funeral, moved forward three hours.
- **Monday 13 August, afternoon.** The resumed inquest, **at the lodge** of the same cemetery: Lyle's
  protest, Blyth's admission, Smith, Bryant, Folkard, Gould, the summing-up, the verdict of wilful murder,
  five verdicts signed, the coroner's warrant. Outside, at three o'clock, the grave is filled in; soon after
  it is covered with flowers; people come and go between the mound and the lodge door all afternoon.

**Still open: board room against lodge.** The opening is reported at the board room and the resumption at
"the small lodge". One building with a room in it, or two, is not established. Do not let the prose move
between them until it is. The Walthamstow Burial Board's own records, or the Walthamstow Guardian of 11
August, would settle it.

## THE FACT THE CHAPTER IS BUILT ON

They buried five children at noon on Monday the thirteenth of August, and that same afternoon, a few yards
away in the same cemetery, a jury sat down to decide how they had died. The grave was filled in at three
o'clock while the inquest was still sitting. The crowd moved between the two all afternoon.

That is the chapter and it needs no commentary at all. **What it cannot say** is that the grave was already
flower-covered when the jury was sworn — the flowers went on after three (Contradiction 48). The Dover
Express's sentence, used as the paper's own and attributed, carries the whole thing without our having to
date anything: "Crowds of people congregated in the cemetery, many inspecting the flower-covered grave of
the five children, and others lingering outside the small lodge while the inquest was going on."

## THE SHAPE — six movements, chronological

**1. Thursday 9 August: Stratford, and then the mortuary.** Charged with all five at half past eight; in a
dock by the middle of the morning. The book's first sight of the machinery. Ada gives evidence for the
first of four times, and this is the version sworn earliest. He smiles at parts of it. Lister remands him
till Saturday. Then, at half past three the same afternoon, Gould opens all five of them, and again at half
past four. The day ends on a table, not in a dock.
**DECIDED 22 September 2026 (Rik): stage the act here, hold the findings for the Monday.** The mortuary
scene gets the hour, the sequence and the fact of a second sitting an hour after the first. No medical
detail here; it arrives in Gould's own words at the resumed inquest, where a room has to hear it. (The
first draft recommended holding the post-mortems back altogether because they could not be dated. They
can: 3.30 and 4.30 p.m., Thursday, Contradiction 45. That recommendation is withdrawn.) Watch in drafting:
the mortuary now appears twice in one chapter, which was the standing objection to this choice; the second
appearance is testimony, not a scene, and should stay that way. Note what the geography gives for
nothing: the mortuary is "close by" the board room where the inquest opened the next day, and both are
in the cemetery the children were buried in on the Monday. The Thursday afternoon, the Friday afternoon and
the Monday all happen on the same few acres of ground.

**2. Friday 10 August: the inquest opens, and the house is walked through.** The bodies viewed in the
mortuary before a word of evidence. The coroner telling the jury to forget everything they have read, in
the middle of the loudest press week of the year. (The jury's size is not stated in any source found; do
not supply a number.) Ada, Emma Clarke, Cheeseman. **The jury asking after
Elizabeth's words and getting them** — "I have heard the mother say she wished the children dead" — and the
coroner putting it aside. Then the burial orders signed in the cemetery, and the coroner and some of his
jurymen walking to the house to see whether three children could be drowned in that cistern. Elizabeth is
through the wall. End the movement on the coroner's own defence of the visit, given three days later: "so
that Mrs. Gouldstone, lying ill in the front room, really did not know what was going on."

**3. Saturday 11 August: the crowd.** The first appearance of the public as a mob rather than a readership.
His brothers in the cells beforehand, and his calm. The wrong station; the half-mile walk because nobody
ordered a cab; not recognised at first, then hooted the whole way. The dock: arms folded on the rail, the
nod and the smile at Ada, the eyes going round the Bench and the reporters, the feet marking time with the
clerk's reading. The exhibits. The Treasury taking over. The coffee shop, and the crowd's short cut to the
station.

**REOPENED by the Guardian, 22 September 2026 (late).** Two things in this movement have moved.
(a) **The throat gesture is contested three ways** (Contradiction 49). The Chronicle has the cord held up
and William pointing "significantly to his throat"; the IPN and the Guardian, printing a fuller version of
one reporter's copy, have the hammer produced wrapped with **his own tartan scarf in a copy of the
Walthamstow Guardian**, and William pointing at the scarf and then his throat, glossed by one paper as
"That is mine" and by the other as "Let me put it around my neck," he being without anything on his neck.
The ominous reading cannot be presented as fact. Either give the papers against each other, which is what
the movement is for, or leave the gesture out.
(b) **The Saturday has two press families, not one**: the Daily Chronicle (reprinted by the Dover Express)
and the IPN/Guardian copy. The decision to tell the movement as the Chronicle's report still works, but it
is no longer forced by single-sourcing, and the sharper version of it may be to let the two papers
disagree in front of the reader.
**DECIDED 22 September 2026 (Rik): the whole movement is told as the Daily Chronicle's report**, named and
dated at its head, including "By some thoughtlessness the police, instead of bringing Gouldstone from the
station to the court in a cab, made him walk the distance of about half a mile." Attributed to the **Daily
Chronicle of 13 August, not the Dover Express**, which is reprinting it (Contradiction 46).

**Why the framing, and not just the quotation.** There is no sworn record of the hearing of 11 August at
all. The JP file goes from Ada's witness statement of 9 August to the examination of 18 August, taken at
the Court House, Great Eastern Road, before two justices; nothing in `sources/trial-docs/` covers the
Saturday. The crowd, the wrong station, the walk, the hooting, the cord held up, the hammer, the coffee
shop and the short cut to the station exist in newsprint and nowhere else. Under the standing press rule a
newspaper is never used unattributed as evidence for what happened, so the movement either announces its
source or it cannot be written. It announces it. That is also the honest position: the Saturday is the day
the case stopped being a set of depositions and became a public spectacle, and telling it through the
paper that made it one puts the form and the content in the same place.

**4. Sunday 12 August: Blyth.** A juryman at the bed of a woman eleven days from a confinement, telling her
the verdict is decided, that her husband will hang or be as good as dead to her, and that the jury blames
her. He calls it a visit "of a kindly nature, to see if he could be of any service". Set it down flat. No
adjectives. The Friday movement has already shown the jury asking for the material he is repeating back to
her; the two scenes do that work between them without a word of ours.

**5. Monday 13 August, noon: the funeral.** Rewritten from the Guardian, which has the whole thing.
Five coffins of stained elm, each with a plate giving a name and an age, so the twins were buried under
names and the register will have them. The coffins under velvet palls on the bier in the mortuary church,
which was not the custom and made the crowd press harder at the door; Sergeant Hawkins keeping them back.
The undertaker is **T. Maynard, who holds the contract for the Union interments**, his manager John Lowick
running it. Shortly after twelve the procession comes out from beside the Board-room behind Grantham
Robinson. **Ten mourners**: the prisoner's aunt, a Mrs Gouldstone, carrying a bouquet, with her daughter;
Mrs Graves of Hackney with a wreath, in whose house they married and in whose house the three eldest boys
were born; Ada Hamilton; Emma Clarke; and **Mrs Millard, who a few weeks before had two boys drowned**.
The attendants carry the coffins out on their shoulders and **Mr Amey carries the twins, one under each
arm**. The grave is in the third-class portion. Children bring flowers in baskets and are pleased with the
task. **Elizabeth is not there, and the paper says people had expected her**, because she had asked to see
her children and been refused. Her absence is now sourced, not inferred.
**DECIDED 22 September 2026 (Rik): neither figure. PART OF THE GROUND HAS SINCE MOVED.** The depth is no
longer irreconcilable: the Guardian, whose reporter was at the graveside, gives ten feet, agreeing with the
Morning Post against the Dover Express's six (Contradiction 52). A ten-foot common grave in the
third-class portion, dug by the Union interment contractor, is the class argument in one hole in the
ground, and it is now two-to-one with the local paper on the right side. **Worth reopening; the decision as
taken stands until it is.** The attendance has a third figure, "over 1,000" computed by the Guardian
(Contradiction 51), and stays out either way. The original reasoning follows. The grave's depth is
Contradiction 42 (6ft against 10ft) and attendance is Contradiction 7 (1,500 against several hundred). The elm coffins and the
third-class portion carry the class point without a number we would have to defend, and the crowd is
rendered in what it does: the hour moved three hours to prevent an assemblage, the assemblage arriving
anyway, troops of people pouring in at three, visitors until the gates closed, children carrying flowers
in baskets. The two funeral reports are genuinely independent of each other, unlike their reports of the
Saturday hearing, so the disagreement is real and not an artefact of reprinting. **The depth is not
permanently unanswerable**, unlike the crowd: the Queen's Road burial register would settle it, along with
the grave number, who paid, and the names the twins were buried under.

**6. Monday 13 August, afternoon: the verdict.** The same cemetery. Lyle's letter, read aloud by Lyle
himself. Blyth: "Well, they might have been. I do not know." The coroner repudiating it on behalf of the
court, and then: "We will now proceed with the case." Smith, Bryant, Folkard, Gould, with the post-mortem
findings arriving here. The coroner's direction that sanity "was a subject that the jury had nothing to do
with, but which concerned another tribunal". The verdict, "without hesitation", and five verdicts signed.
Outside, at three, the grave being filled in.
**End the chapter on the last line of the Dover Express's report, printed immediately after the verdict on
five murdered children: "It is stated that Mrs. Gouldstone is on the whole progressing satisfactorily
towards recovery."** Attributed, under the press rule. It does the Elizabeth thread's work in a borrowed
sentence and the prose adds nothing to it.

## ELIZABETH THROUGH THE CHAPTER

She appears three times and speaks in none of them. Through a wall on the Friday, while the coroner and his
jurymen establish that she did not know they were there. In her bed on the Sunday, being told by one of them
what the jury thinks of her. And on the Monday, absent from her children's funeral and spoken for at the
inquest by her doctor, in a letter, read aloud by him, in a room she is not in.

Her words reach the inquest once, on the Friday, secondhand, through her landlady, because a juryman asked
for them. Two days later a juryman is at her bed.

Chapter Eight already stated the thing once, plainly. This chapter demonstrates it instead.
