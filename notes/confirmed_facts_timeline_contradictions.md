### THE SEQUENCE OF EVENTS, 8 AUGUST 1883

Primary source: Ada Hamilton's sworn testimony across all hearings. **Corrected 21 September 2026: the JP witness statement (9 Aug) is the earliest sworn document, not the coroner deposition (10 Aug).** See the third standing rule in the project instructions.

1. Ada in bedroom washing babies. Charles and Herbert in kitchen. **Frederick on bed in bedroom** with Ada and Elizabeth. [All sources]
2. William arrives c.5:15pm. Ada goes to kitchen: "You did frighten me, I wondered who it was." **He only smiled but made no reply.** [Smile: coroner deposition only] **Sober at this point — Ada's own coroner deposition: "Wm Gouldstone was perfectly sober at the time." One of four independent witnesses (with Cheeseman, Folkard, Wheatley) who contradict his own later cell claim of having drunk beforehand.**
3. William enters bedroom c.10 minutes after arriving. Stands at bedside, silent. Elizabeth asks why home early. Witness statement verbatim (corrected 20 September 2026 against the manuscript image, replacing an earlier "when" misreading): "I left my situation whilst the Manager went to dinner and came home." No comma present around "whilst" in the manuscript. [All sworn sources]
4. **William takes Frederick off the bed and carries him to the kitchen.** All three boys now in kitchen. [All sources from magistrate onwards — see Contradictions: coroner deposition says Elizabeth took Frederick]
5. **There are three kitchen visits, not one and not two — restructured 20 September 2026; see Contradiction 27 for the full source-by-source breakdown.** Kitchen 1 is the arrival at the dresser (no cord, only two children). Kitchen 2 follows the bedside exchange: three boys playing, William on the fireguard, and on the best-supported reading both the cord and the tea exchange. Ada asks if he'd had tea — no — tells him it's ready — no reply. Returns to bedroom, washes the second twin. Second visit: only now does she notice the piece of clothes line in his hand, **tying it** — her word, not an inference (coroner deposition verbatim: "I noticed he had a piece of clothes line in his hand tying it. The piece produced is either the same or similar to it."). Old Bailey cross-exam only, five weeks later, adds that he was looking at his hands and the cord, not at the children. [All sworn sources]
6. Ada goes through to scullery. **Sees chair by cistern. Perambulator had been moved from its place** (coroner deposition: a word is crossed out in the manuscript before "near to the cistern" — paleographic detail, content of cancelled word not yet established). **Chair placed there instead.** [All sources]
7. Ada asks who moved it. **William Gouldstone was within hearing but did not answer.** Three-year-old Charles: **"My daddy put it there"** (coroner deposition verbatim: "I asked as to who had placed the chair there. William Gouldstone was within hearing. Charles Gouldstone replied 'My Daddy put it there.'"). [All sworn sources]
8. Ada returns to bedroom. Gets Elizabeth out of bed, remakes it, Elizabeth gets back in. [All sources]
9. William comes along passage, looks in. Ada tells him he can come in — coroner deposition verbatim: **"I said to him 'You can come in Mr Goldstone.'"** (deposition's own spelling of the surname at this instance; witness statement CRIM 1/19/2 paraphrases this without direct quotation or surname as "I told him he could come in"). He mumbles something, returns to kitchen. Ada offers to leave so he can speak to Elizabeth privately. Elizabeth agrees. [All sworn sources]
10. Ada leaves room and goes downstairs. [Confirmed]
11. Ada hears kitchen door open, William walks across landing, enters bedroom. [Ada]
12. Ada reaches yard door. Hears screams of "Murder!" from Elizabeth above. [All sworn sources]
13. Ada runs upstairs. Tries bedroom door — locked from inside. [All sworn sources]
14. Ada leans over banisters, calls "Help!" three or four times. [All sworn sources]
15. Emma Clarke (in back garden below) runs upstairs. Both try to force door. Cannot. [Ada; Emma Clarke]
16. William opens door himself. Emma Clarke confirmed his words: **"You can come in, it is all done."** [Emma Clarke, coroner deposition; confirmed under cross-examination, Old Bailey]
17. Elizabeth at foot of bedstead: "Come and look at my babies, and see if he has killed them." [Ada, IPN 18 Aug]
18. Ada says: "Oh, you wicked man, what have you done?" William's reply: see Contradictions.
19. Ada enters. Two twins on pillow, bleeding from nose and mouth. One has bruise over eye. Both breathing. Ada covers them. [All sworn sources]
20. Elizabeth tells Ada to check the other three. Ada goes to kitchen. [All sources]
21. Ada meets William coming from scullery carrying Frederick's wet body. He lays him on the floor. [All sworn sources]
22. Ada sees Elizabeth coming onto landing, runs back to stop her. [All sources]
23. Ada stays with Elizabeth approximately ten minutes. [All sources]
24. On returning to kitchen (Emma Clarke with her): three boys in a row, soaked, dead. William standing there. Dr Twining present, leaning over them; nobody speaks. [Witness statement, manuscript image 0008: "A Gentleman was present, a Chemist"; DT 10 Aug; IPN 18 Aug; Old Bailey; Emma Clarke.] **This is BEFORE Cheeseman arrives** (corrected 21 September 2026, late; see Contradiction 29). Ada goes back to Elizabeth. Emma has already run down to the street for the police.
25. PC Cheeseman arrives. William stooping over three bodies. Hat and coat off, shirt sleeves rolled up. [Cheeseman, all sources]
26. William: "Good evening, policeman. I have done it now. I am happy and ready for the rope." [Cheeseman, all sources]
27. Cheeseman cautions him. William puts on hat and coat. Asks to see wife. [All sources]
28. In bedroom: Elizabeth got up during this interval and was found on the landing heading toward the kitchen — Ada had turned her back before this point. It is Elizabeth herself who asks for William to be brought to her, not procedure. She says the same words Ada said at the door forty minutes earlier: "Oh, you wicked man, what have you done?" William: "I have killed all your children. Now you'll be single again and I shall be hanged." Elizabeth asks for money. He gives her what he has. **Corrected 21 September 2026 (late):** Cheeseman's itemised count is "a two-shilling piece or half-crown, and a shilling and two penny pieces" (Old Bailey), i.e. 3s. 2d. OR 3s. 8d., not "roughly 3s 2d"; Ada's 3s. 4d. falls between. The 18 September wording "just over three shillings" is therefore not safe; use "what money he had" / "all he had". See Contradiction 35. He kisses her. "Goodbye." [Cheeseman, all sources]
29. **The hammer. Corrected 21 September 2026 (late): "only now, after William has already been removed from the house" rests on the coroner deposition alone; see Contradiction 30.** Ada's coroner deposition: "After Wm Gouldstone had been removed a Police Constable came, and in consequence of enquiries made by him I went into the bed room and in it I found the hammer now produced on the floor by the side of the door." Never seen it before. Gave it to the constable.
30. Walk to station: "When I took my money last week I thought of buying a revolver to do it with, but I altered my mind, as I thought it would make too much noise. I had a hard job with the two biggest, but the other little bugger, I soon settled him. I thought it was getting too hot. I have had five bloody kids in about three years and a half, so I thought it was time to put a stop to it." **Swearing resolved 18 September 2026: Cheeseman's own coroner deposition spells the first word "B.gg.r" — dotted, effectively confirming "bugger." "Bloody" is inference (grammar and period convention fit; not spelled out the same way) — and doesn't appear at all in his first account to the coroner, only from his 18 Aug statement onward.** [Cheeseman, all sources]
31. At station, c.6pm: "I did it like a man." **Charged in two stages, not one — Folkard's own witness statement.** 8pm: charged with the three dead children and "attempting to murder" the twins, still alive at that point. Asks after "the missus" — "Tolerable" (Gould). Asks if the twins are dead — told no. 8:30am the following morning: charged with all five, both twins now dead. "Yes sir. The other two are dead then?"
32. In cell c.6:45pm: "I have had an extra drop of drink to accomplish the job; there's five of them gone to glory, and it is a good job." [Wheatley]
33. In cell c.7:30pm: "I am sorry for my wife and parents, but the children will be better off in heaven, if there is such a place; it is better than leaving them to the mercy of the world." [Wheatley]
34. In cell c.9pm: "I wish I had killed the little ones out of the way. I don't know whether I hit them once or twice. I have had this **preying** on my mind for a long time." [Wheatley — confirmed under cross-examination: preying, not playing]

### THE HAMMER AND CORD

- Hammer: "ordinary large hammer for knocking in nails" (Ada, Old Bailey). Ada had never seen it before. Found on floor near dressing table. Gave it to Cheeseman. Origin unknown.
- Cord: a piece of clothes line (Ada, coroner deposition — full word-tally across every source in MASTER_NOTES.md). **PC Honey under cross-examination: "I saw the noose on the string which was loose round the child's neck"** — describes it as found on Charles, not before. **Corrected 18 September 2026: no source supports "brought by William for himself" or links it to the BMJ's separate claim about that morning — this was an invented connection, removed.** Origin unknown; likeliest reading is that it was already in the washhouse, a length of clothesline near the cistern it was used at. At remand hearing 20 August, cord produced; William **pointed significantly to his throat.**
- The unnamed figure at the scene — "a gentleman (a chemist)," per press reports of Ada's testimony — is also in her own sworn JP statement, not a press-only detail as previously treated. **The word is legible: "A Gentleman was present, a Chemist" (manuscript image 0008, checked 21 September 2026; earlier notes called it illegible).** **Identification with Dr Twining CONFIRMED 21 September 2026** (Rik's reasoning, 18 September, from Honey's statement). The friction point recorded here — that Ada calls a local doctor "a chemist" and seemingly does not recognise him — is resolved by the explanation already noted: the Gouldstones' own medical attendant was Mr Sutton, not Twining. Her not naming him is therefore evidence FOR the identification, since she would have named Sutton, and it rules Sutton out as the figure. See Contradiction 29 for the full sequence.
- **Order of the three drownings and use of the cord — reasoned inference, added 19 September 2026, not a sourced sequence.** No document states which boy went into the cistern first or when the cord was used relative to the water. But Herbert (2½) was "sickly, still unable to walk" (Elizabeth's own reprieve statement) and Frederick was sixteen months old — neither could stand, run, or resist. Charles (3½) was the only one of the three capable of any of that, and the only one carrying the cord mark. Strongest available reading: Charles, the one real risk of noise or flight, was restrained with the cord and dealt with first, before the two who could not have resisted regardless and needed no restraint. See full reasoning, MASTER_NOTES.md, cord section. Flag as inference wherever used in prose, exactly as the door-wording variants are flagged rather than silently resolved.

### THE TRIAL, 14 SEPTEMBER 1883

- Court: Central Criminal Court (Old Bailey)
- Judge: Mr Justice Day. Applied McNaughten Rules narrowly. Ridiculed second cousin evidence. Publicly questioned Savage's bona fides from the bench.
- Prosecution: Poland, Montagu Williams
- Defence: Grain, Elliott
- Key defence witnesses: Thomas Gouldstone, Robert Gouldstone, Emily Gouldstone, William Graves, Charles Cakebread, Bennett Gouldstone, John Clark, George Skelton, William Henry Westbrook, Howard John Kinnaird, Charles Gouldstone (cousin), John Byford (brother-in-law), Dr Sunderland, Dr Savage

- What the court was not allowed to hear: William had told Savage he had seen heaven opened, heard the voice of God, but "he knew it was only dreaming"
- Jury absent approximately 15 minutes
- Verdict: GUILTY. Sentence: death.

### ELIZABETH STOCK — AFTER THE MURDERS

- Could not divorce William — he was alive in Broadmoor; insanity not straightforward grounds for divorce in Victorian England
- Legally Mrs Gouldstone until William's death in 1935
- Disappears from the record August 1883 to June 1887
- **Albert Jennet Goldstone born 19 June 1887, Workhouse Infirmary, Brook Street, Lambeth**
- Registered 20 June 1887 by J Frost (infirmary staff member, almost certainly a trained nurse), present at the birth
- Elizabeth's address at time of birth: **2 John Street, Hercules Buildings, Lambeth** — renamed Newnham Terrace in 1893 when John Street, Short Street, and Hercules Terrace were merged; site now the Park Plaza Hotel Waterloo
- Elizabeth was **not a workhouse inmate** — she had her own address and came to the infirmary as a pauper patient through the relieving officer system to deliver
- The infirmary on Brook Street (now Brook Drive) was approximately half a mile from Elizabeth's address at John Street
- Elizabeth registered herself as **Elizabeth Goldston** (a version of Gouldstone — her married name; spelling variation is registrar's, not significant in itself; the significant fact is she used her married name, not Stock)
- Albert's middle name Jennet: origin unknown, no family precedent found in Gouldstone, Stock, or Suckling lines (Sarah, Edward Suckling's wife's own surname is itself unconfirmed — see genealogy corrections below) — open question
- Cohabited with William James Madams in Whitstable from approximately 1888
- Died July 1906, aged 51. Registered Blean district, Kent — NOT a confirmed specific place of death (the district covers several parishes near Canterbury; see Contradiction 12)

### THE LAMBETH WORKHOUSE INFIRMARY

- Address: Brook Street (later Brook Drive), Lambeth
- Built approximately 1876-1879 — approximately 8-11 years old when Albert was born there
- Separate institution from the adjacent Renfrew Road workhouse — separate building, separate entrance
- Admissions through the relieving officer — pauper stigma attached [Mrs Emma Brewer's account, 1890, workhouses.org.uk]
- Had a dedicated Lying-in Ward [Mrs Emma Brewer, 1890]
- Trained its own nurses [Mrs Emma Brewer, 1890]
- Charlie Chaplin was briefly a workhouse (not infirmary) inmate in 1896 — nine years after Albert was born at the adjacent infirmary
- Site largely demolished; former Master's House (1871) and water tower survive; Cinema Museum now in Master's House [ezitis.myzen.co.uk]

### REV. THOMAS HARRISON GRANTHAM ROBINSON (1837-1884)

- Full name confirmed from obituary (Church Bells and Illustrated Church News, 1884)
- Born Ireland, July 1837. Educated King's College London and University of Dublin.
- Ordained to Whitechapel; Secretary to the Prayer Book and Homily Society; assistant curate St Augustine's Highbury
- In 1865 offered living of St James's, Walthamstow
- St Saviour's church built through munificence of Mr R. Foster within his parish
- Conducted children's funeral 13 August 1883
- Almost certainly the "Vicar of the parish in which he lived" (BMJ 29 Sep 1883) who wrote to the Home Secretary
- Died Friday 21 March 1884, age 46 — seven months after the murders, five months after the reprieve
- Never knew what became of Elizabeth
- Character: "large-hearted, good humoured, and kind to all"; respected by Nonconformists; mourned by the poor as "a departed leader and friend"

## PART THREE: OUTSTANDING CONTRADICTIONS

### 1. WILLIAM'S WORDS ON OPENING THE BEDROOM DOOR
Multiple sworn versions:
- **Ada's coroner deposition (10 Aug):** *"I am not a wicked man. I am a happy man."*
- **Ada's formal witness statement (CRIM 1/19/2):** *"I have killed the children. I am a happy man now."*
- **Emma Clarke's coroner deposition and Old Bailey cross-examination:** *"You can come in, it is all done."* — confirmed as what she swore to
- **Old Bailey transcript (Ada):** *"No, I am a happy man."*

**Assessment:** Most probable reconstruction — sequential statements. Clarke heard what he said as he opened the door, before Ada spoke. Ada then said "Oh, you wicked man, what have you done?" and he replied. Then the cistern statement to Elizabeth. Different reporters and hearings caught different fragments, and the three utterances are compatible rather than competing.

**CORRECTED 21 September 2026 — this entry previously called the coroner deposition "the earliest sworn document" and rested its preference for that wording on the claim.** It is not the earliest. Ada's JP witness statement was sworn 9 August, the coroner deposition 10 August, as Contradiction 2 below states correctly. This file contradicted itself on the point. The witness statement's version of the reply is therefore the earliest sworn wording: *"I have killed the children. I am a happy man now."* That is an admission; the coroner deposition's *"I am not a wicked man. I am a happy man"* is a denial of Ada's accusation. Materially different men in that doorway, and the choice between them should be made on its merits, not on a date that was wrong. **This is the second instance found in two days of the coroner deposition being wrongly treated as the earliest document** — the other was the "ten minutes" figure, which is also the witness statement's, not the coroner's. Check the date before preferring a document, every time.

### 11. THOMAS STOCK'S FIRST MARRIAGE — UNCONFIRMED
- **Marriage record exists:** Thomas Stock & Sarah Hockley, 20 October 1827, Finchingfield (D/P 14/1/10). No ages, no parents' names given for either party.
- **Against it:** At least three other Thomas Stocks documented in the same small parish cluster across the relevant decades (a "Mary Stock, five children" household at Gravel Pit, Wethersfield, 1841; an unmarried Thomas Stock tracked consistently across 1841 and 1851 at "Village, Finchingfield"; a "Thomas Stock, 20" in an unrelated household in 1841). No census return in 1841 or 1851 shows a Sarah as wife to any Thomas Stock matching our man's known details.
- **A related FamilySearch collaborative-tree profile** compounds the risk: correct baptism and parents for our Thomas, but continues with Sarah as his wife through 1881 with five/six children by her, and omits Jemima and Elizabeth entirely — an evident merge of two separate research threads.

**Assessment, still unresolved after a real attempt, 9 September 2026:** The 1827 marriage record itself is now properly sourced — witnesses **Pamela Crick and William Martin** (not the earlier tentative readings), archive reference **D/P 14/1/10, p.81**, St John the Baptist, Finchingfield, the same church Thomas was baptised in. Both parties marked rather than signed, "Thomas Stock, Bachelor, of this Parish" and "Sarah Hockley, Spinster, of this Parish" — no ages given, consistent with the pre-1837 printed form and not a gap specific to this record. A GRO death-index search for a Sarah Stock naming Thomas as husband was attempted at length this session: several candidates were checked and eliminated — a 77-year-old Braintree 1845 death (age implausible for a first-time spinster bride), a 48-year-old Saffron Walden 1850 death (age and geography both plausible, but the actual certificate turned out to name her as "wife of William Stock" — ruled out directly), and roughly a dozen other Sarah Stock entries nationwide, none matching on both age and place. A promising-looking 1861 census household (Thomas and Sarah Stock, Petches Bridge, Finchingfield) was also checked and ruled out: that Thomas is 54 in 1861 (implying b. c.1807), five years off our Thomas's well-triangulated 1802 birth — almost certainly a fifth Thomas Stock namesake in the same small area, not a new lead. **What IS confirmed directly (1854 marriage register): Thomas was a widower at his marriage to Jemima.** Sarah Hockley's own fate after 1827 remains genuinely open. Per Rik's decision, 9 September 2026: not pursued further — explicitly not load-bearing for the book's argument, and the surname's local density makes any further index-only search unreliable without a document that names a spouse directly (a death certificate, a burial register giving relationship, or an 1841/1851 census catching Thomas with a wife whose age matches his known 1802 birth).

### 16. MABEL GOULDSTONE — DAUGHTER OR GRANDDAUGHTER? RESOLVED, 4 SEPTEMBER 2026

This also resolves what had looked like a legal problem with her 1908 marriage to Charles Bennett Gouldstone (Bennett's son) — as an actual granddaughter via her mother's line, not Thomas and Emily's daughter, she and Charles were first cousins, not aunt and nephew. ### 29. THE "GENTLEMAN (A CHEMIST)" IS DR TWINING — RESOLVED 21 September 2026
**This entry was logged twice the same night in error before reaching the right answer. Both wrong versions are recorded below so the reasoning is auditable.**

**The evidence, positively.** PC Honey's witness statement: *"about 10 minutes to 6 I was on duty in High Street Walthamstow. From information received I went for Dr. Twining and then to Number 8 Courtenay Road. When I got there Dr. Twining was there. He was examining the bodies of three children who were in the kitchen. I saw Dr. Twining take the piece of string produced from the neck of the boy Charles."*

Honey sets out to FETCH Twining and finds him already at the house. That only works if Twining had been summoned earlier by somebody else, and the person who sent for a doctor was Ada, at roughly twenty-five minutes to six, when she found the twins. What Honey then finds Twining doing — examining the three children in the kitchen — is precisely what Ada's unnamed gentleman is doing when she returns to that room.

**The full sequence, with every document in place and none of them bent:**
- c.5.35 — Ada finds the twins injured, covers them, sends for a doctor.
- c.5.37 — Ada goes through to the kitchen, meets William carrying Frederick in from the scullery, turns Elizabeth back from the landing and stays with her in the bedroom for about ten minutes.
- 5.40 — Cheeseman arrests William in the kitchen (his own cross-examination: "about 20 minutes to 6"), takes him into the bedroom to his wife, the money and the kiss, then removes him from the house.
- c.5.45 — Twining arrives on Ada's summons and begins examining the three boys in the kitchen.
- c.5.47 — Ada comes out of the bedroom, sees the three lying in a row, and a gentleman present.
- c.5.52 — Honey arrives, having gone to fetch a man who was already there; sees Twining take the cord from Charles's neck and receives it from him.
- c.6.00 — Gould arrives, called by Folkard. Honey: "I remained there until Dr. Gould came."

**Corroborating, from dramatis_personae.md:** Ada uses the same distinctive phrase in two independent renderings of her testimony, her own witness statement ("A Gentleman was present, a Chemist", fully legible in the manuscript, image 0008) and the Daily Telegraph's report of her magistrates' evidence ("a gentleman (a chemist) leaning over them"). "Chemist" is her lay term for him.

**Status: resolved. The gentleman is Twining.** The prose may name him.

**CORRECTION TO THE TIMINGS ABOVE, 21 September 2026 (late). The identification stands; the minute-by-minute sequence does not.** The sequence above puts Twining's arrival (c.5.45) and Ada's sighting (c.5.47) AFTER the arrest. Ada's own witness statement, sworn 9 August and checked against the manuscript (image 0008), gives the opposite order: "When I went into the kitchen again I saw all three children lying all in a row on the ground... A Gentleman was present, a Chemist. The prisoner was present. I didn't hear him say anything. A Police Officer came and he took the prisoner into custody." The DT (10 Aug) and IPN (18 Aug) reports of the same evidence agree ("with a gentleman (a chemist) leaning over them... I saw a police-constable come afterwards"); the Old Bailey has William "standing there" when she comes back and the policeman arriving after; Emma Clarke saw the row and then saw William apprehended. Only the coroner deposition puts her kitchen visit after the removal, and that reads as a second look ("somebody came and felt their pulses"). The statement in withdrawn error 2 below, that "her own coroner deposition has her returning to the kitchen only after ten minutes with Elizabeth", is also wrong: the coroner deposition gives no duration; "ten minutes" is the witness statement's and "a quarter of an hour" the Old Bailey's. Revised sequence: c.5.35 Ada finds the twins and sends for a doctor, Emma runs down for the police; Ada stays with Elizabeth ten to fifteen minutes; Twining arrives; Ada and Emma go into the kitchen: the row, William standing, Twining leaning over the boys, nobody speaking; Ada returns to Elizabeth; c.5.40–5.45 Cheeseman arrives, the arrest, the bedroom, William removed; c.5.50–6.00 Honey arrives, having gone to fetch a man already there, and receives the cord; after 6, Folkard and Gould. Twining was therefore at the house before Cheeseman, which makes an earlier summons even more necessary and strengthens, rather than weakens, the identification.

**Two withdrawn errors, kept for the record.**
1. Logged first as "Twining CANNOT have been the gentleman, ruled out on chronology", reasoning that Honey had not yet gone to fetch him at ten to six. Unsound: Honey going for him at ten to six shows only that Honey did not know he was already there.
2. Withdrawn and relogged as "plausible, unconfirmed, not ruled out", but keeping an objection that Ada's sequence put the gentleman in the kitchen before the constable arrived, which would have needed Twining there within five minutes of being sent for. That objection came from the press compression of her evidence. Her own coroner deposition has her returning to the kitchen only after ten minutes with Elizabeth, which places the sighting after the arrest and removes the strain entirely.

**Method note.** Both errors came from reasoning about a document without reading the person's own dramatis_personae entry, which already argued the correct case with sources. Read the subject's entry before logging a contradiction about the subject.

### 27. THE THREE KITCHEN VISITS — WHAT EACH SOURCE PUTS IN WHICH

Earlier notes in this file and in MASTER_NOTES said "two separate visits," which was counting only the visits after the bedside exchange and omitting the arrival. Corrected throughout.

Each later document departs from it on exactly one item, in opposite directions, and neither departure is independently corroborated.

The cord's placement is weaker as a conflict than first logged. ### 44. WHEN THE INQUEST RESUMED — MORNING OR AFTERNOON (logged 22 September 2026)
- **Dover Express, 17 Aug:** "The adjourned inquest was resumed the following morning at the lodge of the
  cemetery at Walthamstow."
- **Morning Post:** "At the Lodge of the Walthamstow Cemetery yesterday afternoon, Mr. Charles Carne
  Lewis, the coroner for the southern division of the county of Essex, resumed the inquiry."

**Assessment: afternoon, Monday 13 August. The Dover Express is wrong**, for two independent reasons.
The Morning Post is a daily reporting the previous day, against a weekly assembling agency copy a week
later. And the funeral was moved forward from three o'clock to noon "in order to prevent the assemblage
of a large crowd while the proceedings at the inquest were progressing" (Dover Express itself), which
only works if the inquest sat after the burial on the same day. The Dover Express's own report then
describes crowds "inspecting the flower-covered grave" while the inquest was going on, which requires the
burial to have already happened. Its "following morning" follows nothing that works.

**Also established in the same pass, and not previously recorded anywhere in the project:**
- The inquest OPENED on the afternoon of **Friday 10 August**, "at the board room of the Walthamstow
  Cemetery" (The Times and The Evening News, both 11 August), with three witnesses: Ada Hamilton, Emma
  Clarke and PC Cheeseman (Morning Post).
- The first Stratford hearing was **Thursday 9 August**, confirmed independently by The Times ("she
  repeated the evidence she gave on the previous day before the magistrates") and by the remand itself:
  "Then the prisoner will be remanded till Saturday, and in the meantime further inquiries will be made"
  (Mr Lister, Daily Telegraph 10 Aug; Pall Mall Gazette 9 Aug, "remanded till Saturday").
- The second Stratford hearing was therefore **Saturday 11 August**, not a later date: the cord and the
  throat, the hammer in the neck handkerchief, the Treasury taking over, the week's remand.

**OPEN:** the opening is reported at the "board room" and the resumption at the "lodge". One room or two
is not established. Do not let prose move between them until it is.


### 45. THE POST-MORTEM: WHEN, AND HOW MANY (logged 22 September 2026) — RESOLVES AN OPEN QUESTION
The Chapter Ten skeleton listed "whether the post-mortem was the 9th or the 10th" as open, on the strength
of the Dover Express's loose paraphrase ("Next day I made a post-mortem examination of the babies"). It is
not open.

- **Gould's coroner deposition, checked against the manuscript image (0039) on 22 September 2026:** "On
  Thursday the 9 August Instant at 3.30 P.M. I made a post mortem examination of all the 5 Children",
  and, lower on the same page, "At 4.30 P.M. on Thursday I made a post mortem examination of the other
  Male Infant."
- **Morning Post report of the same sworn evidence:** "I made the post-mortem examination on Thursday, the
  9th inst., at 3.40 p.m., of all the five children."

**Assessment: Thursday 9 August, beginning at 3.30 p.m.** Prefer the deposition over the newspaper's
report of it; the ten-minute difference is immaterial and should not be stated to the minute in prose.
Note that the deposition gives two sittings an hour apart, which the press report flattens into one.

**Consequence for Chapter Ten.** The post-mortems are datable to the hour and fall on the same day as the
first Stratford hearing, which the Pall Mall Gazette of 9 August (an evening paper) places in the
morning: "was examined at the Stratford petty sessions **this morning**". Thursday therefore runs: charged
at 8.30 a.m. (Folkard); before the magistrates that morning; on Gould's table at half past three and half
past four that afternoon. The earlier recommendation to hold the post-mortem back because it "could not be
dated" is withdrawn.


### 46. THE DAILY CHRONICLE (13 AUG) AND THE DOVER EXPRESS (17 AUG) ARE ONE TEXT (logged 22 September 2026)
The Chapter Ten skeleton cited the Saturday hearing as "reported in the Daily Chronicle of 13 August, the
Dover Express of 17 August and the IPN of 18 August", as if three witnesses. The first two are the same
report, reprinted almost word for word: "nodded complacently in recognition to Mrs. Hamilton"; "pointed
significantly to his throat"; "By some thoughtlessness the police, instead of bringing Gouldstone from the
station to the court in a cab, made him walk the distance of about half a mile"; the coffee shop; the short
cut to the station. Variants are trivial and of the kind compositors introduce (pathway/passage,
glance/glimpse, Courtenay/Courtney). **The Daily Chronicle of 13 August is the earlier printing and is the
one to name when this material is quoted as press.**

**This strengthens Contradiction 44.** The Dover Express, a weekly, is stitching a Monday-morning daily's
report of the Saturday hearing to a later report of the inquest, and the bridging phrase it supplies is "The
adjourned inquest was resumed the following morning". Read literally against the section it follows, that
puts the inquest on **Sunday 12 August**, which is impossible. It is a compiler's join, not a datable
observation, and it should not be weighed against the Morning Post's "yesterday afternoon" as though the two
were equivalent claims.


### 47. WHO ACTUALLY WENT ON THE VIEW (logged 22 September 2026)
The project's notes have described the view of 8 Courtenay Place as the coroner and the jury, and the
Elizabeth thread file rendered it "Twelve men and a coroner went through the rooms where her children had
been killed". No source supports twelve.

- **Daily Telegraph, 11 August:** "The inquiry was then adjourned, and after Mr. Lewis had made out the
  orders for the burials of the children he, **with some of the jurymen**, proceeded to view the premises
  where the sad affair occurred."
- Blyth, at the resumed inquest, confirms the same from the other side: he "was unable to accompany the
  coroner and the other jurymen to inspect the premises" (Dover Express).

**Use "the coroner and some of the jury". Corrected in `notes/elizabeth_thread_presence_as_absence.md` and
in the Chapter Ten skeleton the same day.** The coroner's own defence of the visit is unaffected by this and
still reads "I myself and the jurymen accompanying me".

**Also established from the same Daily Telegraph paragraph, and not previously recorded anywhere in the
project:** the burial orders were made out by the coroner in the cemetery board room, immediately after the
adjournment, before he walked to the house. The paperwork that permitted the children to be buried was
signed within a few yards of their bodies.


### 48. WHEN THE GRAVE WAS COVERED IN FLOWERS (logged 22 September 2026)
The proposed Chapter Ten rested its closing image on the claim that "the grave was already covered in
flowers when the jury sat down". Both funeral reports contradict the sequence.

- **Morning Post:** "At three o'clock the grave used for the five children was filled up, and it was soon
  after covered with flowers of all descriptions, which visitors had brought with them."
- **Dover Express:** "as soon as the grave was filled up it was covered with flowers of all kinds... During
  the whole of the afternoon and until the cemetery gates closed, visitors constantly arrived to visit the
  grave."

The burial was at noon; the grave was not filled until three; the flowers came after that. The Dover
Express's crowds "inspecting the flower-covered grave... while the inquest was going on" therefore date
from the later part of the afternoon, and establish only that the inquest was still sitting after about
three o'clock — which is consistent with a coroner who "summed up the case at great length".

**What is sourced, and is enough:** the children were buried at noon; the inquest into their deaths sat that
afternoon in the same cemetery; the grave was filled in at three while it sat; the flowers went on after
that; and the crowd moved between the lodge door and the mound all afternoon. **What is not sourced, and
must not be written:** that the grave was flower-covered when the jury was sworn in, or any statement of
whether the verdict came before or after the flowers.

### 49. THE THROAT GESTURE AT THE SATURDAY HEARING — CORD, OR SCARF (logged 22 September 2026)
The most quoted image of the 11 August hearing, and the project has been carrying one of three versions of
it as though it were the only one.

- **Daily Chronicle, 13 August (reprinted Dover Express, 17 August):** Ada "identified a piece of cord that
  she saw the prisoner handling in the kitchen… When this cord was held up by a policeman for
  identification by the witness the prisoner **pointed significantly to his throat**. The hammer used by the
  prisoner was also produced and identified by the witness."
- **Illustrated Police News, 18 August:** "When the constable produced the hammer it was found **in a neck
  handkerchief**, and the prisoner looked around, pointed to it, and then to his throat, as much as to say,
  **'That is mine.'**"
- **Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian, 18 August:** "When the constable produced the hammer it was found
  along with **a tartan scarf in a copy of the Walthamstow Guardian**, and the prisoner looked around,
  pointed to the scarf, and then to his throat, as much as to say, **'Let me put it around my neck,' he
  being without anything on his neck.**"

**Assessment.** The IPN and the Guardian are printing the same reporter's copy: their surrounding sentences
are near identical ("furtively glanced around alternately at the Bench, the reporters, and the people in
the court", "leaned his full length as much as he could over the breadth of the dock", "moving his feet, as
if marking time with the clerk's reading of the depositions"). They are therefore one account, not two, but
it is a fuller and more closely observed account than the Chronicle's, and the Guardian's printing of it is
fuller than the IPN's. So there are two press families for the Saturday, disagreeing at the crucial point:

- the Chronicle family attaches the gesture to **the cord**, and reads it as **the rope**;
- the IPN/Guardian family attaches it to **his own scarf**, wrapped up with the hammer as an exhibit, and
  reads it as a man pointing out his own property, or asking for it back, because he had nothing round his
  neck.

These are not compatible glosses of one gesture, and they may not even be the same moment: the Chronicle
has the cord and the hammer produced separately. **Nothing here is settled.** The prose must not present
the ominous reading as established fact, and the skeleton's "the cord held up, and William pointing to his
own throat" is one version of three. If the moment is used at all, the honest treatment under the press
rule is to give the papers against each other and let the reader hold both: a man pointing at his throat in
a dock, and two sets of reporters deciding what he meant. Note also that a scarf carries its own charge in
this case: Thomas Gouldstone swore at the Old Bailey that his wife "tried to grain herself with a scarf",
grain being the Essex word for strangle.


### 50. THE CORONER'S REPUDIATION — WHAT HE REPUDIATED (logged 22 September 2026)
- **Dover Express, 17 August:** "The Coroner: So far as I am concerned I repudiate anything said **on behalf
  of this Court**."
- **Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian, 18 August:** "The Coroner: So far as I and the jury are concerned I
  thoroughly repudiate **everything in this letter**. **('Hear, hear' from several of the jury.)** It is
  impossible for us, when the inquiry has been adjourned, to be responsible for what any member of the jury
  [does]…"

**Assessment: materially different, and the difference matters to the Elizabeth thread.** In the Dover
Express the coroner is disclaiming Blythe's words on the court's behalf. In the local paper he is
repudiating **Dr Lyle's letter**, with audible support from the jury. The Guardian's version is the fuller
one and gives the coroner's whole closing speech, which the Dover Express compresses; it is also the local
reporter in the room. **Unresolved. Do not silently pick one.** If the scene is written, the Guardian's
"Hear, hear" is the harder and better-sourced beat, and the disagreement itself belongs in the notes, not
in the prose.


### 51. FUNERAL ATTENDANCE — A THIRD FIGURE (logged 22 September 2026; supersedes Contradiction 7)
- **Morning Post, [14] August:** "several hundred people had assembled".
- **Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian, 18 August:** "it is **computed that over 1,000 persons** were present".
- **Dover Express, 17 August:** "Not less than 1500 persons were present at the funeral."

**Assessment:** three figures, and the local paper sits in the middle with the only one that presents itself

22 September stands: **no number in our own voice.** If a figure is ever wanted it goes in as a named
paper's claim. What all three agree on, and what the prose can use freely, is that a funeral moved forward
three hours specifically to avoid a crowd drew one anyway.


### 52. GRAVE DEPTH — EFFECTIVELY SETTLED AT TEN FEET (logged 22 September 2026; resolves Contradiction 42)
- **Morning Post:** "a 10-foot grave in the third-class portion".
- **Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian, 18 August:** "reaching the grave, which was **10ft. deep**, and in the
  third-class portion of the cemetery".
- **Dover Express, 17 August:** "one common grave, **6ft. deep**".

**Assessment:** two independent papers, one of them the local one whose reporter was at the graveside and
describes the procession in detail, give ten feet. The Dover Express, a Kentish weekly assembling other
people's copy a hundred miles away, gives six. **Ten feet is now the strongly supported reading**, and the
earlier "irreconcilable" verdict at Contradiction 42 was reached without this source. The burial register at
Chingford Mount would confirm it outright and should still be obtained. Note the point the figure carries:
a ten-foot grave is a common grave dug deep for multiple interments, in the third-class portion, by the
contractor who held the Union interment contract. That is the class argument in one hole in the ground.


### NEWLY ESTABLISHED FROM THE GUARDIAN OF 18 AUGUST — none of it previously in the project

**The inquest jury, named in full.** "Messrs. William Smith (foreman), David Casey, **Joseph Blythe**,
Walter Rollick, John Patchett, Joseph Day, George Foxwell, George Banks, George Lloyd, Thomas Mumford,
Stephen Archer, Edwin Lee, William Haggett, and William Weston." **Fourteen men.** This answers the
standing question of Blyth's forename: he is **Joseph Blythe**, and the local paper spells it Blythe, as
the Dover Express does. Every other juror is now a name that can be run against the Walthamstow rate books
and the 1881 census.

**The jury was fourteen, not twelve.** Another reason the "twelve men and a coroner" wording struck out at
Contradiction 47 was wrong.

**The time of the resumed inquest: three o'clock.** "At three o'clock in the afternoon, **the time
appointed for the funeral**, the adjourned inquest was held." Previously unknown. The inquest therefore
opened at the hour the burial had originally been fixed for, and at about the same hour the grave was being
filled in. See Contradiction 48: the flowers still follow the filling, and the prose still must not put
them before the jury.

**Board-room, not lodge, for both sittings.** The Guardian gives "the Board-room of the Walthamstow
Cemetery" for the Friday and "the Boardroom of the cemetery" for the Monday, and has the funeral procession
emerging "from the vicinity of the Board-room". The Morning Post and Dover Express call the Monday venue
the Lodge. The likeliest reading is one building, the cemetery lodge, containing the board room of the
burial board, but this is still not established from a plan or a minute book and the prose should not move
between the two names.

**A mortuary and a mortuary church, both on the ground.** The jury "went to the mortuary to view the bodies
which were there exposed on a slab"; the five coffins "had previously been placed upon the bier in the
mortuary church, which, being contrary to the usual custom, made the crowd of spectators all the more eager
for admittance". Grantham Robinson's letter calls it "the parish mortuary".

**The coroner did not know the post-mortems had been done.** "I was not aware until I came here that
post-mortem examinations had been made… I should have ordered them to have been made, and therefore what
has been done is simply absolutely necessary. I had thought when I came here that I should have had to
adjourn the inquiry for that purpose also." Gould had opened all five children on the Thursday afternoon on
police authority, and the coroner learned of it on arriving at his own inquest and ratified it
retrospectively.

**The funeral, in detail, for the first time.**
- Undertaker: **Mr T. Maynard, "who has the contract for the Union interments"**; the arrangements carried
  out by his manager, **Mr John Lowick**.
- **Each child in a separate coffin of smooth elm, stained, "each coffin having a plate on which their
  respective names and ages were inscribed".** The twins therefore had names on plates. The burial register
  should give them.
- The attendants carried the coffins out of the church on their shoulders, and **"Mr. Amey with the twins,
  one under each arm"**.
- Police under **Sergeant Hawkins** controlled the crowd at the church door.
- The procession left the vicinity of the Board-room **shortly after twelve**, preceded by Grantham
  Robinson.
- **Ten mourners**, named or described: **"Mrs. Gouldstone, the prisoner's aunt, accompanied by her
  daughter"**, the aunt carrying a bouquet; **Mrs Graves and daughter**, Mrs Graves carrying a wreath, "the
  wife of the prisoner's foreman", living at Hackney, and the Guardian adds that **it was from her house
  that William and Elizabeth were married and in her house that the three eldest children were born**;
  **Mrs Ada Hamilton (the nurse)**; **Mrs Clarke (the landlady)**; and **Mrs Millard, "who but a few weeks
  ago had two boys drowned"**.
- **The aunt is a Mrs Gouldstone**, so the wife of one of Thomas's brothers, which strengthens rather than
  settles the link to the "Cousin Gouldstone in Mash-street" of William's Chelmsford letter. The same page
  of this paper carries a notice headed "MARSH-STREET OPEN AIR MISSION", confirming Marsh Street as a
  current Walthamstow locality in 1883.

**Elizabeth's absence from the funeral is now STATED, not inferred.** "Great sympathy was felt for Mrs.
Gouldstone's bereaved and friendless condition, and **many people expected that she would be present at the
funeral**, she displayed so much anxiety to see her little ones before they were removed after the murder."
The standing instruction to write her absence as inference or not at all is lifted. It was noticed at the
time, and the paper explains why people expected her: because she had asked to see them.

**A sixth recorded utterance of Elizabeth's, sworn at the inquest through Ada, and not in the project's
list.** A juryman asked Ada: "Did the wife reproach him for not giving her enough money for expenses?"
Ada: "**No. She said to him, 'Never mind; I'll do what I can to support them.'**" Added to
`notes/elizabeth_thread_presence_as_absence.md`.

**The jury pursued the wife-blame theory from the Friday, in open court.** Two of the jury's questions on
10 August are about Elizabeth: the money question above, put to Ada, and the "By the Jury" question to Emma
Clarke that produced "I have heard the mother say she wished the children dead". Two days later a juryman
was at her bedside telling her the jury held her responsible. The chain is now three links and every one is
sourced.

**Ada's own account of the hammer, given at the Saturday hearing** (also in the IPN): not until William was
taken into custody did she see it; the constable asked her for it; she went into the bedroom and found it
"behind the door, near the dressing-table"; "she noticed it, but saw no marks on it"; she gave it to the
constable. Consistent with Contradiction 30's resolution.

**The Saturday hearing, chaired by Mr Powell**, with Mr Wells the clerk reading Ada's depositions and Mr
Atkinson for the prisoner. William "immediately folded his arms and rested them on the front" of the dock;
"seeing the nurse opposite to him he nodded and smiled"; he "leaned his full length as much as he could
over the breadth of the dock", "moving his feet, as if marking time with the clerk's reading of the
depositions". Remanded a week "to give the Treasury time to prosecute".

**For Part One and Chapter Six, from the paper's character sketch of William:**
- **The 6.4 a.m. is sourced as his regular train**: "on only one occasion did he miss his train, the 6.4
  a.m. from St. James's-street to Liverpool-street, and that was due to a clock going wrong. His landlord

- **"Only one child, the eldest, could walk. They were rather weakly children, nothing sturdy."**

- He was "a great amateur musician, playing **the cornet, the flute**, and other wind instruments" —
  alongside the concertina of Elizabeth's own statement.
- "Gouldstone, in speaking of them, would always say, **'There's my Charley,' or 'There's my Herbert.'**"
- **He killed rabbits for his landlord**: "Mr. Clark keeps these animals, and Gouldstone had often served
  him by killing one, and **it was only last week he disposed of a mischievous doe**."
- He drank beer but was otherwise abstemious; **he did not smoke at all**.
- **On the beanfeast**, Clark "said he saw him at 10 o'clock at night quite sober" — which puts Clark's

- **"He chose his time and went home early on purpose, Mr. Clark ordinarily being first."** The paper's own

- The firm is printed as "the **Kirkley** wharf, 67, Upper Thames-street", almost certainly a mangling of

**Two letters, both in the paper's correspondence column.**

### 53. WILLIAM'S HEIGHT — A SECOND FIGURE (logged 22 September 2026; see Contradiction 8)

reporter's glance across a courtroom, which is what the IPN's looks like. Neither is a measurement we can
check. Do not state a height.

### RESOLVED — THE TRIAL OF LUNATICS ACT WAS PUT TO THE JURY
`notes/part_three_context_law_and_medicine.md` records as **OPEN**: "does the Old Bailey transcript, the
Times of 15 September, or the Essex Weekly News show the Act being mentioned at the trial at all? A search
of the transcript for the Act's wording finds nothing." It was mentioned. Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian,
22 September, reporting Day's summing-up:

> "His lordship, in conclusion, explained to the jury that, by the provisions of a recent Act of Parliament,
> if they found that the prisoner was insane at the time he committed the act, they would have to return a
> special verdict finding that the prisoner did commit the offence, but that he was insane at the time."

**The jury retired at twenty minutes past five and were absent about a quarter of an hour** (same report;
the Daily Chronicle independently says "fifteen minutes' consideration"). So the new verdict was explained
to them, in terms, and they took a quarter of an hour to return the old one. The project's working claim
was that a jury which had the new form available returned the old one; it is now sourced, and the file
should be updated.

### GRANTHAM ROBINSON'S STATEMENT — VERY LIKELY FOUND, AND IT IS PUBLIC
The project has an open question: "What was Grantham Robinson's statement to the Home Secretary? Likely the
same document as the BMJ's 'clear statement of the Vicar of the parish in which he lived.'"
`dramatis_personae.md` downgraded "wrote to the Home Secretary" to unresolved on 15 September 2026, noting
that a second public statement was at least as plausible a reading. Here is a public statement, over his
name, dated 20 September 1883, reprinted by the Guardian on the 22nd:

> "I have just read Mrs. Gouldstone's letter in your paper this morning, and write a line to confirm the
> truth of her statements. From strict personal inquiries, and as the result of a long interview with the
> prisoner in the House of Detention, I firmly believe that he was not in his right mind when he committed
> the fearful deed, and had the question of insanity, which has been undoubtedly proved to be hereditary on
> both sides of his family, been brought before the jury, I am certain the verdict would have been
> otherwise. I may add that I have not seen his wife since her husband's conviction. **We are getting up a
> petition in Walthamstow, praying for a reprieve.**"

**Assessment, and it is an inference, not a proof.** The BMJ of 29 September pairs "the touching narrative
of his wife" with "the clear statement of the Vicar of the parish in which he lived". Both of those
documents were in the public press in the week before the BMJ wrote: her statement on 20 September and his
letter on the 20th. A medical weekly telling its readers that "no medical man can read" two documents is
most naturally describing documents its readers could read. **If that is right, neither document is a lost
Home Office paper**, and the project's standing description of Elizabeth's "fuller narrative to the Home
Secretary" as the most valuable undiscovered document in the archive rests on a reading of the BMJ that
this letter weakens. It does not destroy it: the BMJ could still mean submissions to the Home Office, and
Elizabeth may well have written separately to Harcourt. But the simpler reading now has evidence on its
side and the open question should be reframed rather than left as it stands. **Also new: a reprieve
petition was got up in Walthamstow.** No copy is in the archive.

### ELIZABETH'S STATEMENT — FIRST PUBLICATION IS THURSDAY 20 SEPTEMBER
The project dates it "published c.21 September" from the Essex Weekly News. Her own text says she saw
William at Chelmsford "yesterday (Tuesday)", which is 18 September, so she gave it on Wednesday the 19th;
Grantham Robinson replies on Thursday the 20th having read it "in your paper this morning". The Guardian is
a Saturday weekly, so both were written to a daily. **First publication: Thursday 20 September 1883, in a
London daily not yet identified.** The Essex Weekly News of the 21st and the Guardian of the 22nd both
reprint it. The Guardian's text and the Essex Weekly News text agree word for word as far as they have been
collated, which is the opening third; the rest should be collated before the statement is quoted at length.

### SAVAGE WROTE THREE LETTERS IN FOUR DAYS, AND THEY ARE DIFFERENT DOCUMENTS
1. **The Lancet, dated Bethlem Hospital, 14 September** (already in the archive) — written on the day of
   the verdict, for a medical readership, and the only one of the three that contains "he told me himself,
   but this was not brought out in evidence, that he had very bad dreams and had seen heaven opened and had
   heard the voice of God, but, he added, he knew it was only dreaming", the double hernia, and the extra
   drink taken to fit himself for the crime.
2. **The Times, dated 15 September** (new, via the Guardian) — for the public, arguing for further
   investigation, and it reports that "**the Medical Officer to the House of Detention told me he
   considered him to be suffering from melancholia**". That is a second doctor's opinion, before Orange and
   Clarke, and it is new to the project.
3. **A reply to "One of the Jury", Bethlehem Hospital, 17 September** (new, via the Guardian) — the sharpest
   of the three on the trial itself: "The skilful cross-examination of Mr. Poland gave me no opportunity of
   representing my own opinion on the man's sanity… The judge opposed strongly attempts to get my opinion,
   believing the common sense of the jury to be the best judge of sanity."
Do not treat any of the three as the same letter.

### A JURYMAN FROM THE TRIAL WROTE TO THE PRESS
Signed "ONE OF THE JURY", Tottenham, 15 September, the day after the verdict. He explains what the jury
took from Savage's evidence: that Savage, asked by Poland whether he would certify the prisoner insane,
"distinctly said 'No'". The Daily News treats this letter as representing the view of the rest of the jury,
and Savage wrote his reply of the 17th specifically to correct it. **The trial jury's own account of why it
convicted therefore exists, in print, from a juror's hand, two days after the verdict.** This is Chapter
Twelve material of the first order.

### THE WORKMEN'S LETTER, IN FULL
Signed "WILLIAM GRAVES, Foreman", 67 Upper Thames-street, 15 September: "We, being all the men who work in
the smiths' shop in which William Gouldstone was employed, unhesitatingly say that he was of unsound mind
when he committed the murder. We base this opinion upon the intimate daily intercourse we have had with him
during the last four years and a half, and **we consider it a great hardship that we were not allowed to
put this evidence before the jury**." The Daily News takes up the same point: "we are quite at a loss to
understand why they were not permitted to give evidence as they wished to do."

### EDWARD SMITH OF WALTHAMSTOW WROTE AT LEAST TWICE
The man whose letter of 11 August gave Chapter Eight its title ("uncommonly fine children") wrote again on
19 September, a long argument for detention at Her Majesty's pleasure and for petitions, containing: "Would
the little children on whose graves the flowers are still fresh… would their little voices be up-lifted at
the Throne of Grace for a parent's blood?" He gives his address as 6 Colchester-road on the first letter
and "Walthamstow" on the second. Identity unknown; a rate-book candidate.

### CHELMSFORD, 14 TO 22 SEPTEMBER — NEW DETAIL
- **Arrival**: Friday night, 14 September, by the 8.12 p.m. train, in the charge of Mr Thomson of the
  office of Messrs Gepp and Sons, the Acting Under Sheriffs, and two warders from Springfield Gaol; taken
  to the prison in a cab and placed in the condemned cell.
- His dark tweed suit exchanged for prison dress; **his head shorn of "the light curls, which tended to
  make him somewhat prepossessing in appearance"**.
- Two officers told off to watch him, one by day and one by night; "he is supplied with everything he asks
  for"; "Gouldstone eats and drinks heartily, and takes his rest with the felicity of a child".
- **The chaplain is the Rev. W. Faithful Lumley**, "unremitting in his attentions to him"; William "attends
  service daily".
- **On his first Sunday under sentence of death, 16 September, the Psalm for the day was Psalm 79, whose
  twelfth verse the congregation says in response: "Oh let the sorrowful sighing of the prisoners come
  before thee; according to the greatness of Thy power preserve thou those that are appointed to die." The
  paper points out that the verse is an even one, so the convict said it aloud with the other prisoners.**
- **Tuesday 18 September**: visited by the High Sheriff of Essex, **Mr J. Oxley Parker**, and by his wife,
  his mother and his sister. Elizabeth's own account has his sister and his aunt accompanying her, and has
  him saying, of her, to his relatives: "You will take care of her, won't you?"
- **The execution was fixed for Monday 1 October at H.M. Prison, Springfield.** Marwood, the executioner,
  had died earlier that month, so it fell to the High Sheriff to appoint a hangman, and "**several
  applications for the office have been made both at the Gaol and to the High Sheriff**".

### THE PRESS, COUNTED BY THE LOCAL PAPER
The Guardian of 22 September says the dailies "are exactly divided in opinion, three approving of the death
penalty and an equal number pleading for further inquiry into the state of Gouldstone's mind", and prints
all six at length: **for the sentence**, The Times, the Daily Chronicle and the Morning Advertiser;
**against it**, the Daily Telegraph, the Morning Post and the Daily News. All six are now transcribed. That
count, and that division, is a ready-made structure for the press chapter.

### PRESS-TIER, NOT SWORN: "I HAVE HAD IT ON MY MIND FOR A LONG TIME"
The Daily Telegraph's leader quotes this as "one of the remarks which the murderer made when arrested". It
is not in Cheeseman's witness statement, his coroner deposition, or his Old Bailey evidence. Treat as press
unless it turns up in a sworn document.

### THE RELIEF FUNDS, AND WHERE THE MONEY WENT
- **1 September (Guardian leader):** three separate efforts running at once — Grantham Robinson's
  subscription, "the first to extend a helping hand"; the Rev. T. Jackson's open-air meeting collection; and
  a variety entertainment by the "Club Minstrels" at the Workmen's Hall, which the paper thinks was kindly
  meant and in poor taste. The paper urges that they be concentrated in one channel **"so that a sufficient
  sum of money might be available to start her in some way of business"**, and flatly doubts a Daily News
  report of a public meeting at Walthamstow: "we are not aware of any public meeting having been held, and
  we are generally well informed of matters of such importance".
- **11 October (Graves's letter, printed 20 October):** "We have paid the sum of £50 for the law expenses,
  but only got in £36 from subscriptions, so that not only are we short of the bare sum wanted to meet the
  expenses, but we have nothing to give to the wife." Elizabeth "now stands alone in utter destitution".
- Read the two together and the fund's story is complete: it was opened to set her up in a business and it
  did not cover the lawyers.

### THE REMOVAL TO BROADMOOR, DATED FROM THE OTHER END
Guardian, 20 October: "The convict Wm. Gouldstone left H.M.'s Prison, Chelmsford, on Monday morning, in
charge of Warders Lanham and Parker, en route for Broadmoor Criminal Lunatic Asylum. They proceeded to
Liverpool-street by the 7.45 a.m. train from Chelmsford Station." Monday, in a paper of Saturday 20
October, is **15 October 1883**, confirmed a Monday and already the established Broadmoor admission date.
The two now agree, and this is the only account of the journey itself in the archive.

### THE LAW TIMES ON THE CASE (Guardian, 29 September)
The legal profession's own journal, reprinted locally two days before the original execution date, arguing
from this case that M'Naghten should be enlarged: "though the murderer perfectly understood the nature of
his act and its consequences, nevertheless **his control over his own actions was suspended to such an
extent that he was no longer a free agent**, but became the victim of an insane and irresistible impulse.
It is hard to comprehend on what principle a man should be hung for committing an act which it is
impossible for him to resist." Full text and citations in the transcription. This is the legal counterpart
to the BMJ of 22 September and belongs in
`notes/part_three_context_law_and_medicine.md`.
