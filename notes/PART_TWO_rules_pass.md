### THE PHYSIOLOGY OF DROWNING (paragraph 4), sources recorded 21 October 2026
- D. Szpilman, J. J. L. M. Bierens, A. J. Handley and J. P. Orlowski, "Drowning", New England Journal of Medicine,
  366 (22), 2012, pp. 2102-2110 (copy at lifesavingsociety.com/media/128941/nejmra1013317.pdf): a person who can no
  longer keep the airway clear first spits out or swallows the water; "The next conscious response is to hold one's
  breath, but this lasts for no more than about a minute"; "When the inspiratory drive is too high to resist, some
  amount of water is aspirated into the airways"; hypoxaemia "quickly leads to loss of consciousness and apnea"; "The
  whole drowning process, from submersion or immersion to cardiac arrest, usually occurs in seconds to a few minutes".
- Period corroboration: R. S. Tracy, "The Question of Pain in Drowning", Popular Science Monthly, vol. 13, May 1878
  (Wikisource): breath can be held "for a minute, very many for a minute and a half"; after that "we breathe in spite
  of ourselves"; the drowning person "swallows a quantity of water, sucks some of it into his lungs".
- So the prose's "He holds his breath for as long as he can, which is not long, and then he breathes, because past a
  certain point the body overrules him, and what comes in is water" is supported (the adult figure is about a minute;
  a child's is shorter: "not long" claims no number). "Which means they were swallowing while it happened": supported
  by Szpilman (swallowing is part of the process in a conscious person) read with Gould's finding ("The stomach
  contained some half digested food mixed with water"). "Not quick": the process runs "seconds to a few minutes",
  more than one breath-hold; acceptable as stated.
- STILL TO DO, optional: a Victorian forensic manual (A. S. Taylor, Medical Jurisprudence) on water in the stomach as a
  sign of life at submersion, which is how Gould's finding would have been read in 1883.
