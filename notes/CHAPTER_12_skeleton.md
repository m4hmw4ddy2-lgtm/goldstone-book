## WHERE THE NATURAL BREAKS ARE

There are three bodies of material here, of very unequal weight.

**1. The Saturday, 18 August.** Heavy. Six sworn depositions and the statement of the accused, plus two
independent press texts (The Times of 20 August, reprinted word for word by the Bury Free Press, "We take the
following report of the evidence from The Times of Monday"; and the IPN of 25 August, a different and in places
fuller text). Unlike 11 August, this hearing is in the sworn record. It could carry 1,300 to 1,600 words on its own.

**2. The four weeks between the committal and the trial.** Light. Nothing sworn happens to anyone in the story.
What exists is newsprint (two IPN covers, two Spectator paragraphs, one Guardian leader, a Daily News paragraph we do
not have), one undated fact from Elizabeth's own later statement, and two notices of the sessions. Perhaps 700 to
1,000 words, honestly.

**3. Cole.** Split across time. His crime is 18 or 19 August; the public connection between the two cases in
August is one weekly's paragraph; the substantive pairing of the two men (same court, same prosecutor, both
convicted, both examined by Orange, both reprieved, both attacked together by Tuke as "letters of blood") belongs to
October 1883 and January 1884, after his trial on 18 October and after William's reprieve.

**Proposed:** one chapter, Saturday 18 August to the eve of the trial, in three movements (below). Cole enters it
only as the public met him in August, and his trial, reprieve and the Tuke pairing go to the reprieve and medical
campaign chapter, where they are argument rather than news. See DECIDE 1 and 2.

## THE CHAPTER'S ELIZABETH THREAD

**The committal is the second tribunal to examine what she said and did, and it does so through a man's
cross-examination of other witnesses, in her absence.** Atkinson, for her husband, put questions to Ada and to
Cheeseman whose answers survive in the sworn depositions:

- Ada (image 0011): "Never heard him complain of his wife pawning his things." "I never heard the prisoner say she
  wished the children were dead" (the clerk's grammar; the Times has "She had never heard the wife say that she
  wished the children dead"). "Never heard him say a cross word to his wife."
- Cheeseman (images 0015, 0016): "I never heard the wife of prisoner say she had already wished the children dead."
  "I never heard the word 'Pawn' in his wife's presence." "She made no reply to him when he said he had murdered all
  the children... His wife was crying."

Only the answers survive, so **the questions are recoverable only as subjects, and the purpose not at all**. Why the
defence asked whether Elizabeth pawned her husband's things is not recorded, and the prose must not say (standing
caution of 23 September; see also Contradiction 56's lesson about the coroner). What can be said, in order and
without comment: the Friday jury asked whether she had wished her children dead; a juror told her on the Sunday that
the jury blamed her; the coroner on the Monday said William's sanity "concerned another tribunal"; and on the
Saturday after, before that other tribunal, her husband's solicitor asked her nurse and the arresting constable
whether she had pawned his things and whether she had wished the children dead, and both said they had never heard
it. The deposition form records that all of this was taken "in the presence and hearing of William Gouldstone"
(image 0043). She was not in the building, and could not have been called if she had been.

**Then the four weeks, in which she is written about and pictured, and in which she moves once.**
- **Pictured.** The IPN's cover of 18 August puts her at the centre of its main engraving: a woman sitting up in
  bed, mouth open, two infants on the coverlet, and a bearded man in shirtsleeves with a hammer. It is an
  engraver's invention of the moment of the attack, and it is the first picture of her in the archive. (Whether
  later papers, the IPN of 22 September or the Penny Illustrated of 6 October, pictured her again has not been
  checked; do not write "the only" until it is.) (A week later the same paper printed its portrait of "The Prisoner Gouldstone", clean-shaven but for a
  moustache. Placed side by side, the two pages correct each other without the narrator.)
- **Written about.** "Left in a destitute condition"; a subscription opened by Grantham Robinson; an open-air
  collection; a minstrel show at the Workmen's Hall, which the Guardian thought kindly meant and in poor taste; a
  public meeting reported by the Daily News that the Guardian, "generally well informed", did not believe had been
  held; and the fund's purpose in the paper's own words, "a sufficient sum of money... to start her in some way of
  business". (Graves's letter of 11 October, which belongs to a later chapter, gives the outcome: nothing left for
  her.)
- **Moving.** She went to see him, three times, at the House of Detention, and he wrote to her of the children as
  being in heaven. That is the only thing she is recorded as doing on her own account between the funeral she did
  not attend and the trial. **But it is recorded only in her own statement of 19 September**, and the Elizabeth
  thread file's instruction is that the statement is "a turn, not a sample". See DECIDE 4.

**The shape, stated once.** The chapter opens with men in a courtroom asking about her and closes with her going,
unrecorded as to date or anything said, to the prison to see the man who killed her children. If DECIDE 4 goes the
other way, it closes on her being written about, and the visits become a reveal inside her own statement later.

## THE SHAPE — three movements, chronological

### 1. Saturday 18 August: Stratford
The second tribunal, and the first with a full sworn record. In the order of the day:

- **Into the dock** a little after eleven, the court crowded (IPN). Arms folded on the dock-rail; nodding with a
  smile to someone he recognised; stretching across the dock with his head on his arms; standing erect; leaning
  on the side-rail; a tin mug of water brought for him, which he sipped (IPN, 25 August). The Times has "an air of
  perfect indifference" (the repository transcription reads "affected"; the image reads "perfect").
- **The Treasury's man.** Batchelor asks for committal and says the evidence will be short (IPN). The prosecution
  is now "on behalf of the Public Prosecutor" (Times), which completes what Craggs announced on the 11th in Chapter
  Ten ("The Treasury will take up the prosecution").
- **Ada, recalled.** Her deposition of the 9th read over and resworn (as on the 11th), then cross-examined by
  Atkinson for the first time; on the 11th he had deferred his cross-examination (Chapter Ten). What he drew from
  her: known him since the first of June (see Contradiction 58); a marked difference after the 1st of August; a
  desponding state; never addicted to drink, worse for liquor once; never short of money, never unable to support
  the children; the pawning answer; the "wished the children dead" answer; **the kleptomania answer**: "He never
  spoke to me on the Sunday previous that he had a fit of Cleptomania coming on" (image 0012, clerk's spelling);
  the cord in his hands on the fire guard, "I didn't notice a slip noose in the string"; "At the time the murder
  was committed he seemed Excited." The Times adds "not absent or wandering"; the IPN adds "I've done it, I've
  done it" (Contradiction 34: decided 1 October 2026, not used).

## COLE: WHAT CONTEMPORARIES ACTUALLY SAID CONNECTED THE CASES

The instruction was to establish this before the skeleton called him a copycat. It does not call him one.

1. **The only contemporary claim of a causal link is the Spectator's, 25 August 1883**, in its weekly news
   paragraphs, and it reports it as evidence given somewhere not named: "The evidence proved that Cole had been
   reading the story of the Walthamstow case all the Sunday, and had told his wife that he would settle his own
   children in the same way." Its own framing, opening and closing the paragraph: "Murders of the deliberate kind
   appear to breed each other" and "Gloomy cruelty is infectious." **These wordings come through a web summary of
   the archive's OCR and must be checked against the page image before any of them is quoted.** Which hearing the
   "evidence" was given at (inquest or police court) is not stated. **One text, one witness.** It is a national
   weekly summarising a report it does not name.
2. **The fullest contemporary account of Cole says nothing about Walthamstow at all.** Tuke's report of the trial in
   the Journal of Mental Science, January 1884 (quoted in Clark Bell, Medico-Legal Journal, December 1884, pp.
   349-354, in the repository): Cole, 37, labourer, of Croydon; out of work; "On the evening of the 18th he took the
   child Thomas by the legs and knocked its head against the floor and walls"; a history of insane delusions since
   1877 (his wife hiding men under the floor to poison him; electrifying him); twice discharged from the workhouse
   infirmary; tried at the Central Criminal Court on **18 October 1883 before Mr Justice Denman**, prosecuted by
   **Poland**; guilty; sentenced to death; examined on Harcourt's order by **Orange and "Dr. Glover"** (almost
   certainly Gover) and reprieved. Tuke's account attributes the killing to "a long, lasting state of delusional
   insanity", not to imitation.
3. **What pairs them in print is the law, not imitation.** Tuke: "It would be difficult indeed to conceive any
   circumstances more calculated to bring English Criminal Law into contempt than the results of the trials of
   Gouldstone and Cole for wilful murder... such commentary, written in letters of blood, has indeed been supplied by
   the occurrence of these two trials in rapid succession." Bucknill (BMJ, 15 March 1884, per MASTER_NOTES, not
   transcribed) pairs the two trials the same way. Tuke and Savage's pamphlet, *The Cases of Gouldstone & Cole*, is
   still not in hand.

### ADDED 30 September 2026: Cole's own trial record, read in full
`transcribed/OldBailey_t18831015-964_James_Cole.txt` (Old Bailey Proceedings, t18831015-964, pasted from the
website by Rik; website text, not checked against the printed page). What it settles and what it does not:

- **The date is Sunday 19 August.** Richard Cole (14), Robert Gilbert, Harriet Harding, PC Morton and Dr Martin
  Jackson all give Sunday night, 19 August, between about half past nine and half past ten. Thomas died the next
  morning: "about 25 minutes past 7" (Harding), "found it had just died" at 8 (Jackson). **Tuke's "evening of the
  18th" is wrong; the Spectator's Sunday is right. Contradiction 57 is resolved.** The house was in Pridham Road,
  West Croydon; "Thornton Heath" is where Knight, who caught him, lived. Cause of death: concussion, skull not
  fractured (Jackson).
- **No witness at the trial mentions Walthamstow, Gouldstone's case, or Cole reading a newspaper about it.** The
  only newspapers in the evidence are Cole's delusion that people were "writing about him to the newspaper"
  (Richard). What the witnesses do report him saying that Sunday: to his wife, that "sooner than a woman should do
  as she liked, he would sooner settle the lot" (Harding, about 4 p.m.; cross-examined, "Rather than they should do
  as they like I would settle the lot"); and, if she went into the workhouse, "I will kill a dear innocent baby to
  leave something on your mind" (Richard, before the coroner and again at the trial, with a dispute over "the" and
  "a"). **The Spectator's "had told his wife that he would settle his own children in the same way" shares the
  word "settle" with Harding's evidence and adds the Walthamstow connection, which the sworn trial evidence does
  not contain.** It may have come from the inquest or the Croydon police court (Richard and Harding were both
  examined there), and those reports have not been found. As things stand: one weekly paragraph asserts the link;
  the only surviving sworn account of the day does not support it.
- **Gouldstone's name is in the trial once, and only as a question.** The Wandsworth prison chaplain, cross-examined
  by Geoghegan for the defence: "I am not aware that a convict of the name of Gouldstone was under my care." Why the
  defence asked is not recorded, and William was never at Wandsworth on anything in our archive (Stratford, the
  House of Detention, Chelmsford). Note it; do not interpret it.
- **Clerkenwell, confirmed for Cole.** "Her Majesty's Prison, Clerkenwell... the prisoner has been in that prison
  since 20th August" (Treadwell, assistant surgeon); "Clerkenwell House of Detention" (Dr Thomas Jackson). So a
  Surrey prisoner committed to the Central Criminal Court in the same week as William was held at the Clerkenwell
  House of Detention. Still not proof for William; it strengthens the likelihood.
- **The same prosecutors.** "MESSRS. POLAND and MONTAGU WILLIAMS Prosecuted", as they did against William four
  weeks earlier. Geoghegan defended.
- **Cole's wife is never named and never gives evidence**, though her words run through the trial in other people's
  mouths ("Oh, Jim, what are you doing?", Richard; "Good God, Jim, what are you doing of?", Harding). The defence
  doctor went to Cole in prison telling him "I was a friend of his wife's", and had examined the wife himself: Cole
  had once cracked her skull with a spoon. This is the case in which Tuke says, in print, that the wife "could not
  give evidence in court". It is her silence, not Elizabeth's; the parallel belongs in the notes.

**Consequence for DECIDE 2 and 3.** The recommendation stands and is firmer. If Cole appears in this chapter at all,
it is as the Spectator's paragraph, named and dated, and the prose must not treat its claim as established.
Correction 10 below should now also read: Cole's killing was Sunday 19 August; the child died the following morning.

**Recommendation: "copycat" never appears in our voice.** If the August link is used, it is the Spectator's
sentence, named and dated, placed in the four weeks as something the public read. Whether Cole had in fact been
reading about Walthamstow is not established by anything better than that paragraph, and the trial account that
survives does not mention it.

**DECIDE 1. The chapter's span.** (a) Saturday 18 August to the eve of the trial, one chapter, three movements
(recommended: the four weeks are too thin to stand alone and too important to drop). (b) The committal alone,
ending on "I reserve my defence" or on the crowd, with the four weeks opening the trial chapter. (c) The committal
alone, and the four weeks as their own short chapter. Recommendation (a); (b) is the fallback if movement one runs
long.

**DECIDE 2. Where Cole goes.** (a) Here, as the Spectator reported him on 25 August, one paragraph, attributed; his
trial, reprieve and the Tuke pairing held for the reprieve and medical-campaign chapter (recommended: it keeps the
chronology honest and lets the August reader and the January doctor each say their own thing). (b) Held entirely
for the later chapter. (c) Here in full, running forward to October. Against (c): it breaks the chapter's timeline
and spends the pairing before the reprieve that makes it pointed.

**DECIDE 3. The word "copycat".** Recommended: never in our voice; the notes, the timeline and the instructions
carry it and should be changed to "the Cole case" (see corrections below).

**DECIDE 4. Elizabeth's House of Detention visits: here, or held for her statement?** (a) State them here, flat,
from her statement without quoting it. (b) Hold them, so that the four weeks show her only as others wrote about
and pictured her, and her own statement later reveals that throughout those weeks she had been going to see him.
Recommendation (b), narrowly: it is truer to "presence as absence" and it keeps the statement a turn. The cost is
that this chapter's Elizabeth is entirely other people's, which is also its point.

**DECIDE 5. The kleptomania and pawning answers.** Both are sworn (Ada, images 0011 and 0012) and both are in two
newspapers. They are the strangest things in the day and the most likely to invite a reading. Recommended: set down

them in the notes. They are the substance of the Elizabeth thread for this chapter, so leaving them out has a cost.

**DECIDE 6. The printed forms.** Both 18 August documents are printed forms for theft: the examination cover has

through (image 0043), and the statement of the accused has "did feloniously steal, take and carry away" struck
through (image 0025). The statement is unsigned, against the form's own printed instruction. Observable facts about
the documents, like the Broadmoor register page. Use one sentence of it, or none? Recommended: the unsigned
statement, yes (it is about him); the larceny wording, notes only (it is about the clerk's stationery, and it
reaches for significance).
