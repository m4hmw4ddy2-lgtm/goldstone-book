The second difference is form. From Chapter Seven on, the book moves by dated moments: an hour, a day, a hearing.
Part One moves from census to census, so it reads as a set of tableaux. The sourced dated moments that could carry it
are already in the notes: Jemima's two weddings; the six burials, 1844-47; Elizabeth's birth and registration; census
night, 7 April 1861, which finds BOTH children (Elizabeth, six, at Brewery House; William, four, at the smithy); Emma's
death and burial, October-November 1861; census night 1871, which again finds both (Elizabeth, sixteen, in service in
Stoke Newington; William, fourteen, at the White House); Mabel's birth, May 1873; Jemima's and Thomas's deaths,
autumn 1873; William's arrival in London, autumn 1878; the wedding, 15 March 1879; census night 1881.

Chapters Five and Six are already close to the later style (Six especially). The heaviest work is Chapters One to
Four, about 5,000 words.

## DECIDE POINTS

**A. Shape.**
(a) Keep the six chapters and their order; revise within each chapter.
(b) Make it linear, alternating the two families as their lives run in parallel and converge on Frampton Park Road:
Jemima's losses, Elizabeth's birth (1855) and William's (1856), census night 1861 for both, Emma's death, census night
1871 for both, 1873 (Mabel; Elizabeth's parents), William to London (1878), the wedding (1879), then Chapters Five and
Six much as they stand.
RECOMMEND (b). The paired census nights give Part One a pulse, and two lines converging is momentum the reader feels
without being told. The opening on Elizabeth's birth certificate (ruled) can stand: Jemima's three names on it ("late
Ansell, formerly Suckling") carry her first family as background already past, which the linear rule allows before
the eighth.

**B. The governing test for Part One.** Proposed: what did each of them carry into 8 Courtenay Place? For Elizabeth:
loss, being alone in the world, her schooling, service. For William: the forge, his mother's illness, the head and the
rupture, Graves. Detail that bears on neither goes to the notes, however good.
RECOMMEND as stated.

**C. Length.** No word target. The book is about 33,500 words through Chapter Twenty-One, so cutting is not the aim;
momentum is. Standing instruction (Rik, 21 September 2026): improve without shortening; cut commentary is replaced
with sourced material, not simply removed. So what goes from Chapters One to Four is replaced by the dated moments
listed above, told as scenes from their documents.
RECOMMEND as stated.

**D. Chapter Four's essay material** (the Raven tomb, parish statistics, the St James emigrant train, the 1874
lockout). The plait collapse touches both families (Thomas's sisters Lydia and Ruth; Emma; Ann Westwood) and can stay
as a fact in its year. The rest bears on neither person under B. Note: the paragraph of general examples of how
people left was kept on Rik's authority (MASTER_NOTES, Chapter 4); this would reopen it.
RECOMMEND: keep the plait collapse; the tomb, the statistics, the train and the lockout go to the notes.

**E. William's family history before Thomas** (Edward, Robert and Rebecca, Joseph; the baby Rebecca).
RECOMMEND: one sentence of descent ("at least the fourth generation of Gouldstones in the village") and Joseph's forge;
the rest to the notes.

**F. The trial testimony in Part One** (Thomas on Emily, the juryman's "grain", Robert "in court"). NO GIVEAWAYS bars
naming the trial. Options: (a) keep the evidence and lose the courtroom framing (the facts stated as background, the
source in the notes); (b) hold Thomas's testimony for Chapter Fourteen, where it is already summarised, and give
Part One only what the record of those years shows.
(c) RULE-BREAK, FLAGGED (breaks NO GIVEAWAYS IN PART ONE): keep the courtroom. Thomas's evidence, the juryman's
"Grain is the Essex word for strangle" and Thomas not pausing for it are the most vivid lines in Part One, and the
Prologue has already told the reader that William went to Broadmoor, so a courtroom gives away little that is new.
The reader carries the knowledge that this father will one day stand in a witness box for his son.
RECOMMEND (a), with (c) as the stronger edit if Rik is willing to break the rule: his mother's illness is the most
important thing William carries under B, and Part One needs it.

**G. Inference and general custom** (the list in the table above).
RECOMMEND: all to the notes, except at most one rationed reminder of what can't be known in the whole of Part One,
placed by Rik.

**H. Working order.** One chapter at a time, as for the later chapters: for each, a short skeleton (what stays, what
goes, the dated moments, any line that needs an image check), Rik rules, then a draft, held locally until approved.
RECOMMEND starting with whichever chapter opens Part One after A is ruled.
