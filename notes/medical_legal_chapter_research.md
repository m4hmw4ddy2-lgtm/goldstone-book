## 3. THE TIMELINE OF THE DEBATE (the shape; each line to be sourced before use)
- 1843: M'Naghten's case; the judges' answers (the Rules). [held in notes; part_three_context_law_and_medicine.md]
- 1864: the asylum doctors' resolution against the right-and-wrong test (as Tallack cites it, Times 19 Sep 1883).
  [TO VERIFY the resolution itself, JMS 1864]
- 1883, 25 August: Trial of Lunatics Act. The special verdict: "guilty of the act or omission charged, but insane so
  as not to be responsible, according to law, for his actions"; detention as a criminal lunatic. Passed, by the
  standard account, at Queen Victoria's wish after attempts on her life (Maclean, 1882), to replace "not guilty on
  the ground of insanity". [Wikipedia, citing Crotty 1924 and White 1985; TEXT TO OBTAIN] Royal assent fell between
  the murders (8 August) and the trial (14 September); the defence asked for it; the jury returned guilty.
- 1883, 21 August: Court of Criminal Appeal Bill withdrawn (Hansard, recorded in CHAPTER_15_skeleton.md).
- 1883, 14 September to 1884: the Gouldstone argument in the journals (section 1).
- 1883: Stephen, History of the Criminal Law, argues for widening the test [TO VERIFY].
- 1884, 14 August: Criminal Lunatics Act. s. 2(4): for "a prisoner under sentence of death", if "there is reason to
  believe such prisoner to be insane, the Secretary of State shall appoint two or more legally qualified medical
  practitioners" to examine and certify. [vLex, read 25 October 2026] The procedure Harcourt had used for William
  in September 1883, made law the next year. Whether William's case played any part: NOT KNOWN; not to be implied.
- 1894: the BMA discussion (Weatherly and others): the test still argued over, eleven years on. [held]
- 1907: Court of Criminal Appeal established [TO VERIFY: Criminal Appeal Act 1907].
- 1922: Ronald True. Convicted at the Old Bailey 5 May 1922 of murdering Gertrude Yates; appeal dismissed 25 May;
  reprieved 8 June 1922 by the Home Secretary, Edward Shortt, after three medical experts found him insane under the
  inquiry required by the Criminal Lunatics Act 1884; sent to BROADMOOR. Public outcry (believed favoured for his
  family; the execution of Henry Jacoby the day before). [Wikipedia, "Ronald True", read 25 October 2026] WILLIAM WAS
  IN BROADMOOR THROUGHOUT (1883-1935): the same procedure, thirty-nine years on, and the same asylum.
- 1923/24: Committee on Insanity and Crime (Lord Justice Atkin), Cmd. 2005, set up after the True case (to verify
  the link from the report itself); remit included "cases under section 2(4) of the Criminal Lunatics Act, 1884".
  Recommended that a person be held irresponsible for an act "committed under an impulse which the prisoner was by
  mental disease in substance deprived of any power to resist". NOT ENACTED; a Lords attempt soon after failed.
  [Wellcome catalogue; J. V. Barry, "Insanity in the Criminal Law in Australia", Canadian Bar Review 21 (1943)]
- 1935, 19 January: William dies in Broadmoor. [held]
- 1949-1953: Royal Commission on Capital Punishment recommended that M'Naghten be abrogated and the jury left "to
  determine whether at the time of the act the accused was suffering from disease of the mind (or mental deficiency)
  to such a degree that he ought not to be held responsible." [Wikipedia; REPORT TO OBTAIN]
- 1957: Homicide Act, s. 2: diminished responsibility, a partial defence reducing murder to manslaughter: the
  practical answer, seventy-four years after the trial, to what Savage had argued. [Wikipedia; TEXT TO OBTAIN]
- 1964: Criminal Procedure (Insanity) Act: the 1883 special verdict replaced by "not guilty by reason of insanity"
  [TO VERIFY wording].
- 1965: death penalty for murder suspended [TO VERIFY: Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965].
- Today: the M'Naghten Rules "remain the principal method of deciding insanity in English law" (Wikipedia,
  "Insanity in English law", citing R v Sullivan [1984]: "it does not lie within the power of the courts to alter
  [the insanity test]"); the Butler Committee (1975) and the Law Commission (1989) proposed reform; not adopted.

## 4. THE OUTCOME, IN ONE LINE (for planning, not prose)
The test the doctors attacked in 1883 was never replaced. The law answered around it: a new verdict (1883), a
statutory medical inquiry for the condemned (1884), an appeal court (1907), diminished responsibility (1957), the end
of hanging (1965). M'Naghten itself still stands. William lived through the first fifty-two years of that, in
Broadmoor, and the man whose reprieve reopened the question in 1922 was sent to join him there.

## 6. HOW INSTRUMENTAL WERE WILLIAM'S PROFESSIONALS? (opened 25 October 2026; Rik)
CAUTION (Claude, to Rik): nothing yet shows that William's case SET a precedent or caused any change. What is shown is
that the profession took it up. The question is open; this section gathers the evidence either way.

What is known so far (secondary unless marked):
- WILLIAM ORANGE, who examined William for Harcourt in September 1883 (with Clarke): superintendent of Broadmoor from
  1870; "He also acted as a medical advisor to the Home Office on the sanity of criminals condemned to death"
  (Wikipedia, "William Orange (physician)", giving Christiana Edmunds, 1872, as an example); PRESIDENT OF THE
  MEDICO-PSYCHOLOGICAL ASSOCIATION 1883-1884, with a presidential address on the law and insanity [TO VERIFY: date,
  title, JMS October 1883?, and whether delivered before or after 8 August 1883]; attacked by a patient (Dodwell) in
  1882; retired 1886. [Held: Nicolson's obituary, BMJ 1917, which names the Gouldstone case.]
  => The Home Office's medical inquiry for condemned prisoners was ALREADY PRACTICE before William (Edmunds, 1872).
     The 1884 Act's s. 2(4) put that practice into statute. On present evidence William's case was one instance of
     it, not its origin. Do not imply otherwise.
- GEORGE HENRY SAVAGE: physician-superintendent of Bethlem; co-editor of the Journal of Mental Science; the only
  specialist examined at the trial; wrote to the Lancet, the Times and the Telegraph within days; read a paper on the
  case to the BMA's South London branch in October 1883 (item 53: TO OBTAIN, with the branch's resolution "regarding
  criminals suspected of insanity", and to whom it was sent); the JMS of January 1884 put the case "first and
  foremost" [held]; still speaking in the BMA discussion of 1894 [held]. Knighted later; died 1921 [TO VERIFY].
- So at the moment of the case, the two doctors in it were the president of the alienists' association (Orange) and
  the editor of its journal (Savage). That is a fact of position, not of influence; the chapter can show it plainly.
