## FINDINGS (what the sources say, against the working draft)
1. **Bucknill's lecture has two halves, and the second is about William's procedure.** Part one (15 March 1884)
   attacks the LAW: "Incapacity therefore is, and must be, the real test of irresponsibility"; he gives up "loss of
   self-control" as "too loose, ambiguous, and inaccurate"; his proposed law: "No act is a crime if the person who
   does it is at the time incapable of not doing it by reason of idiocy, or of disease affecting his mind." He quotes
   Day's direction to William's jury and Denman's sentence on Cole as the law "in its most unconditional simplicity".
   Part two (22 March) attacks the PROCEDURE, using Gouldstone and Cole: the reprieves "to a great extent,
   accidental"; tried "in some remote county ... both of them would have been executed"; the Home Secretary and his
   examiners "an informal court of appeal, or an appeal which is not a court"; "monstrous" that the reports are
   secret; terms that "may vary greatly, even to the length of a rope"; "if the law on this matter is bad, the
   procedure is worse." He opposes Orange's pre-trial examination (a confession "fatal to the prisoner") and jabs at
   Orange: it "would exclude his own services." Draft's Bucknill: wrong in substance (too general; no procedure).
   Draft's "superintendent of the Devon County Asylum and a leading figure in the MPA": not in these sources (he
   speaks in July 1883 of having been "medical superintendent of a county asylum"; the county is not named).
2. **The 1884 Act did the opposite of what Bucknill asked.** Five months after "monstrous", s. 2(4) (14 August 1884)
   made the secret post-sentence examination mandatory, with no oath, publication or cross-examination. The
   chapter's irony is in the dates; the prose need only set them side by side.
3. **The July 1883 meeting has a real argument in it, and Harcourt was asked to it.** Tuke read Orange's own 1876
   words back to him: "Surely it is better to abandon this obsolete test than to apply it thus." Orange: his change
   was "only a sign of growth". Yellowlees asked for graded responsibility; Orange: "It must be one thing or the
   other." Tuke "Having himself been present" at Dove's trial (the draft is right). One resolution adopted, on early
   examination; the other dropped without a vote (the draft is right). Harcourt's letter of regret was the first
   Orange read. Bucknill praised Orange's reports to the Home Secretary on condemned men; he attacked the same
   procedure seven months later. Gover was present. (All third-person report: summarise, short phrases only.)
4. **January 1884 JMS: two authors.** The Gouldstone note is Savage's own, signed, first person. The Cole note is
   "D. H. T.". The draft's "the journal" and "its writer" are Tuke. The wife who could not testify, in Tuke, is
   COLE'S. "That had been Elizabeth's position" stands only as our sentence on the law (1898 Act; notes), not as
   Tuke's point. Tuke's "five months" (for five weeks) is an error: not to be quoted.
5. **Savage's January note carries a biography of William** (mother insane at his birth; a fitter of grates; little
   meat; "considered whether he should use a revolver"; more drink than usual). His claims, after a twenty- to
   thirty-minute interview. NOT for this chapter as fact (DECIDE G).
6. **1894: Pitt Lewis's words are not reported.** He is mentioned twice, said nothing on the page. The draft's Pitt
   Lewis position has no source. The argument it resembles is W. C. H. Cross's. Gouldstone is raised by MERCIER
   (Day's latitude against Field's in Hitchens, 1887). Mercier's third objection is William's path unnamed: "an
   unsworn jury of two, without a judge, without counsel, without witnesses ... this amateur court". Weatherly
   rejects the MPA's assessor scheme as an attack on trial by jury; Goodwin Norris defends the right-and-wrong test;
   Hart wants the Lords to question the judges afresh. Three resolutions; the subcommittee named Orange and Savage.
7. **Cullingworth (read 17 January 1895):** his scaffold story is a trial he gave evidence in, and an execution he
   READ about ("and then have read how"); not "bewilderment" but "the surprised look of a man awaking from a dream".
   "Both undoubtedly insane" is his opinion. His four proposals, the poisons-analyst analogy, and his concession that
   doctors pressed irresistible impulse "unduly" are all on the page. His paper reworks an 1885 address (not held).
8. **The BMJ of 27 October 1883** (misnamed "Nov. 10" in the repository) opens Cole's case on Gouldstone: "THE echoes
   of the Gouldstone case have scarcely died away". "Given over to the hangman"; the house-of-detention test: "If a
   man can tell his own name and the day of the week, and put out his tongue to order".
9. **The 1884 Act, s. 4(1):** the Secretary of State "shall, at least once in every three years ... determine whether
   he ought to be discharged". From 1884 William's detention was by law reconsidered at least every three years.
   Whether those reviews survive: a lead (Home Office; the closed file). A usable fact of the law (DECIDE L).
10. **Shortt (1922)** says the inquiry and the trial answer "two issues which are quite distinct"; that the statute
    "is peremptory"; that he "was bound by the law of the land to reprieve him"; one examiner "had been for many
    years superintendent of Broadmoor Asylum". He quotes s. 2(4) with "sanity" for the Act's "insanity" (in both
    printings found). The draft's account of him is supported, pending the Hansard column.
11. **Corrections to my own report of 27 October:** the 1911 census WAS already transcribed and checked (25 October,
    in the 1891-1901 file); the draft's "fifty-five" is right. Tuke was at Dove's trial. One resolution in 1883.
