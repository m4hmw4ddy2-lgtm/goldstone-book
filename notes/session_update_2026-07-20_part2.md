## 1. FINCHINGFIELD — ALL FIVE HISTORIC PUBS NOW ACCOUNTED FOR

Started from an AI-generated theory (surfaced repeatedly this session) claiming "The Finchingfield Lion" was the renamed Green Man, sourced only to a modern property-listing page. This was chased to ground through multiple independent primary and near-primary sources:

- **Green Man**: burned down 1905 (fire attended by Finchingfield and Wethersfield engines; the adjacent 15th-century Guildhall was saved). Previously known as **the Bell Inn** (1740; renamed by 1782, reportedly because the church bells were cast on the site). Rebuilt post-fire, later renamed **The Finch**, now a private house. Source: finchingfield.org.uk (Roger Beckwith), a personal local-history site with contemporary before/after photographs — the single best source found on this whole thread.
- **Swan**: licensee history 1855–1925+ (Robert Pamment → Levi Howland → Thomas Eaton → Alfred Wiffen) via pubwiki.co.uk. Now a private residence at the corner of High Street and Swan Road.
- **Three Tuns**: survives, trading under its own name.
- **Red Lion**: Grade II listed (Historic England 1115632, early C16, listed 1953) as "The Red Lion Inn, Church Hill." Confirmed by Rik's own photographic identification as the building now trading as **The Finchingfield Lion** — the statutory listing keeps the old name; the pub itself doesn't.
- **Fox Inn**: Grade II listed (Historic England 1122699, c.1500, listed 1967). Trades today as **Fox on the Green**. This is its own separate historic pub, not a renamed Green Man or Swan — independently confirmed by Adrian Gray's village history, which lists Fox Inn/Red Lion/Three Tuns as the three coaching-era pubs, matching today's three trading establishments exactly.

**A widely-repeated marketing claim — that the Fox on the Green was a coaching stop on a London–Norwich route — was checked directly against the Historic England listing (states only "House, now a public house") and the pub's own official website (foxonthegreen.co.uk, both homepage and About page). Neither mentions it. The claim appears to originate from nowhere checkable and should not be used as fact.**

Finchingfield Guildhall's own published history confirms **five pubs were still operating as late as 1922** — consistent with all five identities now being accounted for.

## 2. THE WINDMILL, DUCK END — FULLY DOCUMENTED

Grade II listed (Historic England 1122733). Early C18 post mill, moved to the site and erected on the mill mound before 1840; brick roundhouse added 1840. Four single-shuttered spring sails on an all-wooden windshaft (rare, now dismantled but surviving). Carved dates inside, earliest **1753**. Belonged to the Spains Hall estate. **Last worked c.1890** — independently confirmed twice (the listing itself, and Finchingfield Guildhall's own history describing it as "the smallest [windmill] of its kind in Essex... ceased working in 1890"). Restored by the parish, then Essex County Council (1958, and again from 1984). Confirmed still standing today, maintained as a heritage attraction, open to visitors on selected days (photograph, 2026).

## 3. GREAT SAMPFORD — THE COCK INN CORRECTION

**Important correction to the 19/20 July note.** That note stated the windmill/smithy/Cock Inn cluster had "not survived under any name." This was wrong for the Cock Inn specifically.

**The Cock Inn is Grade II listed** (Historic England 1112277, "Hillcrest," c.1686, listed February 1980) and **survives today as a private house called Hillcrest** — confirmed by Rik's direct photographic identification. The associated Historic England map also shows "Mill House" and "The Corn Mill" as genuine nearby modern features, distinct from Hillcrest itself. The working mill and smithy have genuinely not survived under any name — that part of the original note stands; only the pub itself was wrongly marked as lost.

**Full Cock Inn licensee history** (bygoneboozers.co.uk — a rigorously sourced project citing the British Newspaper Archive, census returns, and trade directories throughout): mentioned Ipswich Journal, 16 October 1756 (John Andrews). Pigot's 1839 (Robert Cocke). 1841 census (John Belsham). White's 1848 and 1851 census (**John Metcalf, wheelwright** — also a carpenter per White's 1848, the same multi-trade pattern as Thomas Gouldstone). **William Piggott from 1861** (corrected from the earlier, vaguer "by 1874") for over forty years, through Kelly's 1906. Closed 1914 (East Anglian Daily Times, 6 Feb 1914, "on the grounds of redundancy").

**The Bull/Black Bull** — same source. Licensees include **William Philpott** (White's 1848 — worth cross-checking against the 15 July note's "William Philpott, grocer," 1861 census neighbour; likely the same multi-trade man, unconfirmed) and **Sarah Davis**, independently matching the existing 1874 Post Office Directory citation. Survived as a working pub to c.1997; now a private house, **Bull House**.

**A third historic Great Sampford pub, "the White Horse,"** was flagged by the same source as lost but has not been investigated at all. Genuinely open — worth checking next, along with the "Fighting Cocks" name that surfaced separately (see Section 5).

**The Red Lion Inn was built around 1830** (Adrian Gray) — the only one of Great Sampford's own historic pubs confirmed still trading under its own name today.

A Colne Stour Countryside Association magazine article ("Brewing in East Anglia," Mark Dawson, researched with the Brewery History Society) independently confirms both **Finchingfield and Wethersfield** had their own breweries in this period — a real citation for the Finchingfield brewery claim, which had previously rested only on inference from Malting Yard's industrial character.

## 5. ADRIAN GRAY'S SAMPFORDS HISTORY — MAJOR NEW MATERIAL

Source: recordinguttlesfordhistory.org.uk, drawing on the Sampfords Society's "Heritage Sampford" archaeology-mapping project. Appears twice on the site (combined Sampfords page and Great Sampford's own page) — identical text both times, not two separate sources.

**Corroborates existing notes almost verbatim**: the 1096 grant to Battle Abbey, the Rural Deanery of 21 parishes, and — strikingly — the exact detail already in MASTER_NOTES about the church being unable to afford better than a corrugated-iron nave roof even after Victorian refurbishment. Also independently matches Vision of Britain's population figures (597 in 1801, 906 in 1851, Great Sampford).

**New, genuinely load-bearing material**:
- **"The 1871 census recorded 9 people living in sheds."**
- Cholera struck in 1866; typhoid recurred several times through the 1870s.
- Of 285 houses across both Sampfords in 1851, only 184 remained inhabited seventy years later.
- **"In 1901 the population was 496 and 296, with many people leaving for the towns, especially London, as the ease of travel increased."** This is the single most thematically important line found all session — it explicitly names London as the destination of the Sampfords' own exodus, the same city, the same decades, that drew Elizabeth Stock out of Wethersfield. Two branches of the family, two different villages, one shared regional collapse, one shared destination.
- A new Baptist Chapel was built in Great Sampford in the 1870s, "in the midst of the depression and exodus" — matches the Baptist Chapel already found independently on the OS sheet.
- The Red Lion Inn built c.1830 (see Section 3).

## 6. WATER SUPPLY — GREAT SAMPFORD

A specialist wells-and-pumps gazetteer (recordinguttlesfordhistory.org.uk), citing Dr Thresh's 1905 report on the water supply of Essex: **"Great Sampford: ...there is no public water supply and the residents get their water supply from shallow wells and springs."** Little Sampford had one public pump. Real, physical, daily-life detail — every household in Great Sampford drawing its own water by hand throughout the whole of Thomas Gouldstone's working life there, no communal system at all.

## 7. ORAL HISTORY — FREDERICK BROOK, "MEMORIES OF CHILDHOOD IN LITTLE SAMPFORD"

Recorded 1980 by Dorothy Lee from her father, Frederick Brook (b. 5 March 1905, Clock House Farm). Genuine primary-adjacent testimony, one generation after the family's own residence in the area. Confirms regular chapel attendance at Finchingfield, a blacksmith visited in Great Sampford, a shoemaker ("Mr Richardson, by the Causeway"), farm and threshing life in real detail.

**Three unconnected Stock namesakes appear**:
- A "Mr Stock" who killed pigs for local farms.
- **James Stock**, who thatched straw stacks.
- **Sammy Stock**, an old man living alone at Clock House cottage (one room, earth floor) — a vivid, specific portrait: singing home from "The Fighting Cocks" on Saturday nights, playing his accordion, found dying on his own doorstep after a stroke.

Given how many Stocks this corner of Essex has now produced across tonight's research (John Legerton Stock and William Stock Arnold, Finchingfield's 1863 directory; Harry Stock, Finchingfield's war memorial; now these three), this may reflect a real extended local family, or simply that Stock was a very common surname here. **Unconfirmed either way — do not assume any connection to our Thomas Stock line without direct record-checking.**

A pub called **"The Fighting Cocks"** is named — not yet identified against anything else found tonight. Possibly Little Sampford's own; possibly related to the still-uninvestigated "White Horse" lead; genuinely unknown.
