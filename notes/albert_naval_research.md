# Albert Goldstone naval research: consolidated session note

**Date:** 14 October 2026
**Revised:** 14 October 2026, the same evening, after the press clips were checked against their images and further sources were read. The corrections are listed in section 18.
**Scope:** Research completed in this session into the ships and operational settings on Albert Jennet Goldstone's Royal Navy service sheet (ADM 188/383), with particular attention to sources that overlap his exact dates aboard.
**Status of this note:** a research summary (a lead), not a source. Each finding is to be cited from the source it names. Selection and decisions for Chapter Twenty are recorded in `notes/CHAPTER_20_skeleton.md`, which governs where this note differs.

## 1. Working evidential rule

For Part Five, a ship's documented actions may be used as part of Albert's lived chronology when his service sheet has him continuously borne on that ship across the dated event (plan DECIDE 8, amended). We still distinguish between:

- **Albert's presence:** established by his service sheet.
- **The ship's action or movement:** established by a contemporary or official source saved to the repository.
- **Albert's personal action, station or thoughts:** not to be asserted unless separately evidenced.

The strongest results are therefore events where Albert's service dates and an independently dated ship source overlap exactly.

**Caution: borne is not always aboard.** The sheet records the books a man was carried on. A man drafted out to a distant station could be borne on a ship's books while still on passage to her. Before an event abroad is given as his, check that he had joined the ship by its date (see section 9, Powerful).

**The sheet's breaks matter.** No ship event inside a break in his entry may be given as his. In this period the breaks are 21 December 1904 to 3 January 1905 (Majestic), 2 to 11 March 1906 and 5 to 7 August 1907 (Jupiter). They match the three spells in the cells, but that match is an inference and is not for prose.

## 2. ADM 53 at The National Archives

The Discovery catalogue confirms that the relevant ship logs survive in **ADM 53**. The high-value volumes located today are not digitised and are available only at Kew.

### Prince George

- **Reference:** ADM 53/24983 (as given by Discovery; not re-checked in the session).
- **Coverage:** 8 October 1903 to 14 July 1904.
- **Albert aboard:** 9 September 1903 to 14 July 1904.
- The volume begins nine days before the collision of 17 October 1903 and runs to Albert's last day aboard. It does not cover his first month.
- It should contain the collision, damage control, Ferrol, temporary repairs and the return to Portsmouth.
- Current status: **Kew only.** Useful later for scene-level detail, not required to establish the event.

### Tyne / TB31

- ADM 53 logs for *Tyne* and TB31 survive, also Kew only.
- Online evidence is already strong, because the logs of TB35 and TB36 name TB31 during Albert's posting (section 4).
- Kew priorities: 29 to 30 August 1914; 1 July 1915; 29 February 1916; 22 June 1916.

### Courageous and Alexander

- Logs survive; Kew only.
- *Courageous* priority: 16 to 18 November 1917, the Second Battle of Heligoland Bight on the 17th.
- *Alexander* priority: April to July 1918, especially the Murmansk to Archangel passage and 10 July 1918 at Archangel (section 12).

**Conclusion:** ADM 53 is a defined later research layer, not an immediate online source.

### Organisational context

- Pre-war material places **TB31 in the 8th Destroyer Flotilla at Chatham** (source to be named before use).

The reading "Tyne (T.B.31)" (read by Rik, 13 October 2026, with RMG's "TB 31") is strongly supported: Albert was borne on *Tyne*'s books for TB31, an operational torpedo boat of the flotilla.

## 5. TB31 contemporary press: the crew describes its own war

Source: `sources/images/Media-coverage/tb31/Western_Times_29_October_1914_0002_Clip.jpg`; transcription `transcribed/tb31_transcriptions.txt` (**not yet image-checked**).

The *Western Times* of 29 October 1914 printed a letter dated **26 October 1914** from an officer of H.M. Torpedo Boat 31, signed **Harry M. L. Scott**, thanking the Mayoress of Exeter for warm clothing sent to the boat. He wrote that "a good proportion of us know from former experience what winter in the North Sea means to those serving in torpedo craft, even in peace time, when the work is naturally somewhat less arduous than at present."

It is from Albert's own boat, written during his posting, contemporary, and describes the crew's conditions rather than only the boat's movements.

## 6. Prince George, 1903 to 1904

**Albert aboard:** 9 September 1903 to 14 July 1904 (no break; rated Ordinary Seaman 19 June 1904).
**Collision:** the night of Saturday 17 October 1903.
**Source folder:** `sources/images/Media-coverage/prince-george-ferrol/`; transcription `transcribed/prince_george_ferrol_transcriptions.txt` (**image-checked word by word in full**, 14 October 2026).

### What the contemporary coverage establishes

- The collision was with **HMS Hannibal** during Channel Fleet night manoeuvres off Cape Finisterre, about eighty miles from Corunna.
- Several reports say the fleet was manoeuvring **with lights extinguished / without lights**.
- **The best witness is the Admiral's own telegram**, dated 18 October and printed by the London Evening Standard and the Western Evening Herald: *Hannibal* collided with *Prince George* "during turning movement on the night of the 17th October ... aft submerged torpedo flat of Prince George filled with water. No damage to Hannibal ... No one hurt."
- **The side struck was the port side.** Every contemporary report that names a side says port (side or quarter). Burt, via Wikipedia, says starboard and is the outlier.
- The fleet entered Ferrol on Sunday 18 October: eight battleships and three cruisers.
- She was repaired at Ferrol, reached Portsmouth on **Monday 26 October** escorted by the cruiser Sutlej (printed "Sutley"), and was docked there.
- The Western Times of 27 October, from "those on board": collision quarters sounded, the crew went below to close the remaining watertight compartments, collision mats were got out, and "the whole Fleet's carpenters" built a "copper dam" over nearly a week. She was towed off on Friday [23 October] and found watertight.
- Captain Frederick L. Campbell was court-martialled and **acquitted**.
- The *Southern Daily Mail* of 3 November reported that the First Lord assembled the crew "this afternoon" and gave them the King's message praising "the calmness and courage they displayed after the collision". The day of the assembly is to be confirmed before it is given.

### Where the papers disagree (recorded; not to be harmonised)

- **Beached or repaired afloat:** Reuter (19 October) says beached; the Leeds Mercury's correspondent and Central News (20 October) say she was to be repaired afloat, with beaching only as a last resort; the Western Times (27 October) says she was beached.
- **Towed or under her own power:** the Madrid correspondent says she was "being towed here, being unable to use her own steam or engines"; Burt says she steered with her engines.
- **The hour:** "ten o'clock" (Western Times, from those aboard); about 9.30 (Vanner, from Beresford's memoirs); "early yesterday morning" (Reuter in the Southern Echo, wrong against the Admiral's telegram).
- **Water and damage figures** vary between early reports.

### Shared copy (count each as one witness)

- One Reuter text from Ferrol, 19 October, runs in the London Evening Standard, the Pall Mall Gazette, the Western Evening Herald and the Western Times of 20 October.
- One Madrid correspondent's text runs in the Morning Leader and the Western Evening Herald.
- One Central News telegram from Corunna runs in the Liverpool Evening Express and the London Evening Standard.

### Narrative value

A fully supported Albert episode: his sheet has him aboard continuously across the collision, and the British press, with the Admiral's own telegram, describes it in detail. ADM 53/24983 remains desirable for log-level sequence.

## 7. Jupiter, 1905 to 1908

**Albert aboard:** 15 August 1905 to 1 March 1906; 12 March 1906 to 4 August 1907; 8 August 1907 to 3 February 1908 (Able Seaman 1 January 1908).
**Source folder:** `sources/images/Media-coverage/jupiter/`; transcription `transcribed/jupiter_transcriptions.txt` (**image-checked**, except parts of two long clips; see its header).

### Key findings

- She was **recommissioned at Chatham on 15 August 1905** by Captain H. A. Warren, "late of the Hannibal", for the Channel Fleet, to replace the battleship Revenge (Morning Advertiser, 16 August 1905). The North Star (16 August) says she had been refitted at a cost of £60,000 and "was manned at Chatham yesterday".
- His sheet has him joining **that same day**: he joined her on the day she commissioned. Burt also has her Channel Fleet service end on 3 February 1908, the day his entry closes.
- She left Chatham for Sheerness about 4 September, then went to Portsmouth to join the Reserve Division "until her crew is completed for service in the Channel fleet" (Western Morning News, 2 September; Naval & Military Record, 14 September 1905).
- **Aim-corrector trials:** to Spithead on Tuesday 21 November 1905 "in order to carry out 'aim corrector' trials" (Portsmouth Times, 25 November 1905); back in harbour on Tuesday 19 December, "having been at Spithead for some time in order to carry out aiming director trials" (Portsmouth Times, 23 December 1905). This dates the trials inside his first entry and agrees with RMG HTN/116/B (Hamilton papers), which holds A. H. Pollen's reports on "aim correction trials on HMS Jupiter 1905-06".
- **January 1906:** a sailor named **Corke** was killed aboard at Berehaven on Friday 26 January, "jammed by a large block" (Stamford Mercury, 2 February 1906). She sailed for Portsmouth the next day, with trials of "the previously mentioned North Country bituminous coal" (Evening Irish Times, 30 January 1906).
- **Late March 1906:** Channel Fleet mining exercises at Berehaven (the Morning Post, via the Evening Irish Times of 4 April 1906): "An E M mine-field was laid by the Jupiter"; eighteen 500lb counter-mines in two lines of nine were fired; "the surface was strewn with fish killed or stunned by the concussion". This falls in his second entry if "last week" means the week before the Morning Post's own date, which the clip does not give. To confirm from the Morning Post.
- **Late May 1906:** Jupiter and Ocean, off Portland, took ammunition aboard at sea from the steamship John Sanderson's specially fitted pinnaces (Birmingham Daily Post, 5 June 1906). Inside his second entry.
- **August 1905:** Lloyd's List (26 August) reports that the brigantine *Caroline*, of Marstal, had put in at Dover "after being run into by H.M.S. Jupiter". The date of the collision is not in the clip; the earlier report is needed before use.
- Not Albert: Frederick George Chant, able seaman of Jupiter, dealt with for drunkenness at Gillingham (Chatham paper, 26 August 1905).

### Narrative value

He joined a newly recommissioned Jupiter on the day she commissioned, and was aboard during experimental fire-control work, mining exercises and trials of taking ammunition aboard at sea.

## 8. Shannon, 1908 to 1910

**Albert aboard:** 10 March 1908 to 28 February 1910 (no break).
**Source folder:** `sources/images/Media-coverage/shannon/`; transcription `transcribed/shannon_transcriptions.txt` (**image-checked word by word in full**).

### Key findings

- The *Western Mail*, 11 March 1908: Shannon "was on Tuesday commissioned by Captain J. A. Fergusson for service in the Fifth Cruiser Squadron with the Home Fleet at the Nore as flagship of Rear-Admiral Callaghan. The Shannon is the largest and most powerful cruiser in commission". Tuesday was 10 March.
- The *Sheerness Guardian*, 14 March 1908, says she was commissioned at Chatham "On Wednesday" (11 March). **The papers differ by a day.** The sheet's 10 March agrees with the Western Mail.
- His sheet has him joining on **10 March 1908**: again, very probably on the day she commissioned.
- **Correction to the notes:** the skeleton's earlier "commissioned 19 March 1908 (Burt)" and "his entry opens nine days before she commissioned" are wrong against the press. Burt's date may be her readiness for sea.
- She was laid down at Chatham on 2 January 1905, cost "over £1,400,000", designed speed 23 knots, four 9.2in and ten 7.5in guns (Sheerness Guardian).
- On Monday 16 March she was taken into dock "to be finally prepared for sea" (Chatham, Rochester and Brompton newspaper, 21 March 1908).
- The *West Lothian Courier* (20 March 1908) prints the year's **programme**: gunlayers' tests at the Nore in May, Queensferry as the squadron's headquarters for most of the summer, exercises in June and July, battle practice near the Moray Firth in August. **This is a plan printed in advance, not a record of what was done.** A lead only.
- Her collision with Prince George (Wikipedia: 5 December 1907; its own footnote: Conway's gives December 1909) is not in these clips. If December 1909, it falls inside his entry. Unresolved.

### Narrative value

The commissioning-day overlap gives the posting a clean anchor. What she actually did in 1908 to 1910 still needs a record of events rather than a programme.

## 9. Powerful, 1911

**Albert's entry:** 16 September to 16 December 1911.
**Source folder:** `sources/images/Media-coverage/powerful/`; transcription `transcribed/powerful_transcriptions.txt` (**not yet image-checked**, except the Lloyd's Weekly clip below).

### Was he aboard? To settle first

The Lyttelton Times of 23 September 1911 says the drillships Cambrian, Pyramus, Pioneer and Psyche "are each bringing out men from training at Colombo from H.M. ships Gibraltar and **Crescent**". Albert's sheet has him in Crescent from 8 August to 15 September 1911, then Powerful from 16 September. The Naval & Military Record (22 November 1911, from a letter of 8 October) says "the Psyche and Pioneer will not arrive as early as was expected". **If he came out in one of the drillships, he may have been borne on Powerful's books while still on passage, and not aboard her for the October exercises.** This is the likeliest route to Australia so far found, and it must be settled before any Powerful event in September or October is given as his.

### Key findings in the period of his entry

- Powerful was flagship of the Australian (Australasian) Squadron.
- She went to Jervis Bay to prepare for battle practice.
- With Encounter and the Commonwealth destroyers Yarra and Parramatta she left Sydney for Port Phillip. On two nights the destroyers made night attacks on the cruisers, "the latter being manned and armed to resist destroyer attack, all lights being darkened on board"; "the heavy weather prevented the destroyers from achieving any success" (Naval & Military Record, 22 November 1911).
- She was one of three ships to fire in the battle practice at Melbourne.
- Melbourne in Cup Week (early November); Sydney; Auckland on 20 November; Wellington; Lyttelton on 5 December.
- At Lyttelton, Lieutenant H. H. L. Pennell of the Terra Nova made an official call, and Admiral King-Hall then visited Captain Scott's Antarctic expedition ship (Lyttelton Times, 6 December 1911).
- "The crew of the 'Powerful' will be transferred to the Drake" (Times of Ceylon, 27 November 1911). Albert did not go with them: his sheet has him in Pioneer from 17 December.
- **Lloyd's Weekly Newspaper, 10 December 1911** (transcribed and image-checked in full, 14 October 2026): "The cruiser Powerful, which is returning to Devonport to take the place of the old training ship Impregnable". Impregnable was Albert's first ship (November 1901). A juxtaposition that may point; for Rik to rule.

### Narrative value

The New Zealand circuit in November and December 1911 falls well inside his entry. The October night attacks are the standout event, but only once his presence aboard by then is established.

## 10. Pioneer, 1911 to 1912

**Albert aboard:** 17 December 1911 to 23 May 1912.
**Source folder:** `sources/images/Media-coverage/pioneer/`; transcription `transcribed/pioneer_transcriptions.txt` (**not yet image-checked**).

### New Zealand movements and training

The papers describe Pioneer as the drill ship (Royal Naval Reserve training ship) for New Zealand, moving through Bluff, the West Coast Sounds, Timaru and Lyttelton, with periods of reserve training.

### Search for the Himitangi, March 1912

- The steamer *Himitangi* was overdue from the Chatham Islands.
- The Prime Minister, Sir Joseph Ward, was asked to have Pioneer join the search.
- On Monday 4 March she took aboard **350 tons of Westport coal** and prepared for sea.
- On **5 March 1912, at 2 p.m.**, she left her moorings at **Gladstone Pier**, Lyttelton, for the Chathams.
- The press stressed her wireless and its stated radius of about 2,000 miles.
- At sea she received word by wireless that the Himitangi had been found stranded, and Commander Stopford turned back.
- She returned to Lyttelton on **8 March**.
- The commander's initials appear as both "J. P." and "P. J." Stopford in the transcriptions: to check on the images.

### Search for the dredger Manchester

Late April 1912: Admiralty reports said Pioneer and Encounter had sailed from Auckland to search for the overdue dredger *Manchester*. A report of 4 May still had no news of their movements.

### Other texture

- February 1912: a drunken melee at Timaru involving Pioneer's men; one man identified and charged. Context, not an Albert event.
- March 1912: a cricket match between a Pioneer team and the Canterbury Druids at Sydenham Park.

### Narrative value

The Himitangi search is a strong episode: coal, time of sailing, pier, route, wireless, recall at sea and return date are all in the press.

## 11. Drake, 1912 to 1913

**Albert aboard:** 24 May 1912 to 25 February 1913.
**Source folder:** `sources/images/Media-coverage/drake/`; transcription `transcribed/drake_transcriptions.txt` (**not yet image-checked**). All 22 source files are accounted for. The Lyttelton Times page of 12 July 1912 (page 8) is a full image, read in full on 14 October 2026: it does not name Drake.

### Australasian movements

- July 1912: with the Admiral aboard, she sailed from Sydney for a six weeks' cruise among the islands.
- Suva to Auckland, arriving 24 July; the crew preparing for big-gun firing at Jervis Bay.
- Later: Auckland, Wellington, Picton, and planned movements via Milford Sound.
- By late 1912 she was described as the last Imperial flagship on the old Australian Station.

### Homeward voyage, January to February 1913

- She left Sydney on Wednesday 1 January 1913. The Naval & Military Record (19 February 1913) describes thousands at Farm Cove, the band playing, and the seamen returning cheers from Psyche and Pyramus, to "Home, sweet home", "Auld lang syne" and "The girl I left behind me".
- She carried a draft of about 140 men for the new Australian ships.
- Colombo on 30 January 1913. The *Ceylon Observer* reports very great heat between Fremantle and Colombo, "several stokers fainted", and a crossing-the-line ceremony for the Australian recruits.
- Plymouth on 21 February 1913, then Portsmouth to pay off.
- His sheet has him in her until 25 February, so he is borne aboard throughout the voyage home.

### Narrative value

Island cruising, gunnery preparation, the last Imperial flagship leaving Sydney, heat below decks, Colombo and the return to Britain.

## 12. Alexander, 1918 to 1919

**Albert's entry:** 27 February 1918 (rated P.O. (ns) 1 September 1918) to 16 January 1919; Pembroke I from 17 January 1919. The sheet reads "P.O. (ns)", not "Acting Petty Officer".

Three secondary sources, read 14 October 2026, largely one witness (Wikipedia cites historicalrfa.org for most of its sentences):

- **historicalrfa.org**, "Requisitioned Auxiliary - Alexander" (no sources cited): laid down June 1916 by Armstrong Whitworth, Walker-on-Tyne, yard no. 905; launched 23 December 1916 as ST ALEXANDRE NEVSKY; seized before completion, June 1917; commissioned as HMS Alexander, September 1917; **10 July 1918**: "came face to face with the heavily-armed SVIATOGOR on the North Dvina River at Archangel, but conflict was avoided"; returned to the Russian Government 1921; renamed Lenin 1923.
- **Lloyd's Register Foundation heritage blog**: built under Lloyd's Register's special survey at High Walker, launched 23 December 1916, building supervised by Yevgeny Zamyatin. It names primary documents in the LR archive: the First Entry report (surveyor T. S. Shute; master M. Nicolaieff), the machinery report (T. Field), and the shipyard's plans for no. 905 dated 3 May 1916. It says she was handed over to Russia in 1919.
- **Wikipedia**, "Lenin (1916 icebreaker)": completed June 1917; requisitioned and commissioned September 1917; "handed over to White Russian forces when the British withdrew in October 1919" (attributed to historicalrfa, which says 1921).

**What this settles:** Alexander and Sviatogor were two different icebreakers. Alexander was the *St Alexandre Nevsky*, a Russian order seized at the builders before completion, not a ship "captured after the revolution". Sviatogor (later Krasin) was the other.

**For prose:** 10 July 1918 falls inside his entry with no break. It needs a contemporary or official source first (the North Russia despatches, ADM 53, the press). The handover date, 1919 or 1921, is after he left her.

## 16. Research implications for Chapters Twenty to Twenty-Two

The Navy material need not read as a sequence of ship names. In outline:

**boy sailor -> a battleship collision -> commissioning-day service in Jupiter and experimental gunnery -> commissioning-day service in a new cruiser -> the Australian Station and New Zealand -> the voyage home -> wartime patrols in a torpedo boat -> Courageous at Heligoland Bight -> Alexander in North Russia -> invalided home in 1919.**

That is an interpretive outline, not prose. Each scene is still built only from the service sheet plus dated ship evidence, with nothing of his station, actions or thoughts unless separately evidenced.
