TRANSCRIPTION

WILLIAM GOULDSTONE, who was tried yesterday at the Central Criminal Court for the wilful murder of his five
children, has been found guilty of the crime with which he was charged. The facts of the case were in no dispute.
They are set out very clearly in the opening address of the counsel for the prosecution, MR. POLAND, and they
were proved beyond all doubt by the evidence called in its support. It was on Wednesday, August 8, that the
terrible deeds were done. On that day WILLIAM GOULDSTONE came home in the evening at an earlier hour than usual,
found his two eldest children playing in the kitchen, took his third child into the room with them, and then and
there deliberately killed them all three by drowning them in the cistern. This done, he went up into the bed room
with his wife, who had been just confined of twins, locked the door, and struck the two infants on the head with a
hammer, fracturing their skulls, and inflicting injuries which proved fatal in the course of the following night.
"I have killed the children," was his subsequent account of the matter, "and I am happy now." "Your other three
children," he added, speaking to his wife, "are in the cistern." Nothing could exceed the cool deliberation with
which the whole thing was planned and done and commented upon afterwards by the prisoner. "All the children," he
said to his wife, "are dead now. I shall be hung, and you will be single." When the police came to apprehend him,
he met them with the remark, "Good evening. I have done it; now I am happy and ready for the rope." On his way to
the police-station he entered more fully into the details of the murders, explained how he had done them, and his
reasons for what he had done. He had had five children, he said, within three years and a half, and he thought it
was time to put a stop to it. His one regret seems to have been that he had not made sure of the two infants. He
would have been better satisfied if he had killed them out of the way at once. His manner throughout was that of
a man in his sober senses, perfectly aware of the nature of his acts, and of their certain consequences to
himself. He said, indeed, that he had had "an extra drop of drink to accomplish the job," but he was not drunk,
and could only have taken the liquor as a part of his plan, to give him nerve and make him ready for the
atrocities he was contemplating. His method of procedure, too, had been carefully planned out. He had thought of
using a revolver, but on second thoughts had rejected it, since it would have made too much noise. From first to
last the murders were the work of a man who knew perfectly well what he was doing, who had formed a deliberate
resolve of putting his children to death in order to get rid of them, and who carried his purpose into effect
with a clear sense of what he was about at the time, and with a clear foresight of the after results to himself.
Such was the story which MR. POLAND unfolded yesterday in his opening address, and which was borne out in every
point by his witnesses.

For the defence there was only one plea possible—that of the insanity of the prisoner. This plea was, of course,
put forward, as it generally is when a crime of more than usual atrocity is on trial, and when the evidence for
it is clear and conclusive. It was urged on WILLIAM GOULDSTONE'S behalf that he had been ordinarily a good
husband, a kind father, and a well-conducted man, and that his deeds of murder could be explained only on the
supposition that his mind had become unhinged and that he had ceased to be accountable for his acts. It was shown
that there was insanity in his family, that he himself had been in a depressed state of mind and had declared
repeatedly that he wished himself dead. Medical evidence was called and an opinion given that the prisoner when he
killed his children did not know what he was doing, and did not know that the penalty for the act was death. All
this evidence the jury have heard and have weighed and have set aside, and have not suffered their verdict to be
in any way influenced by it. We do not see that with the facts of the case before them and with WILLIAM
GOULDSTONE'S own words in disproof of the medical testimony, they could have come to any other conclusion.
WILLIAM GOULDSTONE had become reckless about his own life. He was "ready for the rope," and setting no value upon
his own existence, he cared the less about depriving his children of theirs. There was no sign of any delusion to
which he was subject then or at any other time. His conduct was not that of an insane man; there was no sudden
accession of violence; no notion present to his mind that had any tendency to rob him of his powers of judgment.
Cool and collected throughout, he formed his murderous scheme with care and forethought, carried it deliberately
into effect, and did not shrink at the prospect of the fate which he was aware it would entail upon him. If he
had escaped on the plea of madness, the verdict of the jury would in effect have been that atrocious criminality
is by itself proof of madness, and that everybody must be pronounced mad who does what his right-minded fellow
creatures would turn away from with horror. On this theory no criminal could be held deserving of punishment. The
greater his guilt the more clear and valid would be his excuse. The more deliberate his deed, the more evident
must it be held that something must have been radically wrong with him, and that the wretched creature was a more
proper object of pity than of any harsher feeling. In WILLIAM GOULDSTONE'S case, all this could have been pleaded
with exceptional force, but the jury have taken a correct view of their duty and have not suffered themselves to
be misled by it. Society, they have declared, must be protected against him and his like. We admit that he has
acted after his kind; that he has done what his nature has led him to do, but he has given proof thereby that he
is the type of man against whom our criminal code has been enacted and for whom the extreme penalty of the law is
the appointed end. We will not refuse him our pity; no one could be more deserving of it; but we couple our pity
with abhorrence, and we accept unhesitatingly the decision at which the jury have arrived, and the sentence by
which it has been followed.
