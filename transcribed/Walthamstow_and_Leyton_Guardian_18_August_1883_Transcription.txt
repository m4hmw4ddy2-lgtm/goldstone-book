THE SHOCKING TRAGEDY AT WALTHAMSTOW.

More than a week has elapsed since the public mind was disturbed by the intelligence of this distressing
occurrence, and yet the horror excited amongst all classes of the community seems to have been increased
and not at all abated as the details have become more accurately known. It, indeed, is now but too apparent
from the evidence given at the inquest that the horrible deed was deliberately planned, and was not the
result of brutish frenzy. The circumstances are of a shockingly cruel character. Not only did William
Gouldstone murder his three eldest children by drowning them in a cistern, and his two infants by striking
them on the head with a hammer; but it has been placed beyond doubt by the post-mortem examination that
there is an indentation round the neck of the eldest boy, which shows that the murderer must have attempted
to strangle him with a cord before drowning him in the cistern. The enormity of the crime almost makes us
shrink from the duty which devolves upon us to record details so heartrending, and which cannot exercise
any refining influence on the public mind. But there are several chapters of the tragic story which have to
be related before the narrative is complete, and the public are anxious for the fullest information. Since
our last issue the prisoner has been again brought before the magistrates, and a verdict of "Wilful Murder"
has been returned against him by the Coroner's Jury. The following are the details:—

THE INQUEST.

On Friday afternoon the inquest was held at the Board-room of the Walthamstow Cemetery before C. C. Lewis,
Esq., coroner, and the following jury:— Messrs. William Smith (foreman), David Casey, Joseph Blythe, Walter
Rollick, John Patchett, Joseph Day, George Foxwell, George Banks, George Lloyd, Thomas Mumford, Stephen
Archer, Edwin Lee, William Haggett, and William Weston.

Inspector Craggs was present, on behalf of the police, to watch the proceedings.

The jury then went to the mortuary to view the bodies which were there exposed on a slab. It need hardly be
said that it was a very affecting spectacle to see the five children that had been murdered by the
unpitying hand of their parent. They appeared to have been fine healthy children.

On the return of the jury to the Boardroom,

The Coroner said—Before opening this, which I think we must look upon as one of the most painful of all
painful cases, I feel it incumbent upon me to address a few observations to you. You have, as you are fully
aware, been sworn to ascertain under what circumstances these poor, helpless children and infants have come
by their respective deaths. I have no doubt that the evidence will disclose to you those circumstances and
that they will be of a most appalling and heartless character, such as we may say—such at least as we may
trust—have seldom or almost never been submitted to a jury to inquire into. But revolting as those
circumstances may be to your feelings, I must ask you to dismiss from your minds anything and everything
you have either heard or read relative to this case before entering this room; and that you will be
governed entirely by such evidence as may be adduced before you; and, having given that evidence your most
unbiassed and careful consideration, then that you will let your verdict be the result of that
consideration. With regard to the procedure which I purpose to adopt with regard to this particular case, I
may simply mention that I proposed at the outset to take certain preliminary evidence. And then—for the
satisfaction, not only of myself, but of those members of the jury who may not have had an opportunity of
viewing the premises—I propose to adjourn the inquiry to give us all an opportunity of viewing the
premises. Possibly you may be aware that in all cases where a person is likely to be charged with having
caused the death of another, as in the case of the persons whose deaths you are about to inquire into, it
is necessary that a post-mortem examination should be made. For this reason—that the law requires you
should not only have positive evidence as to the cause of death, but also negative evidence that death was
not caused by any other means—in other words that the injuries, if any other injuries are proved, are the
sole cause of the death. Therefore it is necessary always that a post-mortem examination should be made. I
was not aware until I came here that post-mortem examinations had been made, but I have learned that those
examinations have been made. I may tell you that I should have ordered them to have been made, and
therefore what has been done is simply absolutely necessary. I had thought when I came here that I should
have had to adjourn the inquiry for that purpose also. But the post-mortem examinations have already been
made, and we are in a position to take the medical evidence as to the cause of death. But I should
certainly not like to conclude the inquiry without having an opportunity, and without giving the jury an
opportunity, to view the premises. For those of you at any rate who have not already seen them, I think it
very desirable that you should see them.

The first witness called was Mrs. Ada Hamilton, who was acting as nurse. She said: I lodge at the house, 8,
Courtenay-place, with my husband. William Gouldstone and his wife and family also lodged there, occupying a
back room, kitchen, and scullery, on the first floor. The Gouldstone family slept in the back room, and
"lived" in the kitchen. My husband and I occupied the front room on the same floor. I was engaged as nurse
to Mrs. Gouldstone, who was confined with twins on August 1. Gouldstone used to see his wife every night
after her confinement, but he did not look at the infants. He slept in the kitchen. Witness repeated the
evidence given before the magistrate, and added: Mr. Gouldstone was sober at the time of the murders. I did
not see Mr. Gouldstone that morning before he went to work. I saw him the night previous at about six
o'clock, at teatime. He had had a holiday Monday and Tuesday, and at noon on the latter day he seemed as if
he had been crying. He was very fond of his children, but when the twins were born he turned. He said that
it was a very large family, and he wished they were dead. Gouldstone had never lost a day since he had been
at work where he was. He was very steady, and his wife said she had never seen him but once the worse for
liquor. Since the twins were born he had not looked at them, and he would not have gone to see his wife had
witness not persuaded him to do so. Gouldstone earned 25s. a week.

A Juryman: Did he complain of anything in connection with that event?—He said it was a very large family,
and he wished they were dead. I thought he then only referred to the twins. He never looked at them.

Did the wife reproach him for not giving her enough money for expenses?—No. She said to him, "Never mind;
I'll do what I can to support them." He was earning 25s. a week, and never lost a day's work. He was a very
steady man. He was not violent or cross.

Emma Clarke, wife of James Clark, a carpenter, of 8, Courtenay-place, said: William Gouldstone, his wife
and family, have lodged with us eleven months. Gouldstone was generally a steady man. He was a calm, steady
man, and seemed to be very fond of his wife, and kind to his three children. His wife was confined on
August 1st. He was the worse for drink the next night, Aug. 2. That was the first time she had seen him
intoxicated. He seemed very strange when told of the birth of the twins, and he never spoke. On Wednesday
night, at about half-past five o'clock, I was in the back garden, when Mrs. Hamilton called me. I heard
Mrs. Gouldstone say, "He has killed my babies." Mr. Gouldstone opened the bedroom door, and said, "You can
come in; it's all done." I entered the room, and stayed with Mrs. Gouldstone and the nurse. I saw two
babies lying on a pillow. They were breathing, and blood was flowing from their noses and mouths. I
afterwards went into the kitchen, and saw the three children who had been drowned in the cistern.

By the Jury: I have heard the mother say she wished the children dead—that was when she was worried. I did
not think she was in earnest. She never said it in conversation. She had remarked it to the children when
they worried her.

The Coroner said he did not think anything would turn on that.

William Cheeseman, police-constable 86 N, said: On Wednesday afternoon last, at about twenty minutes to six
o'clock, I received information that a man was killing his children at 8, Courtenay-place. I immediately
went to the house, and on going upstairs on to the landing I saw William Gouldstone in the kitchen. He was
in a stooping position over three children, side by side, lying on the floor. The three children appeared
to be dead, and were thoroughly drenched with water. Mr. Gouldstone had his coat and hat off, and his
shirt-sleeves tucked up. As soon as he saw me he came towards the door and said, "Good evening, policeman;
I have done it now; I am happy; I am ready for the rope." I cautioned him that whatever he said would be
used as evidence against him. He then put on his coat and hat and said, "Let me see my wife." I took him
into the bedroom, and his wife said, "Oh, you wicked man; what have you done?" He said, "I have killed all
your children; now you'll be single again, and I shall be hanged." His wife said, "Have you any money?" He
said "Yes," and gave her all he had.

He then kissed her, and bade her "Good-bye." On the way to the police-station he said, "When I took my

thought it was getting too hot. I have had five children in about three years and a half, so I thought I
would put a stop to it." At the police-station he said, "I've done it like a man," and when the charge

RE-EXAMINATION OF THE PRISONER.

On Saturday, the prisoner, William Gouldstone, aged 26 years, was again brought up at the Stratford Petty
Sessions, and charged on remand with wilfully murdering his five children, viz., Charles, aged three and a
half years; Herbert, two and a half years; Frederick, sixteen months, by drowning them in a cistern; and
two infants seven days old, by striking them on the head with a hammer, at 8, Courtenay-place, St.
James's-street, Walthamstow, on the 8th inst. It is said that he was visited in the cell by two of his
brothers, and seemed perfectly unconcerned about his position.

When placed in the dock he immediately folded his arms and rested them on the front. Seeing the nurse
opposite to him he nodded and smiled. He listened attentively to the charge as read by Mr. Powell, the
Chairman. Mr. Atkinson appeared for the prisoner.

Mr. Wells, the clerk, read over the depositions of the witness Ada Hamilton, the nurse, during which the
prisoner furtively glanced around alternately at the Bench, the reporters, and the people in the court,
which was crowded. By and bye he leaned his full length as much as he could over the breadth of the dock,
and again looked around, moving his feet, as if marking time with the clerk's reading of the depositions.
When the constable produced the hammer it was found along with a tartan scarf in a copy of the Walthamstow
Guardian, and the prisoner looked around, pointed to the scarf, and then to his throat, as much as to say,
"Let me put it around my neck," he being without anything on his neck. The reading of the depositions was
then proceeded with, the prisoner meantime watching the clerk intently, though instantly his attention was
drawn to the body of the court.

When the hammer was produced, the nurse added to her evidence that it was not until the prisoner was taken
into custody that she saw it. The constable asked her for it, and she went into the bedroom, and found it
behind the door, near the dressing-table. She noticed it, but saw no marks on it. She gave it to the
constable.

Mr. Wells, the clerk, here had a short conversation with the Bench, and afterwards asked Inspector Craggs
if he wanted a remand. He said yes, and the prisoner was remanded for a week to give the Treasury time to
prosecute.

THE FUNERAL OF THE CHILDREN.

The funeral of the five children took place on Monday at noon at the Walthamstow Cemetery, and such was the
melancholy feeling of interest in it, that although the time appointed was originally fixed for three
o'clock, and afterwards altered at the request of the mourners, it is computed that over 1,000 persons were
present. Great sympathy was felt for Mrs. Gouldstone's bereaved and friendless condition, and many people
expected that she would be present at the funeral, she displayed so much anxiety to see her little ones
before they were removed after the murder. Mr. T. Maynard, who has the contract for the Union interments,
was the undertaker; but the arrangements were carried out with fitting solemnity by his manager, Mr. John
Lowick. The body of each child was placed in a separate coffin, made of smooth elm, stained; each coffin
having a plate on which their respective names and ages were inscribed. The five coffins each covered with
a neat velvet pall had previously been placed upon the bier in the mortuary church, which, being contrary
to the usual custom, made the crowd of spectators all the more eager for admittance. The church, however,
is very limited in its dimensions, and very properly the police arrangements under Sergeant Hawkins
prevented overcrowding, and the orderly demeanour of the spectators was in harmony with the melancholy
nature of the occasion. Shortly after twelve o'clock the procession of mourners emerged from the vicinity
of the Board-room, preceded by the officiating clergyman, the Rev. T. H. Grantham Robinson, M.A., vicar of
St. Saviour's, who has proved himself a friend to the bereaved mother in her deep affliction, and was the
first to raise a helping hand for the alleviation of her suffering and distress. There were ten mourners,
amongst them being Mrs. Gouldstone, the prisoner's aunt, accompanied by her daughter; the aunt carried a
beautiful bouquet of flowers. They were followed by Mrs. Graves and daughter, Mrs. Graves bearing a
beautiful wreath. She is the wife of the prisoner's foreman, and lives at Hackney. It was from her house
that the prisoner was married to Mrs. Gouldstone, and it was in her house that the three eldest children
were born. Next followed Mrs. Ada Hamilton (the nurse), Mrs. Clarke (landlady), Mrs. Millard (who but a few
weeks ago had two boys drowned), and others. When the burial service was commenced by the vicar, and the
solemn words about the resurrection and the life fell upon the ear, grief soon made itself visible in
tearful faces. Indeed, we have never witnessed a more solemn scene. When, however, the attendants left the
church each bearing a coffin upon his shoulder, and Mr. Amey with the twins, one under each arm, followed
by the clergyman and mourners, the sorrowful spectacle drew from the females agonised utterances on all
sides until reaching the grave, which was 10ft. deep, and in the third-class portion of the cemetery. The
burial service at the grave-side having been concluded, the mourners dropped their floral tributes upon the
little coffins and retired, but it was long before the last of the spectators took their departure, many of
whom dropped a flower into the grave before leaving. At about three o'clock troops of people were pouring
into the cemetery to replace those who had seen the funeral, and as soon as the grave was filled up it was
covered with flowers of all kinds, many a tearful eye being observed around the little mound of earth.
During the whole of the afternoon and until the cemetery gates closed, visitors constantly arrived to visit
the grave, the top of which by this time was covered with floral tributes, many of which had been carried
into the cemetery in small baskets by children, who seemed greatly pleased at the task they had in hand,
but withal affected.

THE ADJOURNED INQUEST

At three o'clock in the afternoon, the time appointed for the funeral, the adjourned inquest was held
before C. C. Lewis, Esq., Coroner, at the Boardroom of the cemetery.

Upon the resumption of the inquiry, Dr. Lyle, the medical gentleman in attendance upon Mrs. Gouldstone,
read a letter he had addressed to the coroner, in which he indignantly protested against the "scandalous
and indelicate conduct" of a juryman who had visited Mrs. Gouldstone, the mother of the murdered children,
at her house, where the juror commenced "roundly to abuse her," telling her "it was no use trying to get
her husband off the rope, for if he were not hung he would be locked up for life, and he would be as good
as dead to her;" and it was alleged that the juryman also said it was the opinion of the jury that she had
driven her husband to commit the crime through jealousy. Dr. Lyle complained that the progress of his
patient had been retarded by such behaviour.

Mr. Blythe (one of the jurymen) said that as he was the person referred to in the letter perhaps he might
be allowed to make an explanation. On Friday he was unable to accompany the coroner and the other jurymen
to inspect the premises upon the adjournment of the inquiry, but he went to the house on Sunday morning.
His visit was of a kindly nature, to see if he could be of any service to Mrs. Gouldstone.

The Coroner: Then these observations were not made by you.

Mr. Blythe: Well, they might have been. I do not know.

The Coroner: The object of the other jurymen and myself in going to the house was simply to see if the
children could have been drowned in the cistern. It was purely a view, and no observation should have been
made. I myself and the jurymen accompanying me conducted the view with the greatest possible amount of
quiet, so that Mrs. Gouldstone, lying ill in the front room, really did not know what was going on.

Mr. Blythe: I am in the hands of the other jurymen if I have been indiscreet.

The Coroner: If any such observations were made by you, they were indiscreet.

Another juryman (to Mr. Blythe): Did you connect the other members of the jury with your statement?

Mr. Blythe: I did not connect the jury with it.

takes upon himself to do. The Coroner's court cannot possibly be responsible for that sort of thing.

Dr. Lyle: You may have to make an inquiry as to the cause of her death.

Mr. Blythe: Oh, I don't think there was that motive.

Police-sergeant Smith was then called. He said:—On Wednesday evening last, about six o'clock, having heard
what had taken place at 8, Courtenay-place, I went to the house. I there saw the bodies of the five

Police-sergeant Bryant, the next witness called, gave formal evidence of the removal of the two dead
infants on Thursday morning from the house to the mortuary.

Inspector Folkard said: About six o'clock on Wednesday evening last I was on duty at Lea Bridge-road
Police-station when William Gouldstone was brought to the station-house in charge of Police-constable
Cheeseman. Cheeseman said, "This is a charge of murder. This man has murdered his five children." The
prisoner remarked, "That is right, sir. I did it, and now I am happy. I did it like a man, too." I took Mr.
Gould, a surgeon, to the house, 8, Courtenay-place. We found the three eldest children lying dead on the
floor of the kitchen, and the two infants were in a bed in a front room, having been removed into a Mrs.
Hamilton's room. I returned to the police-station, and took the charge against the prisoner of murdering
three of his children and attempting to murder two others. He said in answer to it, "Quite right, sir. What
condition is the missus in, as I suppose some of you gentlemen have been round." Mr. Gould, who was
present, replied, "Tolerable." The prisoner then asked, "Are the two young ones dead?" I replied, "No."
About half-past eight o'clock on the following morning I told the prisoner that he was also charged with
murdering the two infants, who were dead. His reply was "Yes, sir. The other two are dead then?" I answered
"Yes."

Mr. Henry Gould, divisional police surgeon, of Leyton, said:—On Wednesday evening last, about six o'clock,
I accompanied Inspector Folkard to 8, Courtenay-place, St. James's-street. I found two twin children, seven
days old, lying on a bed in the front room. They were both in a comatose condition, breathing in a
spasmodic and irregular way. I found that the right side of the head and face of one was much bruised and
blackened, and I could feel that the skull was fractured. The back of the head of the other one was much
contused and swollen, and I could feel also that the skull of that one was fractured. Blood and froth were
issuing from the mouth and nose of one. I observed no other injuries upon them, and the infants were not
dead. I then proceeded into the kitchen, where I found three children lying on their backs in a row in the
centre of the room. They were dead, and their clothes were sodden, being wet through. I saw no marks of
violence about the two younger of the three, but upon the eldest one there was an indentation mark round
the neck, apparently caused by a piece of cord such as that which has been produced. Shortly after eight
o'clock the same evening, while at the police-station, I received information that one of the twins was
dead. I went to the house, and saw the dead infant. At ten minutes past seven o'clock next morning I again
visited the house, and found that the other infant had died. Upon Thursday I made a post-mortem examination
of the bodies. With regard to the two infants, death, in my opinion, was caused by a violent blow with a
blunt instrument. The cause of the death of the other three children, whose bodies were well nourished, was
suffocation by drowning. In the case of the eldest boy I do not think the cord used had been tied of itself
sufficiently tight to cause suffocation.

The Coroner, in summing up to the jury, stated that the case, as he said at the commencement of the
inquiry, was a heartless and cruel one. As to the cause of death, the medical evidence was clear, showing
that the two infants died from an injury to the brain, caused by a violent blow with a blunt instrument,
and that the other three children had died from drowning. If the jury were satisfied that death arose from
the violence of William Gouldstone, the only verdict they could return was one of wilful murder against
him. With regard to the question of sanity or insanity, that was a subject that the jury had nothing to do
with, but which concerned another tribunal. If it could be made out that William Gouldstone was not in a
sound state of mind that would be a subject for inquiry before a higher Court.

The jury at once returned a verdict of wilful murder against William Gouldstone, and the Coroner issued his
warrant committing the accused.

This terminated the inquiry. It is stated that Mrs. Gouldstone is on the whole progressing satisfactorily
towards recovery.

The following particulars may serve to throw some light on the prisoner's antecedents:—

William Gouldstone, the murderer, was a man ordinarily by no means of the careworn, roving-eyed description
he presented in court. A uniform quietness of manner characterised him, and to all his surroundings he was
a quiet, simple-minded, retiring man. Though 26 years of age, and rather stoutly built, he preserved a
great deal of boyishness. He was not, according to description, a thoroughly illiterate man, who had no
conversational ability. The young couple had been married four years and a half, but had only lived at
Walthamstow for eleven months. They had the reputation of being as contented a couple as could be found,
and Mr. Clark, their landlord, stated that it was only a day or two before he remarked to his wife how very
fortunate they were in their present lodgers—a remark suggested by previous unsatisfactory experiences.
Gouldstone showed, in a reserved sort of way, a disposition to be friendly with Clark, and they had been
out for a Sunday morning walk on a few occasions. They would stroll along a good distance by the river, and
only on one occasion entered a public-house together. Gouldstone always practised this strict abstemious-
ness, though he did drink beer. He did not smoke at all. On the Saturday previous to the tragedy he went to
his beanfeast, but Mr. Clark said he saw him at 10 o'clock at night quite sober. Despite the young family,
the Gouldstones were never known to be in money difficulties, and they paid their rent with strict
regularity. Gouldstone never spoke to anyone of his affairs, and his fellow-workmen at the wharf in Upper
Thames-street knew little or nothing concerning him. Even in his friendly walks with Clark he never
mentioned anything about himself. The walks were pursued with long intervals of silence, for if his
companion started a conversation on any topic of the day Gouldstone would be out of his element, and was
more at home in some little question of boyish games. All that could be learnt of Gouldstone's story is very
simple. The son of a country farrier, somewhere in the district of Saffron Walden, he had been trained up
in a branch of his father's business. On coming to London he obtained work at the Kirkley wharf, 67, Upper
Thames-street, and for a time lived in Bethnal-green, afterwards moving to Walthamstow. Gouldstone is
believed to have one or two relatives in Walthamstow. His country origin was discovered through his
aptitude in killing rabbits. Mr. Clark keeps these animals, and Gouldstone had often served him by killing
one, and it was only last week he disposed of a mischievous doe. Gouldstone all the time he lived in
Walthamstow came home regularly, and amused himself at home. He was a great amateur musician, playing the
cornet, the flute, and other wind instruments. His conduct with his children was never boisterous or
playful, and the landlord said his manner always seemed reserved even to them, though he was generally
kind. Only one child, the eldest, could walk. They were rather weakly children, nothing sturdy. Gouldstone,
in speaking of them, would always say, "There's my Charley," or "There's my Herbert." The children were not
even noisy infants. From first to last there was nothing in the character of Gouldstone that would ever have
led to its being supposed for one instant he was capable of a crime almost unequalled for its cool and
determined ferocity. His wife is described as "a good housewife and ladylike woman." They were, in fact, a
very ordinary couple, little known to their neighbours, and studied their own affairs only. The few people
that did know Gouldstone liked the man. He was, however, so little known in his own neighbourhood that when
the news of the crime first spread, many a wrong man was mistaken for him. Of the regularity of Gouldstone's
habits, and his apparently well-balanced mind, it is related that on only one occasion did he miss his
train, the 6.4 a.m. from St. James's-street to Liverpool-street, and that was due to a clock going wrong.
His landlord says he was as good as a timepiece. He continued in his own quiet way until the night the twins
were born, but from that time became moody and discontented. When the nurse first announced to him the
intelligence of the double birth, he gave one look of anger, said nothing, but put on his coat and hat, and
went straight out. On his return he made no remark, nor did he subsequently until after the murders. He
would not look at the babies, and never told anyone about them, but, left to himself, brooded and brooded
until the fatal Wednesday. Despite the man's mania, it is to be feared there was only a too fiendish cunning
in the deed. He chose his time and went home early on purpose, Mr. Clark ordinarily being first.

To the Editor.

SIR,—It will doubtless interest your readers to hear something about the poor woman who is the chief
sufferer in the dreadful tragedy which has occurred in this parish. I saw her a few hours after the
murders, and found her wonderfully composed but very weak, and the house, or rather two rooms which the
family occupied, devoid of all comfort, even the plainest food. I supplied her immediate wants, and, with
local help, hope to provide her with everything needful for the present. I trust that, notwithstanding her
terrible bereavement, she may recover, although with her life can scarcely be said to be worth living,
having no near relations or friends, and now in a moment deprived of husband and children. I shall never
forget the five little brothers as I saw them lying side by side in our parish mortuary.

                                   T. H. GRANTHAM ROBINSON,
                                   Vicar of St. Saviour's, Walthamstow.
August 10.

To the Editor.

SIR,—As the public are naturally anxious for the fullest information in relation to the fearful tragedy
which has just taken place at Walthamstow, and entertain the deepest sympathy with the unfortunate and
childless mother, they will be pleased to learn that the accouchement fees, for the payment of which the
poor woman showed herself so anxious at such a supreme moment, were at once kindly returned by Mr. Sutton,
her medical attendant. The fact that the twins, of which Mrs. Gouldstone had been safely delivered, were
uncommonly fine children, makes the circumstances of this sad event still more pitiful. It is to be hoped
that something will be done to aid Mrs. Gouldstone, who at such a fearful time is in the greatest
distress.—I am, Sir, very faithfully yours,

                                   EDWARD SMITH.
6, Colchester-road, Walthamstow, August 11th.

TRANSCRIBER'S NOTES

1. The Edward Smith letter is the same letter, over the same signature and address, that appears in the
   Daily Chronicle of 13 August and is already transcribed in this folder. Chapter Eight takes its title
   from it. The Guardian's printing is dated in full ("August 11th") where the Chronicle has "Aug. 11".
2. "Kirkley wharf, 67, Upper Thames-street" is almost certainly a compositor's mangling of Falkirk. Graves
   gives his own address as 67 Upper Thames-street in his letter of 11 October 1883, and the project's
   notes have the Falkirk Ironworks there. Not corrected in the transcription above, which prints what the
   page prints.
3. "Emma Clarke, wife of James Clark" repeats the press error over the landlord's forename. He was Charles
   Clarke. The paper's own later paragraph calls him "Mr. Clark, their landlord" throughout.
4. The verbal differences between this report and the syndicated reports of the same two sittings are
   substantive in places, not cosmetic. See notes/confirmed_facts_timeline_contradictions.md, Contradictions
   49 to 52, logged the same day this transcription was made.
