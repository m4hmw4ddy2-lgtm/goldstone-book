THE HIMITANGI.

There is no further news of the overdue steamer Himitangi, from the Chatham Islands. Mr J. M. Samson, manager for the owners of the vessel, approached the Prime Minister (Sir Joseph Ward) with a view to H.M.S. Pioneer, at present in Lyttelton, going in search of the missing vessel. The Pioneer has a wireless installation, and by this means could keep in touch with Wellington while searching for the Himitangi, providing the Admiralty authorities consent. Sir Joseph Ward was most sympathetic in the matter, and assured Mr Samson he would represent his views in the proper quarter, in the hope that the Pioneer, which is a fast and—for the purpose—specially equipped vessel, would be able to join in the search.

The Gertie, which is at present searching for the missing vessel, should arrive at Wellington on Wednesday next.

THE MISSING DREDGE.

HOPE ENTERTAINED AT LYTTELTON.

Although H.M.S. Challenger has returned to Sydney after a fruitless search for the dredge Manchester, now twenty-nine days out from Wellington, there are a number of nautical men who still entertain hopes for the safety of the vessel. In support of their contentions, they state that the weather prevailing in the Tasman Sea for the past three weeks has been very boisterous, the dredge has probably been allowed to drift with the sea. If such was the case then the vessel would soon get away from the beaten track, which would account for her not being seen by the Challenger.

Up to the present, no communication has been received of the movements of the Pioneer or Encounter, but it is thought likely that these warships would confine their attention to the north as the direction in which the dredge would probably make in view of weather, provided, of course, she was able to withstand the heavy seas. Although she left this coast deeply laden, many of those conversant with the vessel looked upon her as a thoroughly seaworthy and stable craft. These views were recently endorsed by Captain Stewart, who brought out the new dredge Canterbury out from Home for the Lyttelton Harbour Board, and who at one time was appointed to take the Manchester to Sydney.

As matters are at present there is a good deal of anxiety amongst relatives and friends of the crew, many of whom hail from Lyttelton and Christchurch.

Mr R. Hay Arlow, assistant-secretary to the Lyttelton Harbour Board, sent the following cable message to the Harbour Trust in Sydney:—“The chairman of the Lyttelton Harbour Board much regrets the non-arrival of the dredge Manchester, and trusts that no disaster has occurred, and that the warships’ search will prove successful.” Yesterday, Mr Morris, secretary to the Trust, sent the following reply:—“The Commissioners appreciate the chairman’s sympathetic message.”

THE OVERDUE HIMITANGI.

[PER PRESS ASSOCIATION.]
WELLINGTON, March 4.

Anxiety regarding the Himitangi, which should have returned from the Chathams in the middle of last month, increases, but it is still felt by experienced shipmasters that if she has gone ashore her crew are quite safe. The Gertie, which has left to search for the Himitangi, was due at Waitangi last Saturday, and the Amokura should reach there to-night or early to-morrow. If the Himitangi’s crew are safe they should reach Wellington by the Gertie to-morrow or Wednesday.

The Prime Minister, in accordance with a request made to him yesterday by the Chatham Island Fishing Company, has communicated with the naval authorities suggesting that H.M.S. Pioneer, now in Lyttelton, should also be sent to search for the overdue vessel, but so far he has not heard whether she can be spared for that purpose.

THE HIMITANGI.

H.M.S. PIONEER TO SEARCH.

Although thirty-four days have passed, no tidings have been received of the Chatham Island Fishing Company’s steamer Himitangi, which left Lyttelton on January 31 for the Chathams, and in many quarters there is much anxiety as to the safety of the vessel. Opinions of experienced shipmasters differ, however, and nothing conclusive can be expected until a report has been received from the Islands. Unfortunately the only communication between the Chathams and New Zealand is by steamer, but the Government intends at an early date to remove this reproach by the erection of a wireless plant, which will enable constant communication to be maintained between the group and New Zealand.

During the past few days representations were made to the Prime Minister, Sir Joseph Ward, with the object of having H.M.S. Pioneer, at present in Lyttelton, sent in search of the overdue steamer. On Monday 350 tons of coal were taken on board the warship in order to prepare her for sea. Yesterday morning the final arrangements were made and the cruiser left her moorings at Gladstone Pier, and at 2 p.m. sailed under easy steam for the Chathams. She should reach the Islands about eleven o’clock on Thursday morning.

The Pioneer is fitted with up-to-date wireless apparatus, and her exceptionally long radius (about 2000 miles) will enable her to keep in constant touch with passing vessels, and also with the Wellington post office. This will allow the news to be circulated broadcast in case the Himitangi is sighted.

Yesterday morning Mr W. E. Fuller, manager of Messrs Levin and Co., Wellington, arrived from the north, and had an interview with Commander P. J. Stopford regarding the missing vessel. Speaking to a reporter, Mr Fuller stated that Commander Stopford expressed his willingness to do all he could to clear up the mystery, but he was rather anxious, before proceeding to sea, to know what arrangements had been arrived at with the masters of the Gertie and the New Zealand Government training ship Amokura, which left last week for the Islands. Mr Fuller fully explained what had been agreed upon regarding their courses and other details, and, with this information before him, Commander Stopford was able to despatch the vessel forthwith.

Although it is anticipated that the Gertie will arrive at Wellington to-day, nothing definite can be stated in this direction. As much will depend upon the success or otherwise of the vessel’s mission. Should she meet the Himitangi disabled, it would probably take the Gertie about five or six days to tow her to New Zealand. The training ship Amokura is also on the look out, and should no trace of the overdue steamer be found at the islands, the two masters have mapped out a zig-zag course for the return trip.

The Pioneer will probably intercept both vessels, and should she be successful in sighting either of the steamers her wireless will at once be brought into service, and the news made known to Wellington.

The Pioneer is expected to leave on the return to Lyttelton about Sunday next, and as the Chatham Islands are only 490 miles from New Zealand, she should be back comfortably within a week. She will be in wireless communication with Wellington throughout the cruise.

THE HIMITANGI.

STRANDED AT THE CHATHAMS.

THE VESSEL UNINJURED.

CREW STANDING BY.

[PER PRESS ASSOCIATION.]
WELLINGTON, March 6.

Anxiety in Wellington regarding the overdue vessel Himitangi, was ended at three o’clock this afternoon, when the Marine Department received a message from Cape Palliser stating that the steamer Gertie had just reported that the Himitangi was stranded at the Chathams and that the crew were standing by her. At 6.45 p.m. the Gertie berthed at the wool wharf and landed four of the Himitangi’s passengers, Messrs Fortie, Eager, Cannon and Reynolds. This brief signal was supplemented and anxious waiters were told that the stranding had not been sensational nor attended with any fatalities.

The Gertie left Wellington on the search on Tuesday night, February 27, and called at Lyttelton Heads for the following evening for instructions. She was ordered to proceed to the south, and arrived at Waitangi on Saturday last, anchoring in the roadstead. The Himitangi could be seen lying stranded on the sand side of Petre Bay, at a point about nine and a half miles from the Waitangi landing place.

Captain Koger, who went down with the Gertie as mate, informed a Press representative just after the return of his vessel to-night that he had continued that the Himitangi was found ashore on the morning of Sunday, February 11. She had arrived at Waitangi at two o’clock that morning. After working all the other landing places she was scheduled to visit, anchoring on arrival in the Puwiseah. She was three hours later found to be on the beach, having dragged her anchor. Immediate help was given from those vessels in port. Captain Cown and Captain Coxen and his party, with a view of trying to get her off. These however, were unsuccessful.

The Himitangi was standing upright and broadside on a level sandy beach. It was considered there were prospects of getting her off if a lot of gear and suitable weather. The captain and crew are standing by her and are being well treated by the residents of the island.

After getting the mails aboard the Gertie left Waitangi for Wellington at 2 p.m. on Monday and passed the Amokura, which was also sent in search of the missing vessel, about five miles out from Waitangi. Cape Palliser was passed at noon yesterday, and the news was signalled by flag to the lighthouse for transmission to Wellington.

Captain Manley, who took charge of the Gertie for this trip, did not think the position of the Himitangi a serious one. She lay on soft shifting sand and had lain there since February 11 without sustaining any damage or going further ashore, so her position could not be considered dangerous. At half-tide one could walk right round her. All she wanted now was power. With a little heavier gear and a favouring tide and wind she could be got off easily. Her engines and propeller were all right, but the fish cargo was likely to be ruined. As the water-pipes were being blocked with sand, the temperature could not be kept down. The hatches were, of course, kept down, but the temperature had risen nevertheless to 38deg Fahrenheit. The Gertie could not render assistance in the direction of helping the stranded vessel off, but it was quite safe to leave her as she was. She had three anchors out, and was making no water. All that a relief vessel could do was to take down a little heavier gear. It might be necessary to do some digging out, but she might come off even without this.

“We are getting quite anxious about you,” said a bystander to Mr Elgar, who had been down to the Chathams on a fishing excursion, and was about to return in the Himitangi when the vessel went ashore. “You would not have been anxious if you had seen how we went ashore on Sunday morning,” returned Mr Elgar. According to Mr Elgar there was nothing in the nature of excitement about the occurrence. The boat just went quietly on to the sands. Mr Elgar himself was in his bunk at the time, having had a very busy day. They had been right round the Islands and completed their trip.

The local agents of the Himitangi to-night stated that no course of action in regard to the stranded vessel had yet been decided upon. It is understood that a vessel with the necessary salvage gear will have to be despatched, but the agents can give no information even on this point.

Early in the afternoon of the day when the vessel was stranded J. Merritt, the ship’s cook, died suddenly from natural causes. He was buried in the cemetery close to where the vessel lies on the beach. At the same time the body of Captain Hood, a very old resident of the island, who died a day or two before, was interred. There were altogether six deaths on the island between the time of the stranding of the Himitangi and the departure of the Gertie for Wellington.

H.M.S. Pioneer, which left Lyttelton on Tuesday to assist in the search, was stopped in mid-ocean to-night, and is now returning. She was in touch with the wireless station on the General Post Office, Wellington, all day to-day, and as soon as the Gertie’s report was received a message was despatched to Commander Stopford informing him that the Himitangi was found, and that there was no need to search further. Soon afterwards a message was received from the Commander stating that the Pioneer had turned back, and was making for Lyttelton, where she expected to arrive on Friday morning. The Amokura would discover that her search was ended on Monday evening last. She will not return to Wellington, as she was on her periodic visit to the outlying islands in search of castaways. If the wind is favourable she would go on direct to Antipodes Island, but if unfavourable she would return to Akaroa, thence proceeding to Stewart Island, the Snares, the Auckland Islands, Campbell Group, Antipodes and Bounties.

A magisterial inquiry into the stranding of the Himitangi will be held when the necessary witnesses are available, probably in Wellington. It is likely that a preliminary inquiry will be dispensed with.

At Timaru on Saturday Charles Dunn, a seaman of H.M.S. Pioneer, was fined £2, with the alternative of three days’ imprisonment, for drunkenness and obscene language in the streets, and was convicted on a charge of assaulting the police. This was the outcome of a melee in which two police officers, Sergeant Dart and Constable Fulton, were badly mauled, especially the former, by a number of drunken man-o’-warsmen, assisted by some town hoodlums, but the police were unable to identify the others. The Magistrate said that he made the penalty light, as the man would be dealt with further aboard the ship.
