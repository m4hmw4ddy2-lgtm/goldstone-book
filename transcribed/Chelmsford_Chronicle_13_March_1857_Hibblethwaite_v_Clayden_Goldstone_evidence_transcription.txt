NISI PRIUS COURT.
(BEFORE MR. BARON CHANNELL.)
FRIDAY.
Validity of a Will.
HIBBLETHWAITE v. CLAYDEN.
The evidence in this case was resumed.
[...]

Joseph Goldstone, a blacksmith, who excited much amusement by replying to the counsel as "My learned counsel," which
Mr. James returned by addressing him as "My learned blacksmith," said, I am 65, and knew Henry Hibblethwaite perfectly
well; he was a man of weak intellect, and knew nothing and no business whatever; did not play with him as a boy, as I
did not like to play with such a weak intellect, I knew myself better; he brought things to my shop to be repaired,
but he never could explain what they were to be done to; I had to do what I thought right in my judgment; sometimes
he stopped for the job, and the boys used to tease him, and call him "fictious names," and pull his coat, and I used
to check them. When he went for the militia his mother said she would not pay £10 for her poor silly boy, and he
should go; he did so and was discharged in a few days. After he was grown up I did not attempt to converse with him
like another man, as he had not intellect to answer.

Cross-examined. The boys called him "Five-clawed," because he said he had a chicken that had five claws on one
leg—that annoyed him beyond anything; I don't know a Dorking fowl has five claws; don't know a Dorking from a
Cochin-China, but do know a cock from a hen by the comb; am not an ornithologist as well as a learned blacksmith.
[Laughter.] The testator had strange feet, but can't say whether he had five claws on one leg as I never saw him out
of his shoes. [Laughter.] He had shoes on—they trusted him with them—they were not horse-shoes. [Laughter.]

Thomas Goldstone, son of the last witness. I knew the testator 20 years; the Christmas before he died he came to me
and asked me if I was not going to give him a Christmas-box; I said it was not a common thing for farmers to come to
tradesmen for Christmas-boxes; he laughed; I asked him, and he had a cup of elder-wine; the year before that he asked
for a Christmas-box, and I gave him sixpence.

[...]

[the verdict, column 3:]
The Jury, in less than five minutes, found a verdict for the defendant, and said they thought the testator was in a
sound state of mind when the will was executed.

NOTES (not for prose without a ruling):
- "fictious names" is as printed (for "fictitious").
- JOSEPH'S AGE: "I am 65" (certain on the image). His baptism, 17 March 1793 (dramatis_personae.md), makes him 63, or
  64 within days, in March 1857. The figure is his own as reported; not used for his birth.
- THOMAS: "son of the last witness": Joseph's son Thomas, William's father (born 1821), then the blacksmith at Great
  Sampford, five months after William's birth (10 October 1856). He calls himself, by implication, a tradesman.
- The hearing: "FRIDAY" of the Lent Assizes. The Essex Standard of 11 March says the Assize "commenced on Wednesday"
  (4 March 1857), so the Friday is very probably 6 March 1857: to confirm from the Essex Standard's report (Findmypast
  BL/0000165/18570306 and 18570311) before any date is used.
- The testator: Henry Hibblethwaite, farmer, of Old Sampford; the defendant, Clayden (his sister's husband: Mrs
  Abigail Clayden, by the Essex Standard; "Clayden Abigail (Mrs.), farmer, Thistley farm" in the 1878 directory).
- What it is for: a Sampford court case of 1857 in which two Goldstones, father and son, gave evidence that a man was
  of weak mind, and a jury found him sound. The book's use, if any, is Rik's decision (PART_ONE_search_list.md, item 19).
