TRANSCRIPTION

The following article from a legal contemporary will prove interesting:—

"The conviction of William Gouldstone, for the murder of his five children at Walthamstow, was inevitable,
when his case was tested by the law of insanity, as laid down by the judges and approved of by the House of
Lords in M'Naughten's case (10 Cl., and Fin. 200, 1 c and k 130). It was there decided, and it is still the
law, that if a man is in a condition of mind to know that the act he is committing is wrong, and
consequently punishable, he must be held responsible for the commission of that act. Thus the question of
knowledge of right and wrong in the abstract is not now put generally and indefinitely, as was formerly the
case after R. v. Bellingham (coll. 636), but it is put in reference to the particular act and the particular
time of committing it. It is to be hoped that the circumstances attending the Walthamstow murder will
convince the Legislature that the time has arrived to enlarge the hard and fast rule laid down by
M'Naughten's case. Gouldstone, it is true, was perfectly conscious of the enormity of the crime he was
committing; he knew and expressed himself willing to pay its penalty, and, according to the present state of
the law, he was clearly guilty of murder; but when it is remembered that there was in his case a total
absence of any of those motives which almost invariably dictate the crime of murder, and that insanity was
hereditary in his family, we cannot help seeing that, though the murderer perfectly understood the nature of
his act and its consequences, nevertheless his control over his own actions was suspended to such an extent
that he was no longer a free agent, but became the victim of an insane and irresistible impulse. It is hard
to comprehend on what principle a man should be hung for committing an act which it is impossible for him to
resist. It may be argued that no man would ever commit a murder unless urged by an insane impulse, and that
if the present ruling of the law be enlarged, the murderer will always be able to shelter himself under the
plea of insanity—the j'ai vu rouge of the French criminal. But it is seldom that no inducement besides an
insane impulse is discoverable in the mind of a murderer, and if murder is prompted by absolutely none of
the motives which would influence a sane person, if a man kills a human being whom he loves, merely because
voices in his diseased brain whisper to him, 'The devil wishes it,' 'He must have red blood,' or 'Your loved
ones must die,' surely the prevention of a lunatic asylum is better suited to his case than the cure of the
gallows."—The Law Times.

TRANSCRIBER'S NOTES

1. **This is a significant addition to `notes/part_three_context_law_and_medicine.md`.** That file has the
   medical profession's case against M'Naghten as applied, from the BMJ of 22 September. Here is the legal
   profession's own journal making the same case on the same man, a week later, and going further than the
   BMJ did: it concedes that William knew what he was doing and that the conviction was correct as the law
   stood, and argues from that concession that the law itself should be enlarged to admit irresistible
   impulse. The phrase to note is "his control over his own actions was suspended to such an extent that he
   was no longer a free agent" — the same idea the BMJ reached for as "the effects of an enfeebled or
   paralysed will".
2. Citations as printed: M'Naughten's case, 10 Cl. & Fin. 200, "1 c and k 130" (i.e. 1 Car. & K. 130); R. v.
   Bellingham, "coll. 636" (Collinson on Lunacy). These should be checked against a law report before the
   book quotes them as citations rather than as the Law Times's own text.
3. Dating: the Guardian of 29 September falls between the trial (14 September) and the original execution
   date of 1 October, and two days before it. The respite to 8 October pending the mental-state inquiry was
   reported on 26 September, so this article appears while the outcome was open.
4. The article is unsigned and is reprinted, so it is evidence of what the legal press said, not of
   anything that happened. Under the standing press rule it enters the prose as The Law Times, named.
