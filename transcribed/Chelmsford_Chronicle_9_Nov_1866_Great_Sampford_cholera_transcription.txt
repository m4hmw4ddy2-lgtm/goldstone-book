GREAT SAMPFORD.
MYSTERIOUS CASE.

On Saturday last about three in the afternoon a person named William Winterflood, who, it appears, has been for many
years a pugilist, and having no connexion with Great Sampford, arrived at the Bull Inn there, in company with another
man named James Cavell, from Walworth. The latter having heard that the Bull Inn was likely to want a tenant wished
to persuade Winterflood to hire it with him, but no one else knew that the present landlord was at all likely to
leave. Winterflood took his lodgings at the Bull, and Cavell went to a friend's house to sleep. In the course of the
night Winterflood must have been extremely ill, but strange to say he alarmed no one. As soon as he heard the
landlady moving he knocked for her; she went and found the room in a sad state, the man having all the symptoms of
choleraic diarrhœa.—Shortly afterwards his friend Cavell came, and going to him considered him in the most extreme
danger, and immediately fetched Mr. Barnes, surgeon, of Thaxted. Mr. Barnes found him in a state of collapse with
no pulse, attended to him, and saw him again in the afternoon, having administered medicine and strong stimulants,
but the poor man expired about six o'clock, perfectly conscious all the afternoon, having been ill only 16 hours.
Nothing could be done by the medical man but to certify that it was a decided case of Asiatic cholera. Of course
this created considerable alarm in the parish, and a meeting of the clergyman, guardians, overseers, and relieving
officer was [column 2] held at the Lion Inn, when the man Cavell was closely interrogated, but no satisfactory result
ensued. In the mean time the friends of the deceased were communicated with by telegraph, and on their arrival in the
evening of Monday they wished to have a post-mortem examination made. This was done on Tuesday morning by Mr. Harley,
partner of Mr. Welch, surgeon, of Saffron Walden, in company with Mr. Barnes, and after a careful proceeding and full
inquiry into all the circumstances it was determined that the original certificate given by Mr. Barnes could not be
altered, but that it was deemed a decided case of Asiatic cholera. What renders the matter still more distressing in
the village is, that a boy aged ten years, named Henry Andrews, farm laborer, who was at work with his father on
Monday at Mr. Portway's Houses, and was as late as ten in the evening a spectator in a travelling show standing in the
street, was taken with cramp in the stomach in the night, accompanied with other choleraic symptoms, and died at
eight o'clock on Tuesday morning, after being visited by Mr. Hyde, assistant to Mr. Fry, surgeon, of Thaxted, and not
having been ill more than about eight hours. It should, however, be added that the man Winterflood, 73 years old, had
evidently from the examinations made been a very ailing individual, and the poor boy Andrews was certainly of a very
delicate constitution. Added to this, our correspondent was informed at Sampford that the premises where the boy
Andrews lived and died were reported by Mr. Carslake, the relieving officer and inspector of nuisances, as having
very foul drainage, and the landlord was summoned before the Bardfield bench of magistrates about six weeks ago; the
summons was allowed to be withdrawn on his undertaking to remedy the nuisance complained of, and the alterations are
now in progress. A further distressing feeling was experienced in the village on Tuesday, inasmuch as a carpenter's
son, aged about 19 or 20, named Gray, was taken suddenly ill with such alarming and serious symptoms that in the
course of the day, although having good medical treatment, his life was despaired of. It turned out, however, in
this instance that the young man had been using oil paint a good deal, and that in all probability he was suffering
from what is called painter's colic; we are glad, therefore, to add that in the evening the reports were of a much
more favorable character, and the medical gentlemen present had good hopes of his recovery.
