TRANSCRIPTION

CENTRAL CRIMINAL COURT, Oct. 18.

(Before MR. JUSTICE DENMAN.)

James Cole, 37, labourer, was indicted for the wilful murder of Thomas Cole. Mr. Poland and Mr. Montagu Williams
conducted the prosecution; Mr. Geoghegan, at the request of Mr. Justice Denman, appeared for the defence.

In opening the case, Mr. POLAND said the matter was undoubtedly a very serious one, and would require careful
investigation at the hands of the jury. The prisoner was a brickmaker and labourer, and in August last he was living
with his wife at Croydon. Their two children, Richard Cole a lad of 14, and Thomas Cole three years and eight months
old, the latter of whom was the subject of the indictment, also lived with them. The prisoner had been out of work
for some time, and the family were in great poverty. The wife gained her living by mending chairs, being assisted by
the boy Richard. Upon the day in question Richard Cole went out at about 12 o'clock in the day, and did not return
until 7 in the evening. He found his father and mother quarrelling, the prisoner complaining that he had been kept
all day without food, although at that time there was food on the table. Some time afterwards the wife went to bed,
but the prisoner continued using bad language, and at 9 o'clock she got up and left the house. A little while after
that the prisoner told Richard to call his mother, and he left the house to do so. When he returned he saw the
prisoner holding the child Thomas by the feet. The wife took the child away from him, and laid it upon the bed, and
went out again. Shortly afterwards Richard saw the prisoner holding the child by the leg, and knocking the head
against the floor. The mother came in and caught the prisoner round the neck. The child was lying on the floor, and
she lifted it up. A man named Gilbert, who had heard the cries for help went to the house, and according to his
statement saw the prisoner take the child by the leg and swing its head against the wall. The child was very
seriously injured, and lay upon the floor bleeding and senseless. The witness Gilbert tried to detain the prisoner,
but he broke away. As the prisoner ran away he met a man named Knight, and said to him, "I have murdered my child."
The prisoner was detained by Knight until a policeman arrived, and was afterwards charged with causing the child's
death. A doctor was called, and found the child unconscious, with numerous marks of violence about its body, and
signs of dust, as though it had been dragged about the floor. The child died on the following morning, and the
post-mortem examination showed that, although the skull was not fractured, death was caused by the violent
concussion of knocking the head against the floor or wall. The defence which would be raised on the prisoner's
behalf was one of insanity, but he (Mr. Poland) would call the surgeon of the prison, where the prisoner had been
confined awaiting his trial, and several warders who had seen him daily, who would say that he had exhibited no signs
of unsoundness of mind, but, on the contrary, had conducted himself in every respect as a sane rational individual.

A number of witnesses were called in support of this statement, and it was elicited from the boy Richard that, upon
the night in question the prisoner complained that his wife had hidden people under the floor and in the cupboard to
try to poison him. The prisoner seemed also to have used threats towards his wife, having apparently entertained
feelings of jealousy towards her, but it was stated that she was a respectable hardworking woman.

The surgeon and chief warder of Clerkenwell House of Detention were examined for the purpose of showing that the
prisoner had displayed no symptoms of insanity, but had conducted himself in accordance with the prison regulations.
On one occasion he became violent, and was removed to a padded cell, but it was stated that it did not arise from
unsoundness of mind.

For the defence, a brother of the prisoner was examined, and stated that some members of the family had been subject
to fits.

Dr. Jackson, one of the Aldermen of Croydon, stated that he had examined the prisoner and had conversations with
him. He was quite certain that he was a typical lunatic with dangerous delusions. In cross-examination, witness said
the prisoner seemed to understand the questions put to him, and gave perfectly rational answers. He told him that he
had thought he was being poisoned, that his wife had set men on to him, that he used to shriek out, and wake up at
night, thinking that people were murdering him. The prisoner acknowledged that he drank occasionally, and that he had
been many times in prison for violence. The prisoner said that he found that a little drink made him lose his senses.
What the prisoner told him corroborated what he had heard from the neighbours. The prisoner knew perfectly well that
he was on his trial for murder. When witness asked him how he could have treated the child so cruelly, he made no
answer.

In reply to MR. JUSTICE DENMAN, the witness said he did not question the prisoner as to whether or not he committed
the murder.

In re-examination by Mr. GEOGHEGAN, witness said he believed that the prisoner was of such a state of mind that no
parish doctor ought to allow him at large, as he was dangerous.

Mr. GEOGHEGAN addressed the Court for the defence, arguing that there had been no motive for the commission of the
crime, but that there were strong antecedent probabilities that the prisoner was so unsound in his mind at the time,
that he did not know the nature and quality of the act he was committing. In support of his argument, the learned
counsel reminded the jury of the delusions which the prisoner was shown to have entertained as to an attempt being
made to poison him, and other matters which had no foundation. The very fiendish nature of the crime showed that it
must have been perpetrated while the prisoner was overwhelmed by a paroxysm of insanity, which rendered him
incapable of restraining, or of knowing, the nature and gravity of his actions. If, on the other hand, the evidence
did not show to the satisfaction of the jury that the prisoner was insane, it would be open to them to convict the
prisoner of the minor offence of manslaughter.

Mr. POLAND, replying on the part of the prosecution, commented upon the fact that previous to the commission of the
crime the prisoner was never shown to have committed a single act of insanity, or even eccentricity. No companion or
neighbour had been called to show that the prisoner had conducted himself otherwise than as a perfectly sane and
rational person. On the other hand, the gaol officials, who had seen the prisoner every day since, were of opinion
that there was not the slightest symptom of insanity about him, although it might be true that he was given to
occasional fits of rage. Moreover, the learned counsel argued, the statements of the prisoner as to his belief that
attempts had been made to poison him would not be sufficient for any medical man to certify that he was insane, and
thus necessitate his confinement in a lunatic asylum. The jury must take the whole of the circumstances into
consideration, and it was for them to say whether the prisoner was a violent, drunken man, or whether he was an
insane person and a fit subject for the Broadmoor Lunatic Asylum.

MR. JUSTICE DENMAN, in summing-up, said that it was an appalling case in all its facts and details, whatever view the
jury might take of it. One thing was perfectly clear—namely, that the prisoner caused the death by swinging the
child by the legs and knocking its head against the wall. With regard to the question of manslaughter, although the
jury were not prevented by law from returning such a verdict, could they, in point of common sense, assuming the
prisoner to be a man in whose case insanity could not be established, come to the conclusion that the act was not
done so as to constitute murder? If the prisoner intended to do grevious bodily harm to the child, and it died from
his violence, he would be guilty of murder. With reference to the defence of insanity which had been raised, his
Lordship said that when such defences were put forward, it was necessary that the insanity should be proved
affirmatively. The law as laid down by the House of Lords was that every man was supposed to be responsible for his
acts until the contrary was proved, and it must be shown that he was suffering under such a state of mental disease
as not to know the nature and quality of the act he was committing, or that it was wrong. The learned Judge referred
to the new Act which had been passed regarding the trial of persons who were alleged to be insane, and said that he
observed that last session a learned colleague expressed dissatisfaction with the new enactment, in which he was not
inclined to disagree, the new Act not altering the law as to insanity as it previously stood, but only making a
difference in the formal verdict.

The jury retired to consider their verdict, and after an absence of about an hour and a half, returned into court
finding the prisoner Guilty.

The prisoner, in answer to the usual question whether he had anything to say why sentence of death should not be
passed upon him, replied in the negative.

Silence was formally proclaimed, and

MR. JUSTICE DENMAN, having assumed the black cap, proceeded to pass sentence of death. He said,—James Cole, you have
been found guilty of the crime of wilful murder. An attempt has been made by the very learned and very able counsel
who has defended you, with the assistance of one witness, who had obviously formed a strong opinion in favour of the
theory which he had entertained—namely, that you were not responsible for your actions in what you did—to make out
that you were irresponsible. That attempt has failed, and I must express my opinion that, according to the law of
England, it has rightly failed. Although it was, I think, established in evidence that you had been suffering from
delusions, I cannot entertain a doubt that on the occasion on which you violently caused the death of your child,
you knew you were doing wrong, and knew that you acted contrary to the law of this country, and that you did it under
the influence of passion, which had got possession of your mind, from want of sufficient control, the result being
that the poor child came by a sudden and savage death. I do not wish to say a word to add to the misery you must
experience, but I will confine myself to the simple duty which devolves upon me, of passing upon you the sentence of
the law. The Judge then sentenced the prisoner to death in the usual manner, directing him to be handed over to the
Sheriff of Surrey to carry out the sentence.
