(FROM OUR MILITARY CORRESPONDENT IN IRELAND.)

Lord Grenfell left the Royal Hospital for London last night, and will return to-morrow. On the 9th April Lord Grenfell will proceed to the Cork District on a tour of inspection, the programme of which is as follows:—Monday, 9th April, inspection of the 4th Battalion Northumberland Fusiliers and 3rd Field Artillery Brigade at Limerick (two barracks); Tuesday, 10th April, inspection of the 4th Battalion Lancashire Fusiliers at Tipperary, in the morning, and of the 2nd Battalion Royal Dublin Fusiliers at Buttevant, in the afternoon; Wednesday, 11th April, inspection of the 137th Battery Royal Field Artillery and 1st and 2nd Battalion Durham Light Infantry, and 1st Battalion Gordon Highlanders at Cork, in the morning. In the afternoon Lord Grenfell will motor to Bandon, and inspect the 21st and 27th Companies Army Service Corps and their barracks; Thursday, 12th April, inspection of 131st and 133rd Batteries Royal Field Artillery, 12th Field Company Royal Engineers, 2nd Battalion Royal Irish Regiment, and 2nd Battalion Lancashire Fusiliers at Fermoy. The troops will in all cases parade in drill order, and recruits of less than four months’ service will parade independently for inspection. Lord Grenfell will return to Dublin on Thursday evening, the 12th inst.

A lecture will be given before the Military Society of Ireland, at the Theatre, Leinster House, Kildare street, Dublin, “by kind permission of the Royal Dublin Society,” on Friday next, at 3.30 p.m., on “Captain H. T. Cantan, Superintendent of Gymnasia, Curragh. Subject—‘Physical Training.’” Brigadier-General A. W. Thorneycroft, C.B., commanding the 16th Infantry Brigade, will preside. The upper portion of the gallery will be reserved for Warrant and Non-Commissioned Officers.

Brigadier-General Hon. A. H. Henniker-Major, C.B., in charge of administration, Irish Command, left the Royal Hospital for England last night.

Brigadier-General O. D. Fanshawe has returned from leave of absence, and resumed command of the Coast Defences, Cork, from his station.

Major H. All officers of the Belfast Garrison who are not on duty have been ordered to attend.

From April 1 alterations take effect in the conditions of service in the Royal Fleet Reserve, Class B. The modifications affect the following classes:—(1) All continuous service men and Marines who may enrol in Class B of the Reserve after that date; (2) special service seamen and stokers who enter the Royal Navy after that date. No man will be allowed to re-enrol for further service in the Reserve after completing 20 years’ combined service in the Fleet and Reserve. Men in Class B will receive a gratuity of £50 in full discharge of all claims on discharge from the Reserve on completing 20 years’ combined service in Fleet and Reserve; but no man will receive the gratuity on being released from liability for active service until he reaches the age of 40 years. Men may, however, be retained up to a maximum age of 45 years in order to complete 20 years’ qualifying service towards gratuity, but discharge will be compulsory at that age whether they have completed the necessary qualifying service for a gratuity or not. Subject to the discretion of the Admiralty men serving and eligible for pension under existing conditions will have the option either of receiving a pension at the age of 55 years or, as an alternative, of receiving on the foregoing conditions a gratuity of £50 in lieu of pension with release from all liability for active service. No man, however, will receive this gratuity, or be released from liability for active service until he reaches the age of 40.

The first of a series of experiments with a new type of coaling craft—a hopper—took place at Devonport one day last week. The vessel, which is officially described as a coal “bagging” lighter, is owned by the Thames Ironworks Company, and the tests are being made before it is accepted by the Admiralty. It is claimed that in smooth water warships can be coaled from one of these crafts at the rate of 254 tons per hour, which is a considerably higher rate than by other systems. The trials are being conducted under the personal superintendence of Mr. Cameron, the inventor, and are being watched by representatives of the Admiralty. The vessel experimented with on Thursday was the cruiser Europe, and with the machinery working at less than half speed the quantity of coal dealt with was over 100 tons per hour.

The Morning Post says that particulars of some important mining operations carried out by the vessels of the Channel Fleet at Berehaven last week now are to hand. Four tons of gun-cotton were exploded, and the experiments, it is understood, again have demonstrated conclusively the great value of the mine as a weapon of warfare. The operations extended over several days, the crews of the various ships being exercised first in the laying of E M mine-fields, and particularly in the location and recovery of mines, the position of which was unknown. After these operations had been carried out the mines were taken on board the Ocean and Triumph to be prepared for firing. An E M mine-field was laid by the Jupiter, and on the following morning eighteen 500lb counter-mines were laid. These were in two lines, nine mines in each line. When all was ready the counter-mines were fired, and the concussion discharged the mine-field that it was desired to clear. There was a tremendous explosion, and an enormous body of water shot high into the air. When the waters were again settled for a great distance round the scene of the explosion the surface was strewn with fish killed or stunned by the concussion. Chatham.

CHANGES IN THE STAFF.

MOVEMENTS OF SHIPS.

THE BLACK PRINCE.

CHATHAM, Tuesday.

Before the week has expired Vice-Admiral R. W. Craigie will have severed his connection with Chatham Dockyard. The parting will be regretted, alike by officers and men. A shrewd and business-like officer, Admiral Craigie from the first displayed a keen grasp of the work which he was called upon to perform, and during the time he has been here he has given abundant proof that he is an administrator of great ability. It may fairly be claimed that at no period in the history of the yard has the establishment been in such an efficient state as at present. Many improvements have been carried out on the initiative of the present Admiral-Superintendent, and in such a manner as to cause the least possible friction. To hold the reins of government of an important Dockyard is no light task, and much of the success of Admiral Craigie’s administration is undoubtedly due to the tact and fairness he has invariably displayed. The latter portion of his tenure of office has been marred by the extensive reductions which have taken place, and it is doubtful whether any official has felt this more than the Admiral himself. He has done everything possible to mitigate the hardships which are inseparably associated with discharges of such magnitude, but in view of the imperative orders from the Admiralty, it has been impossible to do a great deal in this direction. It is well known that many of the appeals which have been made, both to the Admiral and to the principal officers, have been of a most pitiful character, and it has been a matter of regret to them that they have been unable to accede to some of the requests.

Rear-Admiral A. C. Corry, the new Superintendent, will arrive here towards the end of the week. He will see the principal officers of the yard on Friday, and the commanding officers on the following day.

For a long time past the figurehead of the Nelson—a fine representation of the famous Admiral—Anson, and Britannia occupied a prominent position at the entrance to No. 1 ship, immediately opposite the main offices, and always attracted considerable attention from visitors. Owing to the changes which have been made to the ship to make it more adaptable for stores purposes (this ship and others have long since ceased to be utilised for building ships) it has been found necessary to remove the figureheads, and they have been placed by the side of the Admiral’s Walk, a narrow roadway leading from Admiralty House to the pier. It is a pity that more of these figureheads have not been retained in the Dockyard.

The Ocean, which brought round the crew of the Niobe for the Devonshire, did not arrive at Chatham until Saturday morning, having been detained at Sheerness. Her refit will be taken in hand shortly. The Devonshire will undergo her gunnery trials this week, and then she will be completed as soon as possible to take the place of the Donegal in the First Cruiser squadron. The Talbot, cruiser, has been selected to take relief crews out to Gibraltar. She will leave in the second week of next month.

It is expected that the cruiser Black Prince, which is being built by the Thames Ironworks, Shipbuilding, and Engineering Co., Blackwall, will arrive at Chatham about the middle of October to be docked prior to proceeding on her trials. The stipulated horse-power under the contract is 23,500, which it is calculated will give a speed of 22.33 knots.

Commissioned ships in port:—Pembroke, Acheron, Slaney, Trent; Albion, Illustrious, battleships; Dryad and Hearty.

Ships in commission with nucleus crews:—Royal Oak, battleship; Aboukir, first class cruiser; Ocean and Jupiter, battleships; Devonshire, first class cruiser; Amphitrite, first class cruiser; Pioneer, third class cruiser; Wear, Sturgeon, Spitfire, Welland, Hunter, Haughty, Sunfish, Charger, Salmon, Hasty, Cheerful, Hardy, Ranger, Dasher, Gala, Starfish, Ure, Rother, Snapper, torpedo-boat destroyers; torpedo-boats 65, 66, 67, 71, 76, and 77.

Special Reserve:—Camperdown and Rodney.

Dockyard Reserve:—Edinburgh, Tenedos, Skate, Pylades, and Seamew.

Other vessels:—Sheldrake, Champion, and Onyx.

Building:—Shannon, first class cruiser.

Completing:—Africa, first class battleship, laid down January 27th, 1904; launched May 20th, 1905.

He had it seemingly in his favour that we were only two or three hours from darkness, and that night might not unreasonably be expected to lend him aid. But such hopes proved faint, indeed. Meantime our battle squadron formed in line ahead, the ships bristling like porcupines with their guns, and every man at the post of duty. While we were setting ourselves in battle array a liner, bound homeward, passed peacefully through the very waters in which shot and shell was to be exchanged to determine the safety or otherwise of her route.

A HAUL OF THREE.

Soon after 8 p.m. as the first vessel of our line came into range, she opened fire, those astern of her following suit in due course. A gun from one of the enemy's ships showed that they too had commenced the action. Long before 9 o'clock the bloodless battle was over. We had captured both battleships and a cruiser—their names are not definitely ascertained as yet—and to this extent had reduced Blue's strength, and assured the progressive character of the Board of Trade returns. Later a vast floating town—for such our fleet, now augmented to 30 ships at least, appeared to be—moved to the south, but only with the intention of spreading, and then sweeping north again. We learned that the Mediterranean Fleet had gone to lat. 44, with the intention of sweeping south again. The co-operation between the two fleets is probably a good deal closer than circumstances indicate. But we have seen as little of the Mediterranean Fleet as we have of the mercantile marine. Admiral Lord Charles Beresford's battleships must, however, have been very near us to-night.

ATLANTIC FLEET SIGHTED.
THURSDAY Night, June 28.

It looked this morning as though we were to be again in luck. At about 8 o'clock—three hours after we had passed a couple of merchant steamers, clearly co-operating in the manœuvres, and as clearly separated from their consorts—we saw on the horizon the smoke of what eventually proved to be five battleships of the Atlantic Fleet. Two of them having presumably been disposed of by us yesterday, we were now in presence of what was practically the rest of the fighting, as distinguished from the harrying strength, of our opponents. One of them had a dummy funnel, probably rigged up with the aid of canvas, but this ingenious device—followed, by the way, by one of the battleships which we put out of action yesterday—deceived nobody. Our Admiral stood first of all to the southward, to round the enemy up to the northward. Of course, in face of our superior force of 13 battleships they were compelled to run, and they did so gracefully, and even obligingly. True they at first showed a preference for west as against north, for the reason, possibly, that they may have suspected us of a design to drive them into the arms of the Mediterranean Fleet. We then settled down to a stern chase, which even in these modern days remains a long chase. At noon our position was lat. 39 22 N., long. 11 10 W. An hour later the enemy were heading to the northward, with the apparent intention of retreating on one or other of their bases. With such a force as ours at their heels they could not hope to bring us to immediate action.

CLOSE TO RANGE.

At one time it seemed that they would never, metaphorically speaking, see harbour again, for we came so close to range that an action looked inevitable. One of the enemy's ships was apparently undergoing some repair, but she must have managed to get out of her difficulties, for instead of our gaining ground in the afternoon they rather increased their distance from us. For a little while we ran into rain and haze, which seemed of promise to the fleeing hosts, but the weather rapidly reverted to its normal brilliance. We recognise, of course, that we are chasing a very powerful fleet, and one of no mean ability in regard to speed. These battleships of the King Edward VII. class—we are in hot haste after Admiral May himself in his flagship of that name—can do 18 knots. Only some of the ships of our battle squadron can steam as well or better. It would be unfair to say that we are in the company of lame ducks, but, while some of our fleet can steam 18½ knots and keep it up, there are others which limp along at a pace equal to about 15½ knots. In other words, if all the units of our battle squadron had the steaming power of the swiftest amongst them we should probably have been in action before the afternoon was over.

WAR v. MANŒUVRES.

As it was, we could hardly let the swiftest slip from the leash, for the superior fighting power of the Atlantic battleships was, in American phraseology, a circumstance. In real war we might have essayed an attack, trusting to our less speedy ships to furnish reinforcements. But in this mimic war we should merely have been put out of action for a combination of impudence and stupidity. So we jogged along, not wholly careless and lighthearted, but with the consciousness that we were discharging police duty in quite an effective form. We were the parish constable following up the enterprising burglar from whose pockets a jemmy was clearly protruding. Our corns did not enable us to catch him, but we reflected that he was powerless for mischief while we had him under observation.

THE DIDO.

The day does not pass without its items of intelligence. Two Blue and two Red destroyers are reported as removed from the list after an engagement near the Scilly Isles, and the Barham, one of the cruisers of the Mediterranean Fleet, has been captured by the Berwick, and sent to Portland. But the news which perhaps more especialy [sic] concerns us is that the Atlantic Fleet, whom we are pursuing, claim that our own plucky cruiser Dido is forfeit. The Dido has been distinctly energetic; so much so, that 'ere she leaves us the Admiral sends her a message which must cause all on board to tingle with pride, since compliments from the flagship are not forthcoming every day. Meantime, what of the enemy's cruisers? In what devilry may they not be engaged?

A LONG CHASE.
FRIDAY Night, June 29.

Soon after midnight on Thursday the battle squadron of the enemy changed course to N.E., the cruisers kept us informed of their doings,

[Column 2]
and we altered helm accordingly. One of the enemy sheered off to the southward, and our two rear battleships were told off in pursuit. Whether they succeeded in their task we have no means of knowing. At this time we had long abandoned all effort to maintain that even progress which had distinguished our cruise for several days after leaving home waters. Five of our rear battleships, including Cæsar, had been told off to sweep to the southward from Finisterre, eight miles apart. Thereafter the Cæsar (flagship of Vice-Admiral the Hon. Sir A. Curzon-Howe) was to direct their movements. This left us half-a-dozen battleships in pursuit of the enemy's four, and, later, the Ocean signalled that she was compelled to reduce, and was soon out of sight. Considerable distances separate the rest of our now rather attenuated squadron.

THE INDIVIDUAL.

Sustained steaming at full speed not only throws a severe strain on the engine-room complement, but it is apt to expose individual weaknesses. Captain Lowry, of the Russell, who daily follows the commendable habit of giving the ship's company a brief outline of events after divisions, was unfortunately able to illustrate this. We were, truth to tell, one boiler short all yesterday. The water was allowed, through one man's neglect, to get too low in one of the boilers, with the result that a tube got burnt, the fires had to be drawn, and the boiler put out of action. Happily, however, repairs were rapidly effected, and to-day we have our full complement of boiler power. But the incident gave Captain Lowry the opportunity of enforcing the moral that in these days, when a ship is such a complicated box of machinery, there is a responsibility on the individual lest failure on his part should jeopardise the efforts of all the rest. Let me not be understood to suggest that Captain Lowry's addresses to his men are ordinarily of a reproachful type. Verily, they have no need to be, for no one could wish to be in a smarter ship, or one in which esprit de corps [italic] is more obvious at every turn.

A GROUP OF MERCHANTMEN.

To-night, at about 9 o'clock, we passed three merchant ships bound south, and keeping stations. As the group system contemplated the sailing in company of from 8 to 12 ships, one may surmise that the merchantmen have not responded to the Admiralty invitation with any great alacrity. In this case the second of the three vessels was proceeding a reasonable distance astern of the guide vessel. The third was apparently half a mile or more astern of the second boat. A long way astern of the third was a fourth merchantman; so far off, in fact, that it would have been impossible to suspect her of any connection with the group but that she flew the red ensign. We have been so long away from the track that it was quite agreeable to-night to have this first evidence that the group system has its votaries.

THE ENEMY'S MISCHIEF.

On the other hand, the conclusion seems irresistible that it will not have been all fun to the merchant steamers taking part in the manœuvres. We learned to-night that the Diana, one of the cruisers of the Mediterranean Fleet, had been captured and sent back to Gibraltar. Further, there was an intimation from the Bulwark (Admiral Lord Charles Beresford's flagship) that he was proceeding to St. Vincent, "as I hear trade is being damaged by enemy's ships or cruisers." Hence, while we flatter ourselves that we are driving mischief in front of us, the enemy is still displaying a great deal of vitality on the southern portion of the trade route.

A POSITION OF DIFFICULTY.
PORTLAND, Saturday Night, June 30.

This morning, at about 1 o'clock, a second convoy of merchant ships was passed. Three of the vessels were apparently keeping good stations. The fourth was a considerable distance astern. They were homeward bound, and it is satisfactory to see that they have got through so far. At 5 40 a.m. we sighted a cruiser on the starboard bow, flying the Blue ensign. She was passed at 6 30, about seven miles off, and was then near a merchant steamer. The occasion, no doubt, was one of some perplexity to Captain Lowry. Was he to come to the rescue of the merchantman, and for that purpose to deviate from his route; or was his first duty towards the battle squadron of which his vessel formed part, and which in his absence would be the less able to meet the enemy? The problem was an awkward one. In the result Captain Lowry left the merchantman to work out her own salvation. In time of war this conduct of his would possibly be denounced as base desertion in the hour of need. The merchant skipper would be certain to write to the Shipping Gazette [italic], and all the shipmasters' associations would be stirred into activity. Such desertion would, however, have been due solely to strategic reasons, and probably there could be no comparison, in the circumstances, between the necessity of maintaining connection between a squadron of battleships and the sacrifice of a merchant ship. Happily the decision was not fatal to the steamer. Captain Lowry sent a wireless message in cypher to the cruiser Grafton, known to be about 30 miles distant, giving her the exact position and course of the enemy's cruiser, and some three hours later the Grafton telegraphed that she had captured the Leda, the vessel which threatened the merchantman. All's well that ends well.

MORE CAPTURES.

At about 8 a.m. a four-funnelled cruiser was sighted hull down on the port beam, but to which fleet she belonged it was impossible to determine. An hour later the Red cruiser Doris was observed to capture Blue's torpedo gunboat Halcyon, and to chase and claim a destroyer of Blue side. Two merchant ships, homeward bound, were witnesses of the destroyer's attempted escape. One of them was the Hawthorn, of Cardiff. Neither seemed to have anything to do with the manœuvres, save as spectators.

"COMMAND OF THE CHANNEL."

By noon we were well in the chops of the English Channel, of which Admiral May, whom we have been chasing, now claims, it is understood, to have the command. Being on the trade route once again, we encounter merchantmen at frequent intervals. This afternoon we spoke the steamer Britannia, of London, outward bound. She reported that coming down Channel she had seen nothing of the enemy. At 3 30 we received directions from the Admiral, who is some miles ahead of us, to proceed to a rendezvous off Portland Bill, doubtless for the purpose of coaling. Probably our fleet will now conclude its long and arduous chase of Admiral May's squadron. As the afternoon wore on we received wireless intimation that the four

[Column 3]
battleships of the enemy were off St. Catherine's Point, Isle of Wight, heading east.

THE MORAL.

The whole story of our long chase of many hundreds of miles may be said to illustrate vividly the importance of battleships of high speed and great power. To commence with, we were 13 battleships of the Channel Fleet, in presence of five large and powerful battleships of the Atlantic Fleet, and only just out of range. After two days' stern and arduous chase we find ourselves with five battleships, considerably astern of the enemy, the few which might possibly have overhauled him being much too weak to attempt to engage him with any hope of success. It is true that we have driven him from the trade route, and that our presence in pursuit would prevent him stopping to do much serious damage. But he could sink or compel to steam with him every vessel he passed en route [italic]. He claims to have command of the Channel. In a sense this is true. It is a most striking proof of the supremacy of the battleship; a vivid instance of the sound policy that conceived the Dreadnought.

SUNDAY Night, July 1.

The hope that this rough log of the manœuvres might, so far as it is completed, be despatched to you from Portland on Saturday night, was destined to disappointment. While the flagship Exmouth and some other ships of the battle squadron put into Portland Harbour to coal, the fate of the Russell was to dodge about in the Channel while her consorts replenished their bunkers. Our own ship, although a large demand had been made on her, more particularly when steaming for practically 48 hours at full speed, was deemed to have sufficient coal for the rest of the manœuvres, and we were accordingly denied all opportunity of communicating with the shore. This was the more unlucky, since other correspondents were doubtless able to transmit their messages last night.

A FINAL SWEEP SOUTHWARD.

In the early hours of Sunday morning the coaled ships came out of Portland, and the battle squadron, now consisting of half-a-dozen vessels—for the Ocean had caught up—moved off, with its attendant cruisers, in the direction of Ushant. The order was that, if nothing interfered, the ships were to spread ten miles apart on the parallel of lat. 47 30 N., and proceed S.S.W. at 10 knots until the manœuvres closed. The Admiral considered that by this time the enemy's armoured cruisers must be much scattered, and that they were either being chased or would be chasing northwards. His object was to spread his forces so as to head them off. As regarded the risk to ourselves, it was surmised that the enemy could not now have more than two battleships in the field, so that there was little chance of our own battleships being met by a superior force. An additional instruction was that if an enemy succeeded in breaking through our line she should not be followed unless capture was clearly certain.

THE "DRAKE" OUT OF ACTION.

This afternoon we learned that Rear-Admiral Prince Louis of Battenberg, who commands the powerful Second Cruiser Squadron of the enemy, had coaled his ships, or some of them, at Lagos on Thursday night. Our Admiral consequently feared that the drive arranged on Friday morning off Cape St. Vincent had failed, and hoped for "better luck next time." Oddly enough, this wish was destined to speedy realisation. Later on this afternoon we learned that the Drake, the flagship of Prince Louis, had been put out of action off Lagos on Friday evening by the Red battleship Formidable, acting in concert with a strong force of Red cruisers. Intelligence also came that the Berwick and the Amethyst, two of the enemy's cruisers, had been put out of action in a previous engagement, in which we lost the cruisers Devonshire and Antrim, of our First Cruiser Squadron. The enemy's cruiser Black Prince was subsequently captured owing to temporary trouble with her boilers. The intimation that the fate of the rest of the squadron was unknown was, at least, suggestive. Apparently within five days of the outbreak of hostilities the enemy's powerful cruiser squadron had been rendered practically powerless for further mischief. Some of our own cruisers were, it should be mentioned, to-day running home almost destitute of coal.

ENEMY'S BATTLESHIPS SIGHTED.
MONDAY, Noon, July 2.

When the manœuvres terminated at 12 o'clock to-day we were, roughly speaking, half way across the Bay of Biscay. In the closing moments of the campaign we experienced a disappointment, for, just before the hatchet was compulsorily buried, two of the enemy's battleships came in sight. They were, in fact, running into our arms, and nothing but the time limit saved them. Then we turned homeward, but not with any intention of hurrying back to Portland, since certain firing practice had to be done en route [italic].

As to the general results of the manœuvres, our somewhat scrappy information does not enable us fully to deduce their teachings. One thing is, however, pretty clear, namely, that although at the outbreak of war an enemy's cruisers must in all probability do serious damage to our ocean commerce, it should not be long before we inflict summary punishment upon him, and rid the trade routes of his presence. Thus, although in the initial stages of a campaign our losses of merchant shipping may be considerable, the present manœuvres may possibly have a steadying effect as establishing that panic during those initial stages is unjustifiable. But as to that history must tell.

PORTLAND, Wednesday, July 4.
H.M.S. Russell arrived in Portland Harbour to-day.

A NAVAL ENGAGEMENT.
WEDNESDAY, Midnight, June 27.

Much has happened in the last few hours. Some minutes after noon the Spartiate, one of our armoured cruisers, reported the Scylla, one of the enemy's cruisers, as in sight in lat. 41 10 N., long. 11 55 W., course south. Half an hour later the Spartiate reported the capture of the Latona another of Blue's unarmoured cruisers, in lat. 41 15 N., long. 11 55 W. At 1 p.m. we observed three merchant steamers, but they were too far off to be communicated with. They were not sailing in company, and there was nothing to show that they were co-operating ships. Of course they may have been captured steamers, released, and on the way to their destination. Meantime our cruisers had been ordered to spread, so as to make a long line of front. Shortly before 5 p.m. the steamship Hesione, of Liverpool, homeward bound, passed through our lines. She dipped her ensign, an act of courtesy which was duly acknowledged, but we did not speak her. Half-an-hour later there also passed to the northward the steamship Surrey, of London, running home comparatively light. In her case, again, there was nothing to suggest that she was under our guardianship or ever had been. An incident of a painful character was the hauling out of her station of H.M.S. Jupiter, for the purpose of the burial of a member of her crew. A funeral service on the quarter-deck is always impressive, and it really lacked nothing in solemnity because, for reasons immediately to be explained, it was on this occasion undesirable for the battle squadron to slow down.

ENEMY IN SIGHT.

It was at 5 p.m. that the comparative monotony of our cruise was broken by the receipt of tidings of the highest import. A report came from the cruiser Leviathan indicating the presence of "two battleships and cruisers" of the enemy in lat. 40 50 N, long. 9 50 W. It was one more proof that Blue Fleet, by making a wide sweep to the westward, had managed to get round to the near neighbourhood of the trade route, and that the Commander-in-Chief of Red side, in deciding to hurry southward, had shown his customary judgment. Of the information at his disposal one can only guess. But he had done the right thing, as every man in the Channel Fleet was quite sure that he would do. Implicit confidence in Admiral Sir Arthur Wilson's skill is, happily, the first article in the creed of everybody serving in the ships under him. It was apparently the more imperative that we should turn the Leviathan's information to good account, for an intercepted wireless message, more or less imperfect in character, indicated considerable activity on the part of the enemy's cruisers. They were reported by the Mediterranean Fleet as "picking up two of our merchant ships." This was the first direct intimation that Blue side was engaged in active mischief in the very waters whose trade we had set out to protect.
