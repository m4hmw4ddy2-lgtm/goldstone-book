TRANSCRIPTION

THE WALTHAMSTOW TRAGEDY.

At the Stratford Petty Sessions on Saturday William Gouldstone, 26, blacksmith, 8, Courtenay-place, St. James's-street, Walthamstow, was brought up in custody by Inspector Craggs, N, and charged on remand with murdering his five children on the 8th inst. by drowning three of them in the cistern, and fracturing the skulls of two others (twins) with a hammer.

Mrs. Hamilton, the nurse, recalled, was cross-examined by Mr. Atkinson. She said—I have known the prisoner since the 1st of June, and had ample opportunities to notice his demeanour. He was very kind to his wife. The twins were born on August 1, and after that date I noticed a great difference. I have never seen him the worse for drink but once. After the 1st of August he appeared to be in a desponding state. He would come in and sit down and seldom speak to anyone. I have never heard him complain of being short of money, or about being unable to support his children. I have never heard him speak against his wife. I have never heard his wife say that she wished the children were dead. Since the birth of the twins I have noticed he appeared absent. I saw him in the bedroom after the occurrence, and I did not notice a hammer till afterwards, when the constable asked for it. When the constable took him into the bedroom he said, "I have murdered all your children; you wished them dead, and now you have got your wish."

William Cheeseman, police-constable 96 N, said, in reply to Mr. Batchelor.—I was on duty at St. James's-street, Walthamstow, on the day in question, and was called to 8, Courtenay-place. I went upstairs into the kitchen, and saw three children lying on the floor there, the prisoner being in a stooping position over them. He had his hat and coat off, and his shirt sleeves were tucked up. The clothes of the children appeared to be drenched with water. When the prisoner saw me he said, "Good evening, policeman; I have done it now. I am happy, and am ready for the rope." I took him into the bedroom to his wife, and when I got there his wife said, "Oh, you wicked man; what have you done?" The prisoner said, "I have killed all your children; you will be single again, and I shall be hanged." His wife asked him for money, and he gave it to her. The prisoner then kissed his wife, wished her "Good-bye," and I took him away. On the way to the station the prisoner said, "When I took my money last week I thought of buying a revolver to do it with; but I altered my mind, as I thought it would make too much noise." He also said, "I thought it was getting too hot, for I have had five kids in three years and a half, so I thought it time to put a stop to it."

Inspector George Folkard, N, stationed at Lea-bridge-road, said he examined the cistern wherein three of the children were drowned. It is about 6ft. from the floor, high up near the ceiling. The top of it to the ceiling was about 10in., the cistern being 14in. deep.

Constable Henry Wheatley, 308 N, stationed at Lea-bridge, said—About a quarter to seven on the evening of the 8th inst. I was in the cell passage of the station leading to the cell where the prisoner was. The prisoner said to me, "I have had an extra to-day to accomplish this job. There's five of them gone to glory, and it is a good job too." Later on, at half-past seven o'clock, he said, "I am sorry for my wife and parents; but the children will be better off in heaven, if there is such a place. It is better than leaving them to the mercy of the world." At about nine o'clock he said, "I wish I had killed the little ones out of the way. I don't know if I hit them once or twice. I have had this preying on my mind for a long time."

Mr. Henry Gould, divisional surgeon of police for the district, said—On the 8th inst., at six o'clock, I was called by Inspector Folkard, and went to him at 8, Courtenay-place. I went into the front room on the first floor, and there on a bed saw two children only a few days old. They were male children. I examined them, and found they had both received injuries to the head. I felt that the skull of each was fractured, and blood and froth was issuing from them. I thought it impossible that the children could live. I went into the kitchen, and there saw three children lying in a row, side by side, their feet being all one way. They were quite dead, and their clothes saturated with water. One of the children was naked, Dr. Twining having taken the clothes off. I saw no marks of violence on two of them, but on the eldest (Charles) I found on the neck an indentation, a mark of a stout cord. It had been put around the neck. At seven o'clock I again went to the house, and saw that one of the two infants had died. Soon after seven o'clock the next morning I went and found that the other one was dead. Since that time I have made post-mortem examinations of the bodies of the whole of the five children. As to the two younger, I found that their death had been caused by the violence used that fractured the skull of each. As to the three others, from the condition of the heart and lungs, I came to the conclusion that they died from suffocation, caused by drowning. All the bodies of the three children were well nourished.

This was the whole of the evidence, and the prisoner was committed for trial, Mr. Atkinson saying he would reserve his defence.

The extremely unconcerned manner in which the prisoner stood in the dock while the proceedings were progressing could not but be remarked. When first ushered in from the cells he scanned hurriedly around the Court, and then folding his arms gazed from time to time on to the floor, apparently thinking, until he was awakened from his reverie by the voice of Mr. Powell, as he commenced to read the charge over to him. When this had been finished the prisoner's attention was attracted by some person he recognised in the body of the Court, to whom he nodded and smiled. Mr. Batchelor, when making a brief speech to the Bench, was scrutinised carefully by the prisoner, who subsequently throughout the whole case kept glancing at the Bench, the clerk, the witnesses, the solicitors, and the reporters. At one moment he would stretch across the dock with his head on his folded arms, resting on the bar in front of him; in a minute or so he would stand firm and erect; and he would rest his back on the side-rail of the dock, so that he could but see the constables in charge of him, the magistrates, the clerk, and the witnesses. In short, it may be said that during the time he was in the dock, he could have been viewed in at least a dozen positions, his demeanour being all the time cool, and not in the least affected by any of the evidence given against him. Before Dr. Gould got into the witness-box the prisoner made some communication to Constable Honey, and that officer shortly afterwards brought him in a mug of water, but the prisoner simply sipped it, and without making any remark went into the centre of the dock, and rested his elbows on the rail. About ten minutes later the case was concluded.

A crowd of several hundreds waited at each of the three stations at Stratford to see the prisoner brought up to the Court, but the police adopted another plan of getting him there, bringing him direct from the House of Detention by road. While he was in his cell Miss Skinner, a lady doing a great and good work in the neighbourhood in connexion with Mrs. Pelly's Refuge Home, was accorded the privilege of visiting him. Gouldstone was delighted and surprised at the appearance of Miss Skinner in his cell, and expressed himself to that effect. Miss Skinner simply spoke to Gouldstone from a spiritual point, and he seemed perfectly aware then of the fearful position he was in, and joined in prayer. In the course of the conversation he said he knew every man's hand was against him, and that he had done that which deserved such a recognition, but he took heart in the fact that the Lord had been merciful to allow him the time to prepare for repentance. In the course of a short prayer given by Miss Skinner the words "Lord have mercy upon me" occurred, and the prisoner, heaving a deep sigh, said fervently, "Amen." The interview over, Miss Skinner knocked at the cell door for exit, and as soon as a police-constable appeared at the portal the prisoner's demeanour at once assumed that defiance which has been characteristic of him since the sad occurrence. Miss Skinner is now desirous of getting permission to again visit the prisoner, inasmuch as he seemed so grateful and touched by the visit of Saturday.

Great ingenuity was displayed by the police to get Gouldstone quietly away from the Court on Saturday. It was suggested that they should clamber over a back wall of the Court into the Broadway, Stratford, through a shop, but the idea was abandoned. A private brougham was in attendance in the Grove, Stratford, the instructions of the coachman being that when he saw a cab drive up to the prisoners' exit he was to draw up at the magistrates' entrance. With a great show of bustle and noise a constable was sent for the cab, and as soon as it went to the prisoners' entrance, the crowd gathered there, it being around the corner of the building and not near the magistrates' door. At the same instant several constables rushed out of the Court over a pathway to the Three Choppers coffee palace, through which prisoner passed last Saturday, and then what had remained of the crowd in the front went to the coffee shop. Then, the brougham having driven up to the magistrates' entrance, the prisoner was rushed into it. He seemed to enjoy the ruse to deceive the crowd. When, however, it was discovered they had been hoaxed, the cab was run after, yelled at, and hooted at, the police fearing that the brougham would be overturned at Angell-lane, where there was a block in the traffic. A large portion of the crowd ran to the Stratford main station, but the brougham did not go there, but proceeded at a good rate up to the House of Detention by road, gradually outpacing all its followers, who hooted and groaned at it for a long time. Ultimately the prisoner was safely lodged at Clerkenwell.
