SHOCKING TRAGEDY AT WALTHAMSTOW.

The even tenour of our way has been broken in upon with no ordinary shock by the occurrence of an appalling
tragedy on Wednesday evening. So rarely do murders occur at Walthamstow that whenever anything of the kind takes
place a most intense feeling of horror is aroused, and when it became generally known that a whole family had been
cruelly murdered by their father, the occurrence was so exceptional that it sent a thrill of horror through the
public mind, and has been the absorbing topic of conversation ever since. As yet the particulars of the tragedy are
only in part known, nor will the whole of the circumstances of the affair be minutely or accurately ascertained
until the inquest, which will probably take place to-day. The scene of the tragedy is No. 8, Courtenay-place, St.
James's-street, and is one of a row of newly-erected houses facing the Great Eastern Railway. The tenant of the
house is Mrs. Emma Clarke, who has it let in lodgings. For the past eleven months a portion of the house has been
occupied by a young man named William Gouldstone, with his wife and family. He is a whitesmith by trade, and
worked for Mr. Kinneard, in Upper Thames-street, having held the situation for the past five years, and looked
upon as a steady and good workman. It is said that he only received 25s. a week, and that latterly he was in
pecuniary straits. We cannot say whether such was really the case. It is, however, a fact that he lived on the most
affectionate terms with his wife and was reputed to be a young man of very quiet and steady habits. It is said also
that as a father he always shewed great affection for his children, and consequently no motive can be ascribed for
the unhappy affair, except despair as to his worldly circumstances. On the first of August his wife was confined of
two male children which increased the number of his family to five children; all males, and may be described as
follows:—Charles, aged three years and a half; Herbert, two years and a half; Frederick, sixteen months, and the
twins only seven days old. During the past week he was noticed to be somewhat desponding, but there was nothing in
his demeanour to lead anyone to suppose that his mind was seriously disturbed. His wife had a monthly nurse to
attend her, and was getting nicely over her confinement; he went regularly and punctually to his employment, and
in other respects, one day was quite a sequel to the other. On Wednesday evening he came home about a quarter-past
five o'clock, which was sooner than usual, and was heard to go into the kitchen where the two eldest children
(Charles and Herbert) were playing together, the other little boy (Frederick) being in the bedroom upstairs along
with his mother and the nurse. The latter was washing one of the babies. She replaced it in bed with its mother and
went downstairs to see if it was the father who had returned. She remarked to him, "Oh, how you did frighten me; I
wondered who it was." To which he made no reply. She then went back and finished washing the baby. The father then
came into the room and stood by his wife's bedside, but did not speak to her. The wife asked him how it was that he
was home so early? He replied that he left whilst the manager was at his dinner, and came home. She then asked him
if he had told at the shop about her being confined? He said "No," and then took little Frederick from the bed and
brought him down into the kitchen. Shortly afterwards, the nurse had occasion to go into the kitchen, and noticed
the father sitting on the fire-guard with a piece of cord in his hand, whilst the three children were playing upon
the floor in front of him. Little did she think when she saw him sitting there twisting the string round his
finger that instead of enjoying the innocent gambols of his children he was in reality maturing his diabolical plan
for their murder which was so soon to be accomplished. His good looks did not indicate the foul purposes of his
mind, and not having the faintest suspicion that he was brooding over anything evil, the nurse asked him if he had
had his tea, and when he replied that he had not, she told him it was ready for him, and went back to the bedroom
to wash the other baby. When this was done she left the babies in bed with the mother, one on either side, and
went again downstairs into the washhouse. As she passed through the kitchen she noticed that the three children
were still playing on the ground, and the prisoner still sitting upon the fire-guard. In the wash-house she saw
that a perambulator had been removed from beneath the cistern and that a chair had been placed there. She asked who
had placed the chair there, and little Charley said that it was his dad that had done it. She then returned to the
bedroom to make the bed. The unfortunate man must then have carried his execrable design into execution, and the
drowning of the three children must have been the work of a very short time, because when the nurse had finished
making the bed, and just as the mother had got into it, he came along the passage and looked into the bedroom. The
nurse told him he might come in, but after mumbling something he went back again into the kitchen. Evidently they
noticed something peculiar about his conduct, as the nurse asked the mother if she should leave the room, as he
might want to speak to her privately. The mother said, "Yes, do my dear!" As she was going downstairs she heard the
prisoner open the kitchen door and walk into the bedroom, and had just got to the yard door when she heard screams
of "Murder!" from his wife, and ran upstairs, and tried to open the door, but it was locked. Hearing the wife say
"Oh! he is murdering my children" the nurse, finding that she could not open the door put her head over the
banisters and called loudly for help. The landlady came to her assistance, and called upon the husband to open the
door, which he did. The wife was still crying out, and the nurse said "Oh! you wicked man, what have you done?" He
said "I've killed the children; I am a happy man now. You will find the other three children in the cistern." The
two infants were lying by the mother's side bleeding from the mouth, and one had a bruise over the eye. Having
worked out his will, the unfortunate man—whether fiend or madman it was unfortunate for him—proceeded with the
utmost deliberation downstairs into the kitchen. The nurse proposed to go for a doctor, but the sorrowing mother
implored her to go and see if he had killed her other three children. She went into the kitchen and saw him
bringing the youngest one from the washhouse and lay it on the floor; it was dead, and appeared to have been just
taken out of the cistern; but being much afraid, and hearing the footsteps of the mother upon the landing, she
hurried back to her to prevent her coming down. About ten minutes afterwards the nurse went again into the kitchen,
and saw the three children lying side by side dead upon the floor, and a gentleman standing over them. The father
made no attempt to escape. A policeman came and took him into custody, and before removing him they went into the
bedroom, when the prisoner said, "All the children are dead now; I shall be hanged, and you will be single," and
having kissed her, he remarked, "You wished they were dead, and now they are dead." Since his apprehension he has
made many statements all tending to show that the crime was premeditated; but it is difficult to realise that
anyone not bereft of reason could be so utterly devoid of manly instinct as thus to become the executioner of all
his family. When searched at the police-station a cutting from a newspaper was found in his possession, containing
an account of two recent murders. No signs of sorrow or shame has yet been detected in his demeanour; on the
contrary, he has manifested the utmost callousness and indifference, and talks of the execrable deed as if he had
performed an heroic action by depriving his children of life. He has stated that at first it was his intention to
shoot them with a revolver, but was afraid that any loud report might frustrate his design. The man must be mad to
talk in that manner, but if he be mad there is as much method in his madness as if he were a rational creature. It
is stated that when arrested he smelt of drink, and said that he took it to enable him to do what he did, so that
however abstemious he may have been heretofore, it is drink that has probably brought him to the brink of endless
misery. He had lived happily with his wife, and in her deep affliction her sorrow for him is most intense. Much
sympathy is felt for her; and we think that no one can read the details of the sad occurrence without feeling
sorry for him also, and it is charitable to hope that his terrible crime is the result of insanity and not of
inhumanity. Public curiosity has led many to visit the scene of the crime, and so largely does this feeling
prevail that all day long groups of people may be seen loitering in the vicinity of the house. All kinds of
rumours have been afloat as to the motive for committing the crime, and whatever may have been the incentive, most
of the rumours are simply idle conjecture. The prisoner's wife has been represented as in a dying state; such,
however, is not the case, as she has all along shown wonderful fortitude, and is getting on towards recovery as
well as could be expected.

On Thursday the prisoner William Gouldstone, who is 26 years of age, was brought up in custody by Inspector
Craggs, of the N Division, at Stratford Police-court, and charged with murdering his three children, Charles, aged
3½ years, Herbert, aged 2½ years, and Frederick, aged 16 months, by putting them into a water-cistern, at his
residence on the 8th inst. He was further charged with murdering his two infant children, aged seven days, by
striking them on the head with a hammer at the same time and place.

Mr. John W. Atkinson, solicitor, of Stratford, appeared on the prisoner's behalf.

The prisoner, when he was taken into custody by Police-constable William Cheeseman, 86 N, was conveyed to the Lea
Bridge-road Station, and was charged with murdering the first three children, and attempting to murder the
infants. When the latter died, however, the charge was altered to one of murder.

The magistrates on the Bench were Mr. Arthur Lister, the Chairman, and Mr. W. W. Glenny.—The prisoner is a man
standing not more than 5ft. 4in., fair, and with light hair. When his case was called

Ada Hamilton, wife of William Hamilton, of 8, Courtenay-place, St. James's-street, Walthamstow, was the first
witness called. She said: I am a monthly nurse, and know the prisoner. He lives at 8, Courtenay-place, St.
James's-street. I have been acting as nurse to his wife, who was confined on last Wednesday week at night of twins,
both male children, and I have been residing in the house as a lodger for the last ten weeks. There were three
other children in the family, Charles Gouldstone, three years and a half old; Herbert Gouldstone, aged two years
and a half; and Frederick Gouldstone, aged 16 months. Last night, between five and six o'clock, I was in Mrs.
Gouldstone's bedroom washing the two babies. I was in the act of washing them when the prisoner came in from the
street, at about five o'clock or a quarter past five. I heard him go into the kitchen. The two eldest children were
at that time in the kitchen playing, and Freddy was in the bedroom with me. When I heard him come in I went to the
kitchen just to see who it was, and I returned. I spoke to him, and said, "Oh, you did frighten me; I wondered who
it was." He made no reply. I did not notice anything particular about him. I went back into the bedroom, and
finished washing the baby, when he came in. This was ten minutes after he came in doors. He stood by his wife's
bedstead, but he did not speak to her. She asked him, "How is it you are home so early?" He said, "I left the place
while the manager had gone to dinner, and I came home." His wife then asked if he had told them at the shop about
her being confined, and he said "No," with that he took the little boy Frederick off the bed, and went into the
kitchen. [Here the witness began to cry.] When I had finished washing the first infant I went into the kitchen,
leaving the twins by their mother's side. I went into the kitchen and saw him sitting on the fireguard, the three
children playing on the floor. He had a piece of string in his hands. I have seen it since. It was similar to that
produced by Inspector Foulkard. I asked him if he had had his tea, and he said "No." I told him it was ready for
him, and with that I went back to the bed-room, and washed the other baby. I put it into the bed by the side of the
mother, and I then returned to the kitchen. I went to the washhouse and saw a chair by the cistern. The children
at this time were still playing in the kitchen. The cistern was in the washhouse up high. The prisoner was sitting
on the fireguard in the kitchen. Having noticed the chair under the cistern; a perambulator had stood there, but
it had been moved out a bit, I asked who put it there, and the little boy Charlie said, "Daddy did." I again went
back into the bedroom, and got Mrs. Gouldstone out of bed. I made her bed, and she got in again. Just at this
time, as she was getting in, the prisoner came along the passage and looked into the room. I said, "You can come
in Mr. Gouldstone." He made some answer—a mumbling noise—which I could not hear. With that I said to Mrs.
Gouldstone, "Shall I leave the room a minute; perhaps Mr. Gouldstone wants to speak to you?" and she said "Yes, do
my dear." With that, I left the room and went downstairs. I heard the prisoner open the kitchen door—which was shut
as I passed it—walk across the landing, and into the bedroom. I got to the yard door when I heard screams of
murder from the prisoner's wife. I ran upstairs and tried to open the door, she still crying "Murder" inside. I
could hear nobody speak but her. I heard her say, "Good God, he is murdering my children." I did not hear the
cries of children. Being unable to open the door I called out, "help" three or four times, and the landlady of the
house, who was in the yard, ran towards me. We both pushed the door to try to force it open, but could not do so.
With that the prisoner opened the door. The prisoner's wife was still crying out. He opened the door and I said,
"Oh you wicked man; what have you done." He said, "I have killed my children. I am a happy man now. Your other
three children are in the cistern." He said the latter to his wife. I then went to his wife's side and saw both the
babies bleeding from the mouth. One of the children had a bruise over its eye. With that he went into the kitchen.
I examined the children, and finding they were not dead I sent for a medical man. They appeared dying to me. The
prisoner went into the kitchen. I stopped with his wife for a few seconds. Mrs. Gouldstone said, "He has killed
the other three children; go and see." I went into the kitchen and saw the prisoner coming in the kitchen door from
the washhouse with the boy Frederick in his arms. He laid him on the floor. I turned back and saw his wife coming
on to the landing. I ran back to her, as I did not want her to see the child. It seemed as if it had been in
water, and appeared to be dead. I was very much frightened. I went back to the prisoner's wife, and I did not leave
her for ten minutes. When I went into the kitchen again I saw the three children lying on the ground all of a row
side by side. They were swamped with water. By this time a gentleman (a chemist) was present. The prisoner was also
in the kitchen at the time. He said nothing, and no one spoke to him. I saw a police-constable come afterwards, and
saw the prisoner taken into custody. He and the policeman went into the bedroom, and his wife asked him to leave
all the money he had as she had the doctor to pay. Both the children were now dead. One died half an hour after,
and the other at two this morning. Witness did not hear the prisoner charged by the police. Prisoner said, "All the
children are dead now. I shall be hanged, and you will be single. You wished them dead, and now they are." He then
kissed his wife. I have lived in the house for ten or eleven weeks, and the prisoner has always lived on good terms
with his wife; but I noticed he appeared strange after the twins were born. He was not unkind to the children, he
seemed fond of them all; but he had not looked at the twins. The wife is now dangerously ill.

Mr. Wells (the clerk): Inspector Craggs, do you ask for a remand at this stage?

Inspector Craggs: Yes.

Mr. Atkinson: I should like to ask a few questions, with your permission, sir.

Mr. Lister: Do you think your case will be improved by cross-examining now, or will you reserve it?

Mr. Atkinson: I leave it entirely in your hands.

Mr. Lister: Then the prisoner will be remanded till Saturday, and in the meantime inquiries will be made.

The prisoner was then removed.

COLUMN 5 (Letters to the Editor), third letter

To the Editor of the WALTHAMSTOW AND LEYTON GUARDIAN.

DEAR SIR,—I have just seen Mrs. Gouldstone. She is very weak and prostrate, utterly stunned, but most resigned.
The family lived in two rooms wretchedly furnished, and I found no nourishment whatever in the house. I have
supplied her immediate wants, and I think it is a case where I may fairly ask for a few pounds to provide present
necessities, and which I will gladly undertake to administer.—Faithfully yours,
                                        T. H. GRANTHAM ROBINSON.
St. Saviour's, Aug. 9.

1. Two things here are not, so far as the repository shows, in any other source:
   (a) "When searched at the police-station a cutting from a newspaper was found in his possession, containing an
       account of two recent murders." The paper's own statement, unattributed, written on the Friday. No sworn
       document mentions it. Press only; logged in the timeline file.
   (b) Grantham Robinson's letter of THURSDAY 9 August, the day after the murders. It is a different and earlier
       letter from the one of 10 August printed in the issue of 18 August ("wonderfully composed but very weak").
       Here she is "very weak and prostrate, utterly stunned, but most resigned".
2. "Mr. Kinneard": the Times of 15 September gives Kennard (dramatis_personae.md, Howard John Kinnaird).
3. The first-hearing report adds, beyond the IPN of 18 August as transcribed: Craggs brought him up; the magistrates
   (Lister in the chair, Glenny); Atkinson's appearance; the remand exchange between Wells, Craggs, Atkinson and
   Lister; and the end of Ada's evidence of 9 August as reported.
4. In the paper's own narrative, William's words at the bedroom: "All the children are dead now; I shall be hanged,
   and you will be single" and "You wished they were dead, and now they are dead"; in the court report of Ada's
   evidence, "You wished them dead, and now they are." Press both; see the timeline file on "you wished them dead".
5. "he went regularly and punctually to his employment" is the paper's, written on the Friday. Graves's evidence
   has him absent on the Tuesday and the Wednesday.
