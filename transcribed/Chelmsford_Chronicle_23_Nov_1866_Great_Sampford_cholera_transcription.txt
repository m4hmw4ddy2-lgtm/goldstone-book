GREAT SAMPFORD.—THE CHOLERA.—It is with deep regret we hear that since our notice of the mysterious appearance of
the disease at this place it has spread with great rapidity and produced considerable alarm in this village. There
have been about 18 cases and seven or eight deaths, and another fatal termination is hourly expected. The relieving
officer, Mr. Kaslake, medical officer, Mr. Fry, the Rev. R. H. Eustace, vicar, and Mrs. Eustace, are all using every
exertion in their various capacities to relieve the sick and prevent by every means in their power the further
spreading of this malignant disease. A large fire is kept burning in the village where the disease is most
prevalent; the dead are buried immediately, clothing, &c., burned, and every precaution is taken. Mr. Fry has taken
up his residence temporarily, and has been the means of saving many who would otherwise have succumbed. Mrs. Horners
has very kindly made beef-tea ad libitum, which, we are told, was found very beneficial; and Messrs. Portway and
Arnold, the guardians, have been unremitting in their attention to the poor. There has been no case at Thaxted, but
Mr. Barnes the surgeon was very ill after the post-mortem examination of Winterflood. The malady has now passed off
so much at Sampford that Mr. Fry, the surgeon, who took up his residence there for a time, sleeps at home.

NOTES: "ad libitum" is in italics in the print. The figures here ("about 18 cases and seven or eight deaths") are the
paper's; the Essex Standard of 28 November says the attacks did not exceed 18 and the ravages had been exaggerated
(see its transcription). The St Michael's burial register, Great Sampford, November 1866, would give the dead by
name and number (Essex Record Office, D/P 289/1/...): TO OBTAIN.
