TRANSCRIPTION

CENTRAL CRIMINAL COURT.—Sept. 14.

(Before Mr. Justice DAY.)

MANSLAUGHTER.—William Haycraft, 33, who pleaded guilty on Thursday to the manslaughter of his wife, was sentenced
to eighteen months' imprisonment with hard labour.

THE WALTHAMSTOW MURDERS.—William Gouldstone, 25, was put upon his trial for the wilful murder of his five
children.—Mr. Poland and Mr. Montagu Williams prosecuted for the Public Prosecutor; and the prisoner was defended
by Mr. Grain and Mr. G. Elliot.—The prisoner was indicted first for the murder of Charles Gouldstone, a child about
four years of age.—Mr. Poland, in opening the case for the prosecution, dwelt on the painful circumstances of the
crime. The evidence for the defence was mainly directed to showing that members of the prisoner's family had
suffered from insanity, that Gouldstone's behaviour had at various times been peculiar, and that therefore it
might be concluded that the accused had been of unsound mind when he murdered his children.—Mr. Justice Day, in
summing up the case to the jury, observed that the point they had to decide—as there could be no doubt that the
prisoner had destroyed the lives of his five children in the way stated—was not whether the prisoner was sane or
insane, but whether at the time he committed the act he was in such a condition of mind as not to know that he was
killing his children, or that he was not doing wrong. This had been solemnly decided by the judges many years ago
to be the law, and he therefore informed them what the real question was that they had to decide. The learned
judge then directed the attention of the jury to the medical evidence and to the fact that it was admitted by one
medical gentleman that he had no doubt that Gouldstone was quite aware when he killed his children that he was
committing the crime of murder, and that death was the penalty for the crime. His lordship, in conclusion,
explained to the jury that, by the provisions of a recent Act of Parliament, if they found that the prisoner was
insane at the time he committed the act, they would have to return a special verdict stating that the prisoner did
commit the offence, but that he was insane at the time.—The jury retired at twenty minutes past five o'clock to
deliberate upon their verdict. After being absent about a quarter of an hour they returned, and found the prisoner
guilty of wilful murder.—Mr. Justice Day, having put on the black cap, addressed the prisoner, and said that he had
been convicted upon overwhelming evidence of the crime of wilful murder, and the jury had no alternative to the
course of finding him guilty of that offence. It was not his practice to make any observations that might add to
the misery of a man in his position, and all he should say was to exhort him to seek for mercy from a quarter from
which alone it could be obtained. His lordship, who was a good deal affected, then passed sentence of death in the
usual form, and the prisoner, who presented a perfectly composed appearance, was removed from the bar.
