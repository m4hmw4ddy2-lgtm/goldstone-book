p2. ONE WITNESS. Its informant for the figures is Charles Jay, the agent.

EMIGRATION OF LABOURERS.—A large number of labourers, some of whom were accompanied by wives and children, left Essex
on Tuesday for Queensland. They travelled to London by the parliamentary train. Mr. Jay, the agent, went with them to
the ship, and he informed our reporter that in all 350 persons were about to set sail. Of these he stated that 50 took
train at Witham and 50 at Chelmsford. At Chelmsford the departure of the emigrants was witnessed by a large crowd of
people, their immediate friends being on the platform and the sight-seers outside the station. There was much shaking
of hands and kissing of children before the train started, and as it moved away cheers were raised and hats and
handkerchiefs waved by the emigrants, their friends answering by similar tokens and cheering shouts of "Good bye and
God bless you." A flag was also shown from the window of one of the carriages. Among those on the platform we noticed
a number of mechanics who appeared to have left their work for a short space to bid farewell to the labourers. For the
most part the emigrants were in capital spirits, but the mood of one or two of the grey beards seemed pensive and sad.
The scene of the departure on the whole was cheerful but not unaffecting. The emigrants sailed the same day from the
East India docks in the Clipper ship St. James chartered by the Queensland government, and bound for Maryborough and
Brisbane. A London contemporary says that on board, of course, there was the confusion usual on these occasions.
Forward, the single young men were hard at work fixing the mattresses, just drawn from the stores into their berths;
while aft the girls were similarly employed, giggling much as they regarded the narrow coffin-like compartments in
which they will have to sleep for the next three months. Down the main hatch the luggage was being stowed, and the
last lot of provisions got on board; and everywhere, on gangways and hatches, in passages and berths, children of all
ages and sizes swarmed—ruddy, chubby youngsters, whose fat cheeks were a treat to look at, and could hardly be matched
in a day's walk through London. But the little folks were not the only well-favoured ones among the passengers. The
men were broad-shouldered, bright-eyed fellows, in the prime of life, evidently enjoying fine health, and their wives
and families were like them. One little girl, scarcely able to walk, and struggling manfully along the deck with a
ship-biscuit in one hand and an enormous unskinned potato in the other, yet manages to lisp out, as she momentarily
ceases her labours at the edibles she carries, that she is bound for "Eensland." In one case, a strong-minded woman,
after dragging her reluctant husband with her to the docks, changed her mind, and suddenly started home again, taking
him with her. Very few among the passengers are locked-out men, but the ships sailing during the next fortnight, adds
our contemporary, will carry more than 800 men from Suffolk and Essex, and two agents sent down specially for the
purpose are busy filling up further lists.

NOTES:
- "50 took train at Witham": Jay's own account (Labourers' Union Chronicle, 25 April 1874, via Brown and Foxearth) has
  Witham as where "the largest party came together", "some 200 working folks". NOT HELD as a primary text. The Chronicle
  figure is Jay's, reported. Old Ch4's "some two hundred ... at Witham" follows Jay's union account; the two do not
  agree, and both come from Jay.
- The Tuesday is 14 April 1874. The cheering and the flag are at CHELMSFORD in this report.
- "A London contemporary": a London paper, quoted second-hand; the on-board scene is its, not the Chronicle's.
