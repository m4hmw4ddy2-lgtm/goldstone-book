TRANSCRIPTION

THE CASE OF WILLIAM GOULDSTONE.

TO THE EDITOR OF THE TIMES.

Sir,—As one of the medical witnesses in this late trial will you please allow me, in The Times, to say that the
jury evidently understood Dr. Savage's answer "No," to Mr. Poland's question—"From your conversation with
Gouldstone could you certify him to be insane" to imply that he did not consider him to be insane at the time;
whereas Dr. Savage's opinion was that he was insane, but that from his interview with Gouldstone he would be
unable to fill up the certificate required by the Lunacy Commissioners.

"Certifying" a lunatic in accordance with these requirements is in most cases a difficult task, and properly. A
medical man may have a strong opinion, but he must have strong facts to satisfy the Commissioners. Dr. Savage's
opinion was firm, but his facts being meagre he could not "certify" to Gouldstone's insanity, therefore he most
properly replied "No" to Mr. Poland's question. Gouldstone is as insane to the medical profession by his massacre
of his five children as if he had sat in a corner for a month with a paper crown and called himself the Emperor
of all the Russias; he is not legally insane until some medical man has been witness of this massacre, so it
appears from the verdict. The hereditary tendency to insanity, though ridiculed in Court, has a most important
bearing on the case and will no doubt receive its due consideration from the advisers of the Home Secretary.

Truly yours,
WM. SUNDERLAND.
Thaxted, Essex.
