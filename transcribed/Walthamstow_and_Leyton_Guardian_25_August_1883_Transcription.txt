THE WALTHAMSTOW TRAGEDY.

This sad occurrence is still the subject of pitying comment, and it might be thought that the only feeling which
could possibly be evoked by the narrative of such a tragedy would be one of pure and unalloyed sorrow. We find,
however, that there is at least one mind on which it has not had such an influence. A letter of a most singular
character, signed "A Voice from the embryo English Revolution," has been sent to the prisoner. The writer
expresses sympathy with the prisoner, and states that instead of seeing his children half-fed and neglected the
prisoner had had the resolution to put them all out of their misery. After a tirade against the Houses of
Parliament, the writer concludes:—"May peace and tranquillity be with you until you are relieved of an existence
in a world where cunning preys upon ignorance as wolves upon sheep." We should think the writer of such a letter
is a fit candidate for a lunatic asylum.

It appears that the prisoner Gouldstone belongs to a respectable family, and that he himself was well conducted
until this sad occurrence. He is said to be a native of Great Sampford, where his father now resides and carries
on the business of a master blacksmith. Previously Mr. Gouldstone, senr., was in business at Wethersfield, and
when he left for Sampford his son William went to London, where he has since worked as a whitesmith. The poor
mother of the murdered children, for whom great sympathy is expressed in her terrible bereavement, is also a
native of Wethersfield, being the daughter of the late Mr. John Ansell, junr. It is stated that the prisoner is
rather a clever musician; and, indeed, at one time his father, his brothers, and himself almost entirely
constituted the Finchingfield Band. He may have been all that is now said on his behalf; yet it is but too
painfully evident that he has not been very healthy-minded, or that his religious training has been somewhat
defective. And this is the more unaccountable, seeing that when a boy he attended a Sunday School, and was a
member of the Church choir. His demeanour since the dreadful occurrence, and especially the statements which he
has made, go far to establish a belief in his insanity. He has alluded to his children as "My five —— kids!" and
said, "They are in Heaven, if there be such a place." These expressions are a sorry burlesque on his religious
experiences, and were there no gleam of insanity in his eyes, prove him to be of weak intellect, or of a very
depraved mind.

On Saturday, at the Stratford Petty Sessions William Gouldstone, 26 years of age, described as a whitesmith, who
lived at 8, Courtenay-place, St. James's-street, Walthamstow, was charged on remand with having murdered his five
children on the 8th inst.—namely, Charles Gouldstone, aged three years and a half; Herbert Gouldstone, two years
and a half old; and Frederick Gouldstone, aged 16 months, by drowning them in a cistern; and two twin infants,
seven days old, by striking them on the head with a hammer. Mr. H. Batchelor conducted the prosecution on behalf
of the Treasury authorities, and Mr. J. W. Atkinson, solicitor, Stratford, represented the accused.

The prisoner, against whom a coroner's jury has already returned a verdict of "wilful murder" was placed in the
dock shortly after eleven o'clock. After a hurried glance at the crowded court, he quietly folded his arms across
the dock-rail, and while the charge was being read he again gazed round the court, nodding, with a smile, to some
one whom he recognised. The accused, while maintaining an unconcerned aspect, so far as regarded the proceedings,
very often changed his position. He would stretch across the dock, with his head resting on his folded arms on
the bar in front of him; then he would stand erect, with great firmness; and next he would lean against the
siderail of the dock, all the while, however, maintaining his cool demeanour. A tin mug full of water was brought
into the dock for his use during the hearing, but he merely sipped a little water, and standing in the centre of
the dock, rested his elbows on the rail till the case was concluded.

Mr. Batchelor, at the commencement of the proceedings, intimated that the evidence he would call would be very
short, and he asked that the prisoner should be committed for trial.

Mrs. Hamilton, a lodger at 8, Courtenay-place, St. James's-street, and who attended as nurse upon Mrs. Gouldstone,
was recalled and cross-examined by Mr. Atkinson. She said: I have known the prisoner since the 1st of June, and
had ample opportunities to notice his demeanour. Up to this time he was always very kind to his wife. The twins
were born on Aug. 1, and after that date I noticed a great difference in the prisoner. I have never seen him the
worse for drink but once. He was not addicted to drink. After Aug. 1 he appeared to be in a desponding state. He
would come in and sit down and seldom speak to any one. I have never heard him complain of being short of money,
and have never heard him speak about being unable to support his children. I have never heard him speak a thing
against his wife, and never heard him complain of her pawning his things. He was very kind to her, and I have
never known him to speak a cross word to her. I have never heard his wife say that she wished the children were
dead. Since the birth of these twins I have noticed he appeared absent. He never said anything to witness about a
fit of kleptomania coming on him. He did not speak to that effect on the Sunday previous. As to the twins I heard
him say nothing when I was by his wife's side. When he was sitting on the fireguard in the kitchen just before the
murder he was not gazing into the fire. He had his back to it, and was twiddling a piece of string about. I did
not notice that there was a slip-noose in the cord. It may have been there, but I did not notice it. He looked in
the bed-room and muttered something. I saw him in the bed-room after the occurrence, and I did not notice a hammer
till afterwards, when the constable asked for it. After the death of the three children I said, "Oh, you wicked
man, what have you done?" He replied, "I am happy now." I saw one of the children first, and shortly afterwards
the other two. I went to see if they were dead or alive, when I saw him carrying the first one. He seemed very
excited—not absent or wandering, but excited. He said, "I've done it, I've done it." When the constable took him
into the bed-room, he said to Mrs. Gouldstone, "I have murdered all your children; you wished them dead, and now
you have got your wish." I do not know any of his family.

Police-constable William Cheeseman, 86 N, said: I was on duty at St. James's-street, Walthamstow, on the day in
question, and was called to 8, Courtenay-place. I went upstairs into the kitchen, and saw three children lying on
the floor there, the prisoner being in a stooping position over them. He had his hat and coat off, and his shirt
sleeves were tucked up. The clothes of the children appeared to be drenched with water, and were thoroughly wet.
When the prisoner saw me he said, "Good evening, policeman; I have done it; now I am happy, and am ready for the
rope." I told him that what he said would be used in evidence against him, and then he put on his hat and coat,
and asked me to let him see his wife. I then took him into the bedroom to his wife, and when I got there his wife
said, "Oh, you wicked man; what have you done?" The prisoner said, "I have killed all your children, you will be
single again, and I shall be hanged." His wife asked him for money, and he gave it her. The prisoner then kissed
his wife, wished her "Good bye," and I took him away. On the way to the station the prisoner said, "When I took
my money last week I thought of buying a revolver to do it with; but I altered my mind, as I thought it would make
too much noise." He also said, "I had a hard job with the two biggest, but the other little one I soon settled. I
thought it was getting too hot, for I have had five kids in three years and a half, so I thought it time to put a
stop to it." When I got him to the station he said, "I did it like a man." When the charge was read over to him at
the station he said, "Quite right, sir." When I saw the three children at the house I satisfied myself that they
were dead.

By Mr. Atkinson: The moment the prisoner saw me he spoke first. He seemed excited at the time, but not such an
excitement as from drink. He had no appearance of drink. When he spoke about being "ready for the rope," he had
not a piece of rope in his hand. When in the bedroom I did not hear the wife say that she wished the children were
dead. She did not supplement her request for money with the wish that the children were dead. The whole of the
children, as a matter of fact, were not dead. It was at the station that he said, "Quite right, sir." He said
nothing there about not having the money to support his children. I did not hear the word "pawn" mentioned in the
wife's presence. The wife made no reply whatever when he said that he had murdered all her children. I saw the two
infants were covered up. They had blood on them. I did not see them move. When the wife asked for money he gave
her some—a two-shilling piece or a half-crown and a shilling and some coppers. It was about twenty minutes to six
that I went to the house. I saw no evidence of any meals about—no provisions of any kind.

Police-constable James Honey, 41 N Reserve, was next called. He said: I am stationed at the Lea Bridge-road. At
about ten o'clock on Aug. 8, I was in the High-street, Walthamstow, and in consequence of information received I
went to Dr. Twining, and then to 8, Courtenay-place. When I got there Dr. Twining was there and examining the
bodies of the three children in the kitchen.
[AS PRINTED. Honey's sworn statement of the same day reads "about 10 minutes to 6"; "ten o'clock" is the
reporter's garbling of "ten to six". The IPN has the same "ten o'clock", with "August 9".]

be seriously injured. I remained there till Dr. Gould came.

By Mr. Atkinson: The string which had been around the eldest child's neck was not tight; it was loose.

George Folkard, a police-inspector of the N Division, stationed at Lea Bridge-road Station, said: I was at the
station on the 8th inst., when the prisoner was brought in by Police-constable Cheeseman. The constable said,
"This is a charge of murder; this man has murdered his five children." The prisoner said, "That is right, sir. I
did it like a man, too." I went to the house with Dr. Gould, and at about eight o'clock returned to the station,
and charged the prisoner with murdering his three children, Charles, Herbert, and Frederick, and with attempting
to murder his two male infants. He said, "Quite right, sir. What condition was the missus in? I suppose some of
you gentlemen have been around." Dr. Gould said, "Tolerable." The prisoner then asked, "Are the two young ones
dead?" I said, "No." At half-past eight o'clock the following morning I charged him with the murder of the two
younger children. He said, "Yes, sir. The other two are dead, then?" I said, "Yes." When I went to 8,
Courtenay-place, I examined the cistern. It is about 6ft. from the floor, high up, near the ceiling. It was the
top of it that was 6ft. from the floor. The top of it to the ceiling was about 10in., the cistern being about 14in.
deep.

By Mr. Atkinson: The prisoner volunteered these statements. He was a little excited, I thought, but very slight.
He was not so excited as I should expect. He braced himself up when he said, "I did it like a man, too." About two
hours had elapsed since the prisoner last saw his wife. Then he asked me how she was.

Henry Wheatley, police-constable 208 N, stationed at Lea-bridge, said: About a quarter to seven on the evening of
the 8th inst. I was in the cell passage of the station leading to the cell where the prisoner was. The prisoner
said to me, "I have had an extra drop of drink to-day to accomplish this job. There's five of them gone to glory,
and it is a good job, too." Later on, at half-past seven o'clock, he said, "I am sorry for my wife and parents;
but the children will be better off in Heaven, if there is such a place. It is better than leaving them to the
mercy of the world." At about nine o'clock he said, "I wish I had killed the little ones out of the way. I don't
know if I hit them once or twice. I have had this playing on my mind for a long time."

By Mr. Atkinson: He volunteered these statements. He was under my immediate observation all the time. I did not
hear him rambling or mumbling to himself. I observed no traces of drink about the prisoner. He first stated he
had killed the five, and then said he wished he had killed the little ones. I did not in the least observe any
strangeness about him; he was very cool.

Mr. Henry Gould, examined by Mr. Batchelor, said: I reside at Lea-bridge-road, Leyton. I am an M.R.C.S., and
L.S.A. London, and divisional surgeon of police for the district. On the 8th inst., at six o'clock, I was called
by Inspector Foulkard, and went to 8, Courtenay-place. I went into the front room on the first floor, and there on
a bed saw two children only a few days old. They were male children. I examined them, and found they had both
received injuries to the head. I felt that the skull of each was fractured, and blood and froth was issuing from
them. I thought it impossible that the children could live. I went into the kitchen, and there saw three children
lying in a row, side by side, their feet being all one way. They were quite dead and their clothes saturated with
water. One of the children was naked, Dr. Twining having taken the clothes off. I saw no marks of violence on two
of them, but on the eldest (Charles) I found on the neck an indentation mark of a stout cord. It had been put round
the neck. At seven o'clock I again went to the house, and saw that one of the two infants had died. Soon after
seven o'clock the next morning I went and found that the other one was dead. Since that time I have made
post-mortem examinations of the bodies of the whole of the five children. As to the two younger, I found that
their death had been caused by the violence used that fractured the skull of each. As to the three others, from
the condition of the heart and lungs, I came to the conclusion that they died from suffocation, caused by
drowning. All the bodies of the three children were well nourished. On the three there were no marks of violence,
except the indentation mark on the neck of the eldest.

By Mr. Atkinson: I have only seen the prisoner in connection with this case. I would not like to say that I am
perfectly acquainted with the "Cyclopædia of Practical Medicine."

Mr. Wells, the clerk, said this was scientific evidence of the doctor, and he was not well up in questions of
insanity, if that was to be referred to, as that was a speciality.

Mr. Batchelor concurred with Mr. Wells that insanity was not a question for the consideration of this Court.

Mr. Atkinson said he would not press the question.

This concluded the evidence, and the prisoner was committed for trial, Mr. Atkinson intimating that the accused
would reserve his defence.

[rule]

A great number of people assembled outside the courthouse in the hope of either gaining admission to the Court,
or of seeing the prisoner removed. A large crowd of persons assembled at the Stratford Main and Maryland Point
Railway Stations in order to see the prisoner on his way to the courthouse; but they were disappointed, as the
prisoner was brought direct from the House of Detention to the court in a brougham. After the committal, a large
contingent of the K and N divisions were requisitioned, and a cordon drawn round the brougham and magistrates'
entrance. In this brougham the prisoner was driven off, and safely lodged at Clerkenwell.

The Vicar of St. Saviour's, preaching last Sunday evening from the parable of the Prodigal Son, concluded his
sermon as follows, (having showed how the parable taught us two great truths, one respecting sin, and the other
respecting repentance), he said:—By Baptism we are everyone brought into the Church. We are brought in at a time
when our own wills have not come into action, that is, we are baptized in infancy. In that we learn this profound
truth—that God's fatherly relations with us do not depend upon our own wills, convictions, or states of mind. God
in His Providence does not wait till we are full grown before He asks us to become members of His household. He
claims us in our infancy; He gathers us into His Church while as yet we cannot say "I will come," or "I will not
come." He places us in a state of salvation, and there we commence our intellectual and spiritual life.
Accordingly, when a baby is baptized it becomes "a Member of Christ," a "Child of God," and an "Inheritor of the
Kingdom of Heaven." If it dies in infancy we are as sure of its salvation as if it had died after seventy years of
unsullied sanctity. See, then, the position in which Christians personally are placed with regard to sin. Sin
with them is a going out of the Father's house. It is a falling from that state of salvation in which God's mercy
has placed them. This view is of incalculable importance in the religious training of the young. If you teach
children that they are not in a state of salvation, that they are not the children of God, you hurt their minds
in two ways—you first lead them to believe that they cannot commence the service of God until some new relations
have been established between their souls and heaven, and what is far worse you give them an utterly false idea
of sin. Practically, you tell them if they want to serve Satan, they are just in the right position for doing so,
but if they want to serve God they must get into a new position before they can commence. The consequence is the
child, if unhappily it thinks at all about the matter, is bewildered with thoughts about conversion and
sanctification, processes which it cannot possibly understand. One thing only is clear to it—"I am not God's child
yet; some day, somehow, I may become one, but I must wait." If this parable applies in any way to individual
spiritual life, it gives a death-blow to such a view of sin. If the younger son was in any application of the
parable a figure of a Jewish sinner then, or a Christian sinner now, we must, if we follow an honest
interpretation, treat each sinner as one who has departed from the household of God. If our view of human nature
is that persons are not placed in a state of salvation until they become responsible, and then enter the
household of God, we cannot regard this parable as in any way dealing with the spiritual course of Christians. On
the other hand, if we read it in the light of the doctrine of baptism held by our Church, we can treat it as a
story of the human soul, told with the utmost fidelity. By baptism the Church of England teaches that children are
called to a state of salvation, in other words, they are brought into the Father's house. The sin which follows in
succeeding life is the departure—the voluntary departure—from the household of faith. Like the younger son, the
soul in the plenitude of strength and selfish expectation calls for the portion of goods that falleth to it.
Health, talents, opportunities are carried outside the Christian family, such are employed for selfish ends in the
far country of the world; yet, though this perpetually happens, it is not God's intention for any human soul;
God's intention is, that where the soul is placed, there it should remain, ever with the Father, having no
property apart from Him, no purposes that are not of His approval. Here then lies the true power of this parable
as a call to sinners. It tells men that at one time, by their own act and deed they went out from the Father's
house. As the religious sanctions which environs childhood gradually lost strength, as with approaching manhood,
or womanhood, the idea of obeying God gradually receded, and the idea of obeying the world, and living for it, as
gradually became prominent; all this time

the soul was departing, going farther and farther from the divine house, relinquishing the state of salvation. If
you hold that up to any Christian who has lived a worldly life, he will confess, "Yes, that is the true statement
of the case; God did not at first in a state of banishment. I was in the house. I freely left it. Life slowly lost
its sacredness, and duty more and more resolved itself into interest." I might have made the position in which I
was first placed a stepping-stone to higher things, but I chose a lower life—a life that was ever getting lower. I
have, indeed, committed no social offence; outwardly I am respectable and respected, but when looking into my own
heart, I remember the high thoughts, the pure aspirations, the freshness of feeling with which I started in life,
and contrast these with my present feelings, I acknowledge that I have wasted my substance. Thus read in the light
of Christian doctrine the parable of the Prodigal Son, is, in a quiet way, the most awakening view of his
spiritual state that you can set before a sinner. It is true to human nature and experience, no really wise man
will dispute the reality of the portraiture, and the feature which most of all will arouse his attention, and
possibly quicken his repentance, is that where the younger son is described as leaving his father's house against
his father's will. And, oh! what a terrible illustration of this truth our own parish has afforded during the past
few days. On Friday last I saw the unhappy man, whose fearful deed has startled the country, at the House of
Detention, and he quietly and soberly told me that had he not forsaken his God and his church, had he not gone out
from his Father's house, he would not have been in his awful position. He had been a Sunday school boy, he had
been a member of the choir, but he gave up attending church. During his ten months' residence in Walthamstow he
had never attended a place of worship; and now he traces his misery and crime to his own carelessness and neglect,
his wilful departure from the Church into which he had been baptized. God grant that, notwithstanding his
apostacy, he may even now realize His heavenly Sonship, that he is a member of Christ, a child of God, and an
inheritor of the Kingdom of Heaven; and amid the loneliness and misery of a prison life, and the prospect of an
almost inevitable fate, he may yet humbly and sincerely say, "I will arise, and go to my Father."
