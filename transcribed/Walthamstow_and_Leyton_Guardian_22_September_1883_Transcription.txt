THE WALTHAMSTOW TRAGEDY. (column 2)
=========================================================================

At the Central Criminal Court, on Friday week, before Mr. Justice Day, the prisoner, William Gouldstone,
was put upon his trial for the murder of his five children, but the public having been made familiar with
all the details of the evidence and incidents of the crime through the medium of the daily papers, we deem
it unnecessary to reproduce more than a brief summary of the proceedings.

Mr. Poland and Mr. Montagu Williams prosecuted for the Public Prosecutor; and the prisoner was defended by
Mr. Grain and Mr. G. Elliot.—The prisoner was indicted first for the murder of Charles Gouldstone, a child
about four years of age.—Mr. Poland, in opening the case for the prosecution, dwelt on the painful
circumstances of the crime. The evidence for the defence was mainly directed to shewing that members of the
prisoner's family had suffered from insanity, that Gouldstone's behaviour had at various times been
peculiar, and that therefore it might be concluded that the accused had been of unsound mind when he
murdered his children.—Mr. Justice Day, in summing up the case to the jury, observed that the point they
had to decide—as there could be no doubt that the prisoner had destroyed the lives of his children in the
way stated—was not whether the prisoner was sane or insane, but whether at the time he committed the act he
was in such a condition of mind as not to know that he was killing his children, and that he was not doing
wrong. This had been solemnly decided by the judges many years ago to be the law, and he therefore informed
them what the real question was that they had to decide. The learned judge then directed the attention of
the jury to the medical evidence and to the fact that it was admitted by one medical gentleman that he had
no doubt that Gouldstone was quite aware when he killed his children that he was committing the crime of
murder, and that death was the penalty for the crime. **His lordship, in conclusion, explained to the jury
that, by the provisions of a recent Act of Parliament, if they found that the prisoner was insane at the
time he committed the act, they would have to return a special verdict finding that the prisoner did commit
the offence, but that he was insane at the time.**—The jury retired at twenty minutes past five o'clock to
deliberate upon their verdict. After being absent about a quarter of an hour they returned, and found the
prisoner guilty of wilful murder.—Mr. Justice Day having put on the black cap, addressed the prisoner, and
said that he had been convicted upon overwhelming evidence of the crime of wilful murder, and the jury had
no alternative to the course of finding him guilty of that offence. It was not his practice to make any
observations that might add to the misery of a man in his position, and all he should say was to exhort him
to ask for mercy from a quarter from which alone it could be obtained. His lordship, who was a good deal
affected, then passed sentence of death in the usual form, and the prisoner, who presented a perfectly
composed appearance, was removed from the bar.

THE CONVICT AT THE COUNTY GAOL.

The prisoner arrived at Chelmsford on Friday night, having been brought down by the 8.12 p.m. train, in
charge of Mr. Thomson (from the office of Messrs. Gepp and Sons, the Acting Under Sheriffs) and two warders
from Springfield Gaol. He was immediately conveyed to the prison in a cab, and placed in the condemned
cell. The dark tweed suit in which the convict was dressed on his arrival at Chelmsford has since been
exchanged for the usual prison dress, and his head has been shorn of the light curls, which tended to make
him somewhat prepossessing in appearance. In height he stands 5ft. 6in.; he is stoutly built, his
complexion is fair, and his demeanour stolid and indifferent. A prison officer is constantly in attendance
upon him, two men having been told off for this work—one watching by day, and the other by night—and he is
supplied with everything he asks for. In spite of his terrible situation, Gouldstone eats and drinks
heartily, and takes his rest with the felicity of a child. On Sunday he attended the ministrations of the
Rev. W. F. Lumley, the Gaol chaplain, at the chapel; and singularly enough the Psalm for the day (Psalm 79)
contained the following verse: "Oh let the sorrowful sighing of the prisoners come before thee; according
to the greatness of Thy power preserve thou those that are appointed to die." The verse happens to be an
even one (the 12th), and therefore the convict, with the remainder of the prisoners, would repeat it at the
service in response to the clergyman.

OPINIONS OF THE PRESS.

It will be seen from the following extracts that the daily papers which have commented on the case are
exactly divided in opinion, three approving of the death penalty and an equal number pleading for further
inquiry into the state of Gouldstone's mind:—

**The Times**, commenting on the verdict, says:—

For the defence there was only one plea possible—that of the insanity of the prisoner. This plea was, of
course, put forward, as it generally is when a crime of more than usual atrocity is on trial, and when the
evidence for it is clear and conclusive. It was urged on William Gouldstone's behalf that he had been
ordinarily a good husband, a kind father, and a well-conducted man, and that his deeds of murder could be
explained only on the supposition that his mind had become unhinged and that he had ceased to be
accountable for his acts. It was shown that there was insanity in his family, that he himself had been in a
depressed state of mind and had declared repeatedly that he wished himself dead. Medical evidence was
called and an opinion given that the prisoner when he killed his children did not know what he was doing,
and did not know that the penalty for the act was death. All this evidence the jury have heard and have
weighed and have set aside, and have not suffered their verdict to be in any way influenced by it. We do
not see that with the facts of the case before them and with William Gouldstone's own words in disproof of
the medical testimony, they could have come to any other conclusion. William Gouldstone had become reckless
about his own life. He was "ready for the rope," and setting no value upon his own existence, he cared the
less about depriving his children of theirs. There was no sign of any delusion to which he was subject then
or at any other time. His conduct was not that of an insane man; there was no sudden accession of violence;
no notion present to his mind that had any tendency to rob him of his powers of judgment. Cool and
collected throughout, he formed his murderous scheme with care and forethought, carried it deliberately
into effect, and did not shrink at the prospect of the fate which he was aware it would entail upon him. If
he had escaped on the plea of madness, the verdict of the jury would in effect have been that atrocious
criminality is by itself proof of madness, and that everybody must be pronounced mad who does what his
right-minded fellow creatures would turn away from with horror. On this theory no criminal could be held
deserving of punishment. The greater his guilt the more clear and valid would be his excuse. The more
deliberate his deed, the more evident must it be held that something must have been radically wrong with
him, and that the wretched creature was a more proper object of pity than of any harsher feeling. In
William Gouldstone's case, all this could have been pleaded with exceptional force, but the jury have taken
a correct view of their duty and have not suffered themselves to be misled by it. Society, they have
declared, must be protected against him and his like. We admit that he has acted after his kind; that he
has done what his nature has led him to do, but he has given proof thereby that he is the type of man
against whom our criminal code has been enacted and for whom the extreme penalty of the law is the
appointed end. We will not refuse him our pity; no one could be more deserving of it; but we couple our
pity with abhorrence, and we accept unhesitatingly the decision at which the jury have arrived, and the
sentence by which it has been followed.

**The Daily Chronicle** approves of both verdict and sentence, and says:—

However "foul, strange, and unnatural" the murders committed a short time ago at Walthamstow by William
Gouldstone, there was nothing in his own history or in the circumstances under which he committed his
crimes which could warrant the conclusion that his mental condition when he took the lives of his five
children rendered him irresponsible for his actions. This is the view of his case which the jury took after
fifteen minutes' consideration. He has been pronounced guilty of these barbarous murders, and sentence of
death has been unhesitatingly passed upon him by Mr. Justice Day. The case, however, was not without its
peculiarities. Had William Gouldstone been a drunken, brutal, and lazy parent, who habitually neglected to
provide for his wife and his offspring, it would have excited little wonder, perhaps, that his misdeeds
should have culminated in his killing all his little ones. But from the evidence given in the course of his
trial it does not appear that, up to a certain time, he failed in any marked degree to perform his duty to
those dependent upon him. It is, at all events, certain that he devoted his wages, amounting to twenty-five
shillings a week, to support himself and his family; but when one day he found that twins had been added to
his responsibilities, he seems to have lost little time in determining to get rid of all his children by
"one fell swoop." We must admit that it is almost incomprehensible to us that a man, young, strong, and in
constant employment should become furious at the prospect of having to support five children instead of
three, and resolve forthwith on destroying them, when so many thousands of the wage-earning class toil on
and live happily with ten, and even a dozen children. But there can be no doubt, we think, that this
miserable man, whose hours are now numbered, had fully weighed the consequences of his acts on the day in
question. When he had drowned three of his children and had butchered his new-born babes, even while they
were reposing on their mother's bosom, he said, "All the children are dead now, and I shall be hanged." At
a later period, when in the custody of a policeman, he remarked, "I am happy, and ready for the rope." We
think that the culprit's remarks immediately after killing his children, as quoted by us, sufficiently show
that he was not mad in the legal sense of that term.

**The Morning Advertiser** declares that no extenuation of the crime is possible. There will doubtless be an
effort made to save William Gouldstone's life. The mad doctors will be called in, and the usual influences
brought to bear upon the Home Secretary. But it will be difficult to punish the wretch who a week or two
ago knocked the brains out of his little child in the presence of his wife, if the sentence of Mr. Justice
Day is not carried out.

**The Daily Telegraph** says:—Criminal annals supply few stories equalling in atrocity the tale of how
William Gouldstone, found guilty and sentenced to be hanged, barbarously murdered his five innocent
children at Walthamstow. Fortunately for our belief in human nature, a monster of this description only
rarely emerges above the horizon of existence, to horrify ordinary human beings with a deed of awful
pitilessness. For a monster William Gouldstone is, whatever was the state of mind in which he executed his
atrocious purpose, and whether he be responsible for his actions or not. The sole question in regard to
such a crime must be—was the man mad who did it? If, as in the present case, there is an absence of all
reasonable motive which could in any way explain the appalling act, then the presumption of insanity
becomes terribly strong. Mr. Justice Day told the jury that the sole question they had to determine was
whether, at the time when William Gouldstone killed his children, he knew the nature and quality of the act
which he committed. The condemnatory verdict of the jury was only a natural outcome of such a judicial
direction; but, whatever the law may be on the subject, ordinary common sense tells us that a man may be a
raving maniac, and yet know perfectly well "the nature and quality of his actions." The worst sort of
insanity is where a man of quiet and temperate habits feels irresistibly impelled to some frightful deed
which he utterly detests, and the full enormity of which he has the intellectual power to recognise.

"I have had it on my mind for a long time," was one of the remarks which the murderer made when arrested.
What was it that William Gouldstone "had on his mind," which led to such direful results? From other
remarks which he made to the police, it is perfectly evident that what was preying upon his spirits was the
idea of his inability to support a wife and five children. On the 1st of August, the two twins, so soon to
be violently destroyed, saw the light, and on the following day the foreman where the father worked noticed
"something strange" about him. On the day in question he had his attention called to the prisoner's strange
behaviour, and was obliged to complain of the way in which he performed his work, the only excuse that
Gouldstone offered being that "his head felt very queer." In connection with the plea of insanity set up on
the prisoner's behalf, these pains in the head become of great importance, more especially when, as two
witnesses deposed, they were accompanied by threats of suicide. But, in addition to this evidence, which in
itself may not be considered conclusive, two medical men were called for the defence, one of whom swore
that Gouldstone's mother was imbecile, whilst the other affirmed that the prisoner himself was "of weak
mind." Doctors in the witness-box are obliged to be cautious, but there seems no real reason to doubt that
the convicted murderer was tainted with some hereditary mental defect, which might easily ripen into
downright madness. Unless, therefore, the verdict of the jury and the sentence of the Judge are to be
unqualified, it would be well that one or two eminent specialists in cases of mental derangement should be
permitted to examine the convicted murderer. We have purposely avoided dwelling on the sickening details of
that most merciless deed. It would serve no good purpose to picture the deaths of these little ones, who,
although their anguish was soon ended, must have suffered the keenest torments during its brief duration.
The most thorough sympathy must be felt for the unfortunate wife, who was deprived at a single blow of
every one of her children. Her lot is indeed a hard one, and it might have been some small consolation to
her had the jury been able to take the charitable view of her husband's conduct which would have freed him
from responsibility. But she must know that the dictates of public justice and the supreme law of
self-preservation demand that a man like William Gouldstone should, if he be not insane, suffer the severest
penalty which the law allows. The steady, sober citizen, whom the arrival of a child more or less could
drive to such a deed of blood as that at Walthamstow, is adjudged to be hopelessly insane by an instinct
more potent than a legal verdict.

**The Morning Post** writes:—The total absence of any of those motives which almost universally dictate the
crime of murder will inevitably in the present case provoke widespread discussion whether the verdict was
sound, and whether the extreme penalty of the law should be inflicted. Even placing on one side the
evidence adduced on the trial that there has been insanity in the prisoner's family, there are many who will
contend, with much plausibility, that the crime carries per se conclusive proof of madness in the murderer.
Gouldstone appears to have been an affectionate father and a good husband, and it will be contended that
nothing save temporary mental aberration could have led him to destroy his offspring. It will be said that
none but an insane man would have fancied that the only way out of the difficulties which embarrassed him
lay in murdering his children, and voluntarily consigning himself to the scaffold. And this to a certain
extent is true. But under the existing law, or, perhaps, more correctly speaking, its interpretation by the
Judges, this view of insanity is untenable. The law was laid down in M'Naughten's case when the Judges, in
reply to questions put by the House of Lords, answered that if a man was in a condition of mind to know that
the act he was committing was wrong, and consequently punishable, he should be held responsible for the
commission of that act. Grave doubts have been cast on the soundness of this ruling, but it can now only be
set aside by Act of Parliament. In summing up to the jury at the Old Bailey, Mr. Justice Day followed the
lines laid down in the case to which we refer, and tested by the standard thus supplied, Gouldstone's guilt
was never for a moment in doubt. He was perfectly conscious of the heinousness of the crime which he
committed; it was premeditated according to his own admission for some days before its perpetration, and he
perfectly understood the consequences which its commission would entail. The object was logical, though
inadequate—namely, to get rid of what he considered an intolerable burden. He further knew that he would be
called to account for his act, and that his life would be forfeited; and that this consummation was part of
his design is shown by the fact that he congratulated his wife on the prospect of speedily becoming a single
woman. From beginning to end he argued and he acted as a perfectly rational man within the definition laid
down by the Judges in M'Naughten's case. But, notwithstanding the failure of the defence, public opinion,
unless we are very much mistaken, will pronounce against this man being dealt with like an ordinary
murderer. The very enormity of the offence and its motiveless character when measured by the standard which
regulates the action of sane men will be considered by many as conclusive proof of madness on the part of
the convict.

**The Daily News** writes:—The doubts which on Saturday last we expressed with regard to the verdict in the
trial of William Gouldstone for the murder of his five children are more than confirmed by statements which
have since been made public. All his shopmates express the opinion that he was of unsound mind when he
committed the murder. This may not amount to much. Still the testimony of men who had worked with
Gouldstone for years must certainly have been entitled to some weight, and if, as the foreman of the shop in
which he worked states, his comrades were unhesitatingly of opinion, based on their intimate daily
intercourse with him, that he was insane when he committed the murder, we are quite at a loss to understand
why they were not permitted to give evidence as they wished to do. But not only was some evidence which
might have been adduced in the prisoner's favour withheld from the jury, but from two other letters it seems
quite clear that some of the most important of the evidence which was before them was strangely
misunderstood. If we may assume—as no doubt we may—that a communication written by one of the jury correctly
represents the view of the rest of the jurymen, they were, to say the least of it, greatly influenced against
the verdict of insanity by the testimony of Dr. Savage, senior physician at Bethlehem. "The prisoner had
been examined on the Thursday by the head physician from the Bethlehem Hospital Lunatic Asylum, who had been
conversing with him for, say, from twenty minutes to half an hour. When asked by Mr. Poland if he would
certify that the prisoner was insane he distinctly stated 'No,' also declaring he believed the prisoner knew
at the time he was committing the crime of murder." Yet the witness whose testimony was thus understood by
the jury writes a letter, in which he says "the facts are plain. A young man of 26, who had been a
well-behaved and industrious man, odd in some of his ways, is seized with fear of impending ruin to himself
and family, and kills them to send them to heaven. The act is an insane one." As to his being unable to
certify the insanity of the man from his personal interview of fifteen to thirty minutes, Dr. Savage says
that a good deal was made of it, but it does not follow that the prisoner may not have been insane at the
time the act was committed. That, however, would seem to have been the inference of the jury, and upon this
inference evidently they partly based their verdict. Dr. Savage seems to have had no doubt whatever about
the man's irresponsibility. "The poor man Gouldstone is," he says, "to my mind a typical case of insanity
associated with insane parentage." It is certainly very singular that a witness whose opinion was so
strongly in favour of a verdict of insanity should have been so largely instrumental in getting a verdict of
wilful murder. It is very satisfactory that the whole case will be subjected to the revision of a
clear-headed Home Secretary.

"SIR,—I feel bound to take notice of the letter written to you by "One of the Jury" in this case, as there
seems to be great danger that the prisoner will suffer through misunderstanding of my opinion. The skilful
cross-examination of Mr. Poland gave me no opportunity of representing my own opinion on the man's sanity. I
was forced to own that in a short interview, from the facts seen by myself, I could not have signed a
certificate of insanity. I doubt not but that if I had expressed a willingness to sign one that the haste of
the proceeding would have been used as an argument against its value.

"I did say, however, that, taking my examination with the history of the man and the crime, I have no doubt
that he was of unsound mind. The judge opposed strongly attempts to get my opinion, believing the common
sense of the jury to be the best judge of sanity. This is all very well if the facts are explained by one
understanding their value, and not otherwise. That the patient knew he had killed his children, and that he
knew he might be hanged, I could not deny, but knowledge of this kind does not exclude insanity.

"I have patients of the most insanely dangerous class here who have said the same things which Gouldstone
said, and who know as much as he does. Yet they are mad. William Gouldstone ought not to suffer without a
careful independent investigation of his history and the history of his crime, one not confined to an
examination of twenty minutes or half an hour.—I am, yours truly,

                                   "GEO. H. SAVAGE, M.D.
"Bethlehem Hospital, Sept. 17."

The following letter appears in The Times:—

"SIR,—I feel it my duty to write shortly about the case of William Gouldstone, the murderer of his five
children. Justice demands further investigation of the case. The facts are plain. A young man of 26, who had
been a well behaved and industrious man, odd in some of his ways, is seized with fear of impending ruin to
himself and family, and kills them to send them to Heaven. The act is an insane one, and I think little more
should have been needed to prove it to be such; but it was proved that his mother and aunt both suffered
from precisely similar fears of ruin, and though the Judge ridiculed the importance of a second cousin on
his father's side being insane, I would repeat emphatically that there being an insane taint which could
have been shown to exist in several second cousins and others on the father's side, was of great importance.
A great deal was made of my statement that I could not certify to his insanity from my personal interview of
fifteen to thirty minutes. It does not follow that the man may not have been insane at the time the act was
committed. There is the feeling abroad that a man if insane and irresponsible is always so, whereas the most
insane people often are collected enough during the greater part of their lives. The poor man Gouldstone is,
to my mind, a typical case of insanity associated with insane parentage. He has done his work, which was
purely mechanical. Well, but he had no power to resist, and the act he perpetrated depended on an insane
feeling of misery. I have no doubt he would have sooner or latter developed delusions. The Medical Officer
to the House of Detention told me he considered him to be suffering from melancholia. I trust the prisoner
will not be allowed to be hanged. I may say that I am not one who is in the habit of defending criminals on
the plea of insanity.—I am, yours truly,

                                   GEO. H. SAVAGE, M.D., Physician Bethlehem Hospital.
                                   September 15th."

"Pass we now to the much controverted question as the abolition of capital punishment, and let me ask
whether or not in this case a life-long imprisonment would not fully meet its requirements? Many are of
opinion that even as regards punishment a life-long imprisonment is quite equal to a brief death-agony; but
let us take a higher view of this point, and compare the value of what would in the one case be little more
than a death-bed repentance, but in the other a repentance, the fruit of clearer ideas and better thoughts,
produced by the instruction of an earnest and able minister.

"There is still another point worthy of consideration—and that is the case of the stricken wife. Her husband
has murdered all her children, but she clings to the belief that he knew not what he did. Would her present

"SIR,—The jury seemed to have founded their conclusion upon the fact that the wretched man knew that he had
committed murder as appeared from his remarks to the police-constable. I think everyone who has any
experience of insane persons will admit that this knowledge on the part of the prisoner has really no
bearing on the question of his responsibility. If you ask any person who has had charge of lunatics to give
you his experience of assaults committed by persons in that condition he will tell you that these people
almost invariably acknowledge what they have done, go to the keeper directly afterwards and tell the whole
story, and appear to glory in it. I believe the most unanimous opinion of those who have paid close
attention to the facts in this case is that this poor creature, whatever you may call his degree of madness,
was at the time he committed this act bereft of reason; so that, instead of knowing that he was doing a
frightful murder, he was possessed of the delusion that he was committing a righteous action. Whatever may
be the refinements of lawyers, no man of common sense can understand how a man, who up to the moment of this
tragedy had been of exemplary conduct, would have barbarously destroyed the little children to whom he was
most affectionately attached, unless he was under some uncontrollable delusion, which makes him rather an
object for compassion than for punishment.—I am, Sir, yours, &c.,

                                   "ONE WHO WAS PRESENT AT THE TRIAL.
"Sept. 17."

STATEMENT OF THE CONVICT'S WIFE. (column 5)
=========================================================================

Mrs. Gouldstone, who is exerting herself to the utmost to save her unfortunate husband's life, writes to
state her solemn belief that when the crime was committed the convict was not responsible for his act. She
says: "During our five years' married life I frequently noticed that my husband was absent-minded and given
to brooding. If he were asked a question he would not, no matter how simple it was, or however easy to
answer, reply at once. He would think over it, and sometimes say something not concerning the question. We
commenced housekeeping on 21s. a week, and latterly he had 25s. It was sometimes difficult to make ends
meet, when the children came so rapidly, and this weighed on his mind, I fear, although he never spoke of it
to me—except in the way of saying how sorry he was that my hands were so full, and that we could not go out
together as we used to do. He was very kindly with me and the children, handy and willing if they were
ailing, and he constantly helped me in household duties. For hours together he would keep the children going
with his concertina, which he was very fond of playing, while I was engaged in household duties. But shortly
before my last babies were born he said to me that he believed he had not many weeks to live, which was a
strange remark that I have often thought of since. He never complained, except that he did not know how I
should manage with my hands already so full, and soon to be even fuller. I never knew until very lately that
there was any insanity in the family. Knowing that now, and looking back on all that has happened, I am
certain he was insane, and that the times when I found him sitting thinking lately he was distressing
himself about our growing family. Since the occurrence I have seen my husband three times at the House of
Detention, once at the Old Bailey, and yesterday (Tuesday) in the prison at Chelmsford. On each occasion he
has been as happy as a child at play. At Chelmsford he said he had seen several relatives whom he had not
met for a long time, but neither he nor I made any reference to the fate of the children. His sister and
aunt accompanied me yesterday. He asked me to take care of myself, and turning to his relatives, said, 'You
will take care of her, won't you?' Then he said, 'I have broken God's commandment, and I must expect to
suffer, but He will forgive me for what I have done.' He realises his position in so far as he believes he
will die, but he seems to have no notion of the enormity of killing the children. In a letter I had from him
he spoke of them as being in heaven, where he hoped soon to join them. Here is a letter I have from him,
dated the 15th, in the same strain:—

My Dear Wife—I feel it is a Pleasure to Write a few Lines to you and trusting to God that it will find you
as well as Can Be Expected after the Dreadfull news but I am thankful to God that I feel Happey as I may
Expect and I Hope you will Look to God to Help you and He Will Comfort you as Well as myselfe Dearest Wife I
Hope you will Receive this Letter this Evening and then I Hope you wont fret much about me as what God Doest
is for the Best and I Hope you will Be able to Come and See Me Over a Short Time and I Hope Aunt and all at
Home are quite Well and all Cousins and Rember me to all that know me, and Let Cousin Gouldstone in
Mash-street know How I am and I am going to Send a few Lines to Mr. G. Sampford, and I Hope all Will forgive
me for I am thinking about you all. But I hope you all Will Pray to God to Comfort One and all of you and
Bear that in mind that God Will forgive all. Dear Wife do not fret about what I Have to Live Upon Because I
Can Have What I Wish for in my Liveing from the Prison and you may Believe that the Chapeling will Give me
all I Require and I thank God that I Have Been Preparing for the Other World and which I shall do more so
now and Live in Hopes to meet you all In Heaven. So Good By God Bless you

                                   WILLIAM GOULDSTONE.
Her Majesty's Prison, Chelmsford, Essex.
                                   Sept. 15, 1883.

I have no doubt he did not know and could not help what he did. He was always most affectionate to me and
the children, and I cannot think that he should suffer a shameful death for what would be to him, if he only
understood it, the most awful distress."

                        ___________________

SIR,—I have just read Mrs. Gouldstone's letter in your paper this morning, and write a line to confirm the
truth of her statements. From strict personal inquiries, and as the result of a long interview with the
prisoner in the House of Detention, I firmly believe that he was not in his right mind when he committed the
fearful deed, and had the question of insanity, which has been undoubtedly proved to be hereditary on both
sides of his family, been brought before the jury, I am certain the verdict would have been otherwise. I may
add that I have not seen his wife since her husband's conviction. We are getting up a petition in
Walthamstow, praying for a reprieve.—Faithfully yours,

                                   J. H. GRANTHAM ROBINSON,
                                   Vicar of St. Saviour's, Walthamstow.
Sept. 20th.

                        ___________________

The prisoner was visited on Tuesday by the High Sheriff of the county (Mr. J. Oxley Parker), and also by his
wife, mother, and sister. Gouldstone attends service daily, and the prison chaplain, the Rev. W. Faithful
Lumley, is unremitting in his attentions to him.

                        ___________________

The execution will take place at Her Majesty's Prison at Springfield on Monday morning, October the 1st; and
it will therefore devolve upon Mr. J. Oxley Parker, the High Sheriff of Essex, to select a hangman in place
of Marwood, the late executioner. Several applications for the office have been made both at the Gaol and to
the High Sheriff.

1. **The Trial of Lunatics Act 1883 WAS put to the jury.** `notes/part_three_context_law_and_medicine.md`
   records as OPEN whether the Act was mentioned at the trial at all, noting that a search of the Old
   Bailey transcript finds nothing. This report has Mr Justice Day explaining it to the jury in terms, as
   "the provisions of a recent Act of Parliament", and telling them that a finding of insanity would
   require the special verdict. The jury retired at 5.20 p.m., were out about a quarter of an hour, and
   returned the old verdict. The book's sharper claim is now sourced.
2. **The initials "J. H. Grantham Robinson"** are a misprint. He was Thomas Harrison Grantham Robinson.
   The Guardian prints T. H. elsewhere on the same page and in its funeral report of 18 August.
3. **Dating of Elizabeth's statement.** Her own text says she saw William at Chelmsford "yesterday
   (Tuesday)", which was 18 September, so she gave the statement on Wednesday 19 September; Grantham
   Robinson replies on Thursday 20 September having read it "in your paper this morning". The Guardian is
   a Saturday weekly, so both letters were written to a daily and are reprinted here. The project has dated
   her statement "c.21 September" from the Essex Weekly News. The first publication was Thursday
   20 September, in a London daily not yet identified.
4. **Elizabeth's statement here and the Essex Weekly News version of 21 September should be collated
   word by word.** They appear to be the same text. Any divergence matters, because this is the only
   document in which she speaks at length.
5. Savage's two letters are distinct: one to the paper carrying "One of the Jury" (Bethlehem Hospital,
   17 September) and one to The Times (15 September). Check both against
   `transcribed/William_Gouldstone_Lancet_letter_transcription.txt` before treating any of the three as the
   same document.
6. The Guardian's own framing of the press, "exactly divided in opinion, three approving of the death
   penalty and an equal number pleading for further inquiry", is a usable structure for the press chapter
   and it names the six: The Times, the Daily Chronicle and the Morning Advertiser for the sentence; the
   Daily Telegraph, the Morning Post and the Daily News against it.
