THE FATE OF GOULDSTONE.

William Gouldstone, condemned for the murder of his five children at Walthamstow, under circumstances with which our
readers are familiar, has been respited for seven days. The reason for this is stated in another column, and even
without such publication it would not be difficult for the public to surmise why the culprit, who was to have been
hanged next Monday, has had granted to him a further week's life. As we state elsewhere, on Tuesday the High Sheriff
of Essex received a letter from the Home Secretary signifying her Majesty's commands that Gouldstone be respited until
Monday, October 8th, in order that medical inquiry may be made as to his sanity. Such an inquiry is the only rational
outcome of the conflict of opinion which has prevailed ever since the sentence of death was pronounced upon him after
a somewhat curt comment by the Judge and a brief conference by the Jury who tried him. The daily press have discussed
the subject at length, seeing that Parliament is prorogued, and have been almost equally divided in opinion, the
Times taking a prominent position in favour of carrying out the capital sentence. It is not the first time that the
"leading" journal has been at variance with its once acknowledged attributes. Remarking that the plea of insanity is
generally set up when a crime of more than usual atrocity is on trial, and when the evidence for it is clear and
conclusive, and reflecting on the prisoner's coolness throughout those transactions, the very mention of which, we
fancy, must make every really sane man shudder, our contemporary argues: "If he had escaped on the plea of madness,
the verdict of the Jury would in effect have been that atrocious criminality is by itself proof of madness. On this
theory no criminal could be held deserving of punishment." The argument here misses the mark, we think, inasmuch as
the absence of motive, and the unquestionable revengeless character of the crime, are entirely overlooked. The Times
follows up its arguments by accepting "unhesitatingly" the verdict, and the sentence by which it was followed. The
Daily Telegraph, avoiding the "harrowing" part of the subject, arrives at a conclusion which will be more in accord
with the reflecting public: "The steady, sober citizen, whom the arrival of a child more or less could drive to such a
deed of blood as that at Walthamstow, is adjudged to be hopelessly insane by an instinct more potent than a legal
verdict." Thus two typical journals are as much at variance as is possible on the question at issue, and more might
be quoted equally diverse in their views. But we think it must be generally accepted that the hereditary taint in the
prisoner's family, the previous history of the man himself, his demeanour at the time he committed the appalling
deed, and his conduct afterwards, down to the writing of the letter which we published in our last, all point to the
conclusion that the man was mad—as any man must be who, without any reasonable motive, butchers the children whom he
has previously loved tenderly, because of an addition to the number of mouths to fill—although he may, to use the
legal phrase, know "the nature and quality of his actions." He cannot be adjudged the less insane because, immediately
after the horrible act, he coolly says "I have had it on my mind for a long time," and tells the policeman he is
"ready for the rope." The distinction drawn by Mr. Sunderland, surgeon, of Thaxted, in a letter we publish to-day,
between medical madness and legal madness is an interesting point in the controversy, and helps to prove the
necessity of some alteration in the law as to murder and insanity, if a Jury are to receive such a direction as they
did in this case from the Judge, and, acting upon it, return a simple verdict of guilty, which is followed up by the
capital sentence, to be followed in turn by a violent agitation of public feeling, then by a respite, a medical
inquiry, and finally—for we believe that is what it will come to in the end—the setting aside of the sentence, and a
life-long confinement in a lunatic asylum.
