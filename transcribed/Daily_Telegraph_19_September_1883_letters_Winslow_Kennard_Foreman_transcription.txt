THE CONVICT GOULDSTONE.

TO THE EDITOR OF "THE DAILY TELEGRAPH."

SIR—The summing-up of Mr. Justice Day and conviction of the unfortunate man Gouldstone, now under sentence of death
for the murder of his five children at Walthamstow, naturally draw attention to the existing law relating to trials of
persons in those cases where the "plea of insanity" has been raised.

In England, if a person, either of sound or unsound mind, commits a murder, and at the time of the act it can be shown
to the satisfaction of the jury that he was conscious of the gravity of what he was doing, and knew what the penalty
for the crime was, he is held accountable for his actions and renders himself amenable to the law of the land.
Contrary opinions have from time to time been expressed on this question, both by medical and legal authorities, with
suggestions for amendment of the law dealing with criminal lunatics; but the fact stands out in all its awfulness that
there is a great probability of a poor criminal lunatic, however insane he may be, being handed over to the public
executioner simply because he can discriminate between what is right and what is wrong, the crime being committed
with a diseased and disordered brain, and, to all who have studied mental physiology, considered as an irresponsible
being.

If we examine the inmates confined in any asylum, with the exception of the idiots and demented cases, we find there
are few, if any, who cannot distinguish between right and wrong. Are we, therefore, to hold these certificated
patients as responsible beings? Mr. Justice Day informed the jury that the law considers every person of sound mind
unless legally proved to be otherwise. This, in common parlance, means that before an individual can be properly
pronounced non compos mentis a Commission of Lunacy must be first held upon the case, and the pronounced mental
condition must depend upon the verdict at such inquiry. In England a Commission of Lunacy is rarely held, and is only
insisted upon where a question of dealing with property arises. Of the 77,121 certificated persons in asylums at the
present day, 356 have only been found lunatic by such inquisition, the law, it may be presumed, considering the
remaining 76,765 as responsible individuals, and answerable for any infringement of our statutes. No comments are
required to show the fallacy of such a law, or the necessity for the amendment and alteration of it.

For upwards of forty years the judges of our land have been obliged to rule as Mr. Justice Day did in the case of
Gouldstone.

In discussing briefly the case at issue, I would draw attention to the evidence of Dr. Savage, resident superintendent
of Bethlehem Hospital, his expressed opinion being that he considered "his mind was unsound at the time the act was
committed," though apparently knowing the difference between right and wrong. Another strong point is the hereditary
predisposition to insanity. His mother was of unsound mind at the time of his birth, whilst other members of his
family, closely related to himself, were and are similarly affected. The case is one where it appears to me strict
inquiry should be made before allowing the extreme penalty of the law to take its course, which it surely will, unless
the authorities at the Home Office ignore the rule of law as mentioned above, and show clemency to a fellow-creature
whose mind was considered by Dr. Savage to be "unsound at the time the act was committed," an opinion not only derived
from hearsay evidence, but formed by Dr. Savage as a result of a personal examination.—Faithfully yours,

FORBES WINSLOW.
14, Cavendish-place, Sept. 17.

TO THE EDITOR OF "THE DAILY TELEGRAPH."

SIR—I am very happy to see that the true issue of this case is slowly but surely being placed before the public. As
the employer of William Gouldstone, I am as sure as are his foreman and shopmates that he was insane when he committed
the act. The ruling of the judge, although, doubtless, a proper exposition of the law, precluded the jury from
bringing in any other verdict than that of wilful murder. Had I myself been on the jury I should have felt compelled
to return that verdict.

That the man knew what would be the consequences of his act there can be no possible doubt; but that the chief
physician of Bethlehem Hospital should be prevented from telling the jury that, having examined Gouldstone and having
heard the evidence, he believed him to be of unsound mind, and that the jury should not be permitted to receive my
evidence to the effect that it was the unanimous opinion of Gouldstone's fellow-workmen that he was insane, may perhaps
be law, but it will be for the public to determine whether it is justice.

Sir William Harcourt will have the whole matter carefully and fully laid before him, and it is satisfactory to know
that his decision will not be based upon the dry letter of the law alone. This appears, indeed, to be a case in which
law and common-sense are at variance.—Yours truly,

H. J. KENNARD.
67, Upper Thames-street, Sept. 18.

TO THE EDITOR OF "THE DAILY TELEGRAPH."

SIR—The judge presiding at the Gouldstone trial told us (the jury) that the law regarding insanity was this, "That if
a person was proved to be of sound mind up to the time of committing a certain deed; if he knew the nature of that
deed and the penalty it involved, and if after this he still appeared of sound mind, we were bound according to this
law to say such a person was not insane." What was the evidence? For five years he had worked at the Falkirk Iron
Works, and had always done his work satisfactorily like a sane man. The seven witnesses who were called from these
works admitted this, and they also stated that at intervals he had said he wished he was dead, that he would like to
throw himself in front of a train or down the lift hole if he were sure of killing himself without any pain. He
suffered from a rupture, and these remarks were often made when it pained him. When the great addition to his family
came, he appeared somewhat upset, but did not mention the fact to any of his fellow-workmen. He then resolved to rid
himself of his children, and intended at first to do it with a revolver, but thought that would make too much noise,
so he planned it the other way, going out first, as he said, to have an extra drop of drink to nerve him to the job.
When it was over he said, "Now I shall have the rope." The constable who took him, and the officer to whom he was
handed in prison, stated that he seemed perfectly aware of what he had done, and quite sane. We did not misunderstand
Dr. Savage, who said he would not certify him as insane, though he had examined him, but he "considered he was of weak
mind owing to his slowness of appreciation of questions concerning the crime of which he was accused." The father and
a sister stated that his mother and an aunt were at times of unsound mind and required looking after, but neither of
them had ever been confined as lunatics. There was, however, a cousin of his father's on the male side who had been a
soldier, and died in a lunatic asylum. These were the salient points of the evidence, and upon this the jury were
quite agreed that the case of insanity was not made out, and therefore were compelled to return a verdict of guilty.
Whatever now may be the end of this poor unhappy man, I hope that his wife, whom he appears to have been fond of to the
last, will not suffer from want in this awful calamity; and should a subscription be made for her, I shall be very
pleased to assist it.—Yours obediently,

THE FOREMAN OF THE JURY.
