Mr. Justice Day, in summing up the case to the jury, observed that the point they had to decide—as there could be no
doubt that the prisoner had destroyed the lives of his five children in the way stated—was not whether the prisoner
was sane or insane, but whether at the time he committed the crime, he was in such a condition of mind as not to know
that he was killing his children or that he was not doing wrong. This had been solemnly decided by the judges many
years ago to be the law, and he therefore informed them as to the real question they had to decide. The learned judge
then directed the attention of the jury to the medical evidence, and to the fact that it was admitted by Dr. Savage
that he had no doubt that when the prisoner killed his children he knew perfectly well that he was committing the
crime of murder, and that death was the penalty for the crime. His lordship in conclusion explained that by the
provisions of a recent Act of Parliament, if they found that the prisoner was insane at the time he committed the
act, they would have to return a special verdict stating that the prisoner did commit the offence, but that he was
insane at the time.

The jury retired at twenty minutes past five o'clock to deliberate upon their verdict. After being absent about a
quarter of an hour, they returned and found the prisoner guilty of wilful murder.

Mr. Justice Day, having put on the black cap, addressed the prisoner, and said that he had been convicted upon
overwhelming evidence of the crime of wilful murder, and the jury had no alternative but to find him guilty of that
offence. It was not his practice to make any observations that might add to the misery of a man in his position, and
all he should do was to exhort him to seek for mercy from the quarter from which alone it could be obtained. His
lordship, who was a good deal affected, then passed sentence of death in the usual form, and the prisoner, who
presented a perfectly calm and composed appearance, was removed from the bar.
