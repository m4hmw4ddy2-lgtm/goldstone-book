Chapter Twelve

AT ANOTHER PLACE

On the Saturday morning, the eighteenth of August, the crowd of onlookers returned to the railway stations. The week before they had waited at Maryland Point while he came in at Stratford. This time there were crowds at all three stations. They were disappointed, the Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian reported, "as the prisoner was brought direct from the House of Detention to the court in a brougham." The closed carriage spared him the railway stations altogether. A great number of others waited outside the Court House in Great Eastern Road, in the hope, the paper said, "of either gaining admission to the Court, or of seeing the prisoner removed."

It was the third time in ten days that he had been brought before the magistrates at Stratford Petty Sessions. On the first morning he had been remanded till the Saturday, and on the Saturday for a week more. In between, on the Monday, the coroner's jury had returned its verdict of wilful murder, and the coroner had issued his warrant committing him for trial. That was one road to the Old Bailey. The magistrates still had to come to the end of theirs, and decide for themselves whether there was evidence enough to send him there.

He was placed in the dock shortly after eleven o'clock. Mr Nathaniel Powell was in the chair, and with him Mr Arthur Lister, who had remanded him on the first morning. Mr Wells was the clerk, and Mr Atkinson was there again for the prisoner. The prosecution had changed hands. A week before, Craggs had told the bench that the Treasury would take it up, and now Mr Batchelor, from the office of the Solicitor to the Treasury, appeared on behalf of the Public Prosecutor.

The Guardian set down what William did in the dock from then until the end of the case.

"After a hurried glance at the crowded court, he quietly folded his arms across the dock-rail, and while the charge was being read he again gazed round the court, nodding, with a smile, to some one whom he recognised. The accused, while maintaining an unconcerned aspect, so far as regarded the proceedings, very often changed his position. He would stretch across the dock, with his head resting on his folded arms on the bar in front of him; then he would stand erect, with great firmness; and next he would lean against the siderail of the dock, all the while, however, maintaining his cool demeanour. A tin mug full of water was brought into the dock for his use during the hearing, but he merely sipped a little water, and standing in the centre of the dock, rested his elbows on the rail till the case was concluded."

Mr Batchelor told the bench that the evidence he would call would be very short, and that at the end of it he would ask for the prisoner to be committed for trial. Six witnesses were sworn that morning. The printed heading of their depositions recorded that each had been examined "in the presence and hearing of William Gouldstone".

Ada was recalled first. Twice before at Stratford she had given her evidence without a question from the defence. This time Mr Atkinson cross-examined her, and the clerk wrote down her answers in her own first person, but not the questions that drew them.

She had known the prisoner, she said, "since 1st June last", and had had "ample opportunity of observing prisoner's demeanour". Up to the time of the occurrence he had seemed kind to his wife and children. After the twins were born on the first of August she saw "a marked difference" in him, and from then on "he appeared to be in a desponding state." He had never been a drinking man, and she had seen him the worse for liquor only once.

Then came the things she had never heard. "I never heard him complain of being short of money. Never heard him talk of not being able to support his children. Never heard him complain of his wife pawning his things. Never heard him say a cross word to his wife." The Times reported her next answer: "She had never heard the wife say that she wished the children dead."

"He never spoke to me on the Sunday previous that he had a fit of cleptomania coming on," she said. She had seen him on the fire guard "with his back to the fire, with a piece of cord in his hand", and had not noticed a slip noose in it. "At the time the murder was committed he seemed excited." She signed her answers and was sworn to them again.

Police-constable Cheeseman went through it once more, from the kitchen to the station, and Mr Atkinson cross-examined him too. His answers were recorded the same way. When the constable first saw him, William had seemed excited, but "didn't appear to be in drink", and "hadn't a piece of string in his hand." Cheeseman had never heard the wife say she had wished the children dead, nor heard the word "pawn" in her presence, and William had never told him he was short of money. Of the bedroom he said: "She made no reply to him when he said he had murdered all the children. I saw blood on the babies' faces. His wife was crying."

Police-constable Honey had gone for Dr Twining at about ten minutes to six that evening, and had found him already at the house, examining the three children in the kitchen, and had watched him take the string from Charles's neck. In reply to the bench, the Guardian reported, Mr Batchelor said "he did not propose to call Dr. Twining, as he was very ill. If it was necessary at the trial he would, of course, have to do so." Cross-examined, Honey said: "I saw the noose on the string which was loose round the child's neck."

Inspector Folkard told again what William had said to him at the station, and of those words he said, "He volunteered all this statement to me." To Folkard he had seemed "very slightly excited". He "was not vacant but was rather cool." Police-constable Wheatley, who had been in the passage outside the cell that night, gave the three things William had said to him there. He had not heard him mumble or talk to himself, and had seen no traces of drink about him. He had appeared, Wheatley said, "perfectly cool", and "not strange in the least."

Last came Henry Gould, the divisional surgeon, with his findings from the house and the post-mortems, as the inquest had heard them five days before.

"All the bodies of the children were well nourished."

Mr Atkinson cross-examined Gould too, and The Times took down what followed. "I have only seen the prisoner in connexion with this case," Gould said. "I should not like to say that I am perfectly acquainted with the 'Cyclopædia of Practical Medicine.'"

Mr Atkinson spoke to the clerk. "I have a long extract, will you take it down."

"It is scientific evidence, I presume, I wait the order of the Bench," said Mr Wells.

Gould said that he was afraid he was not well up in questions of insanity, to which he presumed the questions referred, "as it was a speciality". Mr Batchelor said that, "as Mr. Wells had remarked, insanity was not a question for the consideration of this Court", and that if Mr Atkinson wanted information he could have evidence produced at the trial.

Mr Atkinson said he only wanted "a few words on the depositions before the case went before a Judge and Jury." He was in the hands of the bench, he said, and if they dissented he would have to get the questions put "through counsel at another place."

The bench dissented, and Mr Batchelor said that this was his case. None of the exchange went into Gould's deposition, which gives his cross-examination in a single line.

Before it closed, Mr Lister recalled Gould, who said he "was not aware if any means had been taken to restore animation in the three children. Dr. Twining had been at the house before him."

Then the charge was read to William, and he was addressed in the words printed on the form for the purpose. "Having heard the Evidence, do you wish to say anything in answer to the Charge? You are not obliged to say anything unless you desire to do so; but whatever you say will be taken down in Writing, and may be given in Evidence against you upon your Trial."

Beneath the printed line "Whereupon the said William Gouldstone saith as follows", the clerk wrote: "I reserve my defence." A footnote printed on the form told him to set down whatever the prisoner said "in his very words, as nearly as possible. Get him to sign it if he will." The space for William's signature was left empty, and Powell signed alone.

He was committed to take his trial at the Central Criminal Court. The Daily News reported the committal with "Mr. Atkinson saying he would reserve his defence." The Guardian reported Mr Atkinson "intimating that the accused would reserve his defence." At the foot of Gould's deposition someone added in ink that Inspector Craggs was bound over to prosecute.

Afterwards Miss Skinner visited him in his cell. She did good work in the neighbourhood for Mrs Pelly's Refuge Home, which the wife of the vicar of St John's, Stratford, had founded that year. The Times reported that he "manifested a repentant mood." The Morning Advertiser had more. He was "delighted and surprised" to see her, and said so. She spoke to him "from a spiritual point", and he joined her in prayer. He said that he knew every man's hand was against him, and that he had done that which deserved it, but that he took heart that the Lord had been merciful enough to allow him time to prepare for repentance. When the words "Lord have mercy upon me" came in her prayer, he sighed deeply and said, "Amen." Then she knocked at the cell door to be let out, and as soon as a constable appeared his manner "at once assumed that defiance which has been characteristic of him since the sad occurrence."

Outside, the large crowd had stayed, and "evidently were much incensed against him." The police thought of taking him over a back wall of the court and through a shop into the Broadway, and gave the idea up. A private brougham waited in the Grove. Its coachman had been told to draw up at the magistrates' entrance as soon as he saw a cab arrive at the prisoners' exit. A constable was sent for a cab with a great show of bustle and noise, and when it drew up at the prisoners' exit, round the corner of the building, the crowd went to it. At the same moment several constables rushed out of the court towards the Three Choppers coffee palace, the way he had been taken the week before, and the rest of the crowd went after them. The brougham drew up at the magistrates' entrance, men of the K and N divisions drew a cordon round it, and William was rushed inside. He seemed, to the Advertiser, "to enjoy the ruse to deceive the crowd."

When the crowd found it had been hoaxed, it gave chase, yelling and hooting. At Angell Lane the traffic was blocked, and the police feared the brougham would be overturned. A large part of the crowd ran to Stratford main station, but the brougham kept to the road and gradually left them all behind, though they hooted and groaned at it for a long time.

He was driven on to Clerkenwell.
