Chapter Four

THE EMPTYING

In the burial ground of the United Reformed Church at Wethersfield, the old Independent chapel, stands a family tomb: a pillar of polished red granite with an inscription on each of its four faces. The first is for Thomas Raven, the brewer in whose yard the Stocks had lived in 1861, who died March 23rd 1878, aged sixty-three years, and beneath him his wife, Elizabeth Victoria, wife of the above, who died July 12th 1901, aged sixty-nine. She had been a widow for twenty-three years. The Ravens were wealthy people. More than a century later, the village still remembered them that way.

Another face belongs to the women of the house: Jane Raven, who died in 1863, aged forty-seven; Charlotte Raven, who died in 1868; and Sarah Raven, who lived until 1897. Jane and Sarah were the sisters who had kept his house in 1861.

The children have a face of their own. Udea Elizabeth Jane, the eldest beloved child of Thomas and Elizabeth Victoria Raven, died April 23rd 1874, aged nine; Thomas Henry, their only son, died January 10th 1873, aged seven weeks. Two children buried within sixteen months of each other, on the richest monument in the village. The fourth face is for their second daughter, Charlotte Mary, who died at Edinburgh in the first days of 1900, aged twenty-eight. Beneath her name: "If love and care could death prevent, thy days had not so soon been spent."

Of their four children, only Edith, born in the year Udea died, outlived her mother, and there was no son to take on the brewery. The one family in these pages that never had to leave Wethersfield emptied all the same.

Wethersfield and Finchingfield could look settled from almost any angle. Both villages had done well out of the middle of the century: mills turning, breweries brewing, straw passing through women's fingers on its way to Luton and Dunstable, farms working land that had fed the county for generations. Both villages reached the highest population the census would ever record for them in the same year, 1851. Wethersfield, 1,770 souls. Finchingfield, 2,594. Neither would see those numbers again.

Whatever pulled people away did not do it quickly. It took twenty years to show itself on a census page, and then it seemed to happen all at once. Between 1871 and 1881, Wethersfield lost 281 people, the single largest change, in either direction, that the parish's records have shown across a hundred and sixty years of counting. Finchingfield lost 434 in the same ten years, the largest change in its own book. A few miles north, at Great Sampford, where a blacksmith named Thomas Gouldstone kept his forge and his beer licence both, the same decade took nearly a third of the village, the steepest fall of the three. By 1881 one house in six there stood empty. By 1901, one in five.

Some of what that loss looked like on the ground survives in the record. The 1871 census at Great Sampford found nine people living in sheds. The village had no public water supply at all through the whole of Thomas Gouldstone's working life there; every household drew its own from a shallow well or a spring, by hand, the way it always had, because nobody had ever built anything better. A village already losing its people was unlikely to be first in line for something better.

And the straw went still in women's hands. For most of the century, plaiting straw into long lengths had kept households solvent when the fields alone could not. Thomas Gouldstone's own sisters had done it: Lydia, twenty-one, and Ruth, sixteen, both recorded as straw plaiters in the family's Little Sampford household in 1851. In Finchingfield the trade had grown proper businesses around it: Joseph Linsell and Son, straw plait manufacturers; Josiah Simms, a plait dealer; Mary White, who made the hats themselves. From about 1870, cheap plait began arriving from Italy, then China, then Japan, worked by hands paid far less than an Essex woman's, and no cottager here could match the price. The trade eroded through the decade and collapsed outright between 1879 and 1881, the same years the census was recording empty houses. The hat manufacturers in Luton were buying the cheap imported plait and prospered through the same years the plaiters went without.

None of this happened as a single event a person could point to and name. A census records twenty years of change as one number, but nobody living through it experienced the decade in a day. It happened in smaller pieces than that, repeated across a hundred households in slightly different order: a daughter going into service in London and not coming home for Christmas; a son taking the train to a building site and writing once, then less; a cottage that had held a family for three generations standing shut through a whole winter because nobody in the parish could afford the rent, let alone anyone outside it. Ten years of erosion, and the enumerator arrives to find the number down by 281, and calls it a statistic.

Sometimes, though, the change was obvious.

On the fourteenth of April 1874, at Witham station, some two hundred working people from Essex and Suffolk boarded a train in a single morning to begin the journey to Queensland. Joseph Arch's National Agricultural Labourers' Union had chartered a ship, the St James, to carry three hundred labourers and their families out of the two counties: free passage, a pound a man, ten shillings a wife, five a child. Every station between Suffolk and Witham filled with people come to watch the train go by, women said to be waving flags from cottage doors the whole length of the line into Chelmsford, and at Witham itself, where the crowd was largest, the neighbours cheered them off. The union's own paper printed it afterwards as a day nobody there would forget. It was, at least, the loudest and most visible way a person could leave Essex that year: named, dated and celebrated.

The men who stayed fared no better that summer. The same union called its members out for higher wages, and the farmers locked them out in return. The dispute reached Halstead, the next market town over from Finchingfield: close enough that men from these villages would have known people caught up in it. By June the union was paying strike pay to 339 men across Essex. By the tenth of August the money had run out, and the men went back to work on the farmers' own terms. In a single summer the union that had promised to raise wages had shown exactly how little bargaining power an Essex labourer actually held.

Elizabeth Stock was not on that train. By 1874 she had been in service in London since at least 1871, one of the thousands who left the way almost everyone actually left: unphotographed, unreported, a servant among servants in somebody else's home.

William Gouldstone left later, and more of his leaving survives.

On the first of May 1873, when he was sixteen, his elder sister Emily gave birth in Great Sampford to a daughter, Mabel Frances. Emily was unmarried. The father's column on the certificate is empty, and Emily went to the registrar herself four weeks later and signed for the birth as the mother. Eight years on, the census would list Mabel as her mother's sister, a daughter of the house. Twenty years later, it listed her correctly, as a granddaughter.

By his late teens William was working with his eldest brother, Robert, as a blacksmith at Finchingfield, a few miles south of home. Robert would remember those years in court. There was an accident, and William was ruptured, and from then on he complained of his head, and complained of it more whenever the rupture troubled him. Smithing, as Robert said, is a very noisy trade.

In the autumn of 1878, at twenty-two, William went to London, to the Falkirk Ironworks in Upper Thames Street, where the foreman of his department was a man named William Graves, who had known him since the day he was born. He arrived a blacksmith. The firm made him a whitesmith.

Graves lived at 88 Frampton Park Road, in Hackney. By the following March, so did Elizabeth Stock.
