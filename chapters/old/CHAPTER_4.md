Chapter Four

THE EMPTYING

In Wethersfield churchyard stands a family tomb, marble, its west face turned to the path so that anyone walking to church reads it first. The inscription opens with Thomas Raven: died the 23rd of March 1878, aged sixty-three years. Beside him, his wife: Elizabeth Victoria, wife of the above, who died July 12th 1901, aged sixty-nine. She had been a widow for twenty-three years. He was the brewer at Hope Brewery, the largest concern in the village. Four of its buildings still stand today, part of a site later judged one of only three in Essex of national or regional importance. The Ravens were wealthy people. Everyone in Wethersfield who remembered them said so.

It is only on the reverse face, turned away from the path, that the tomb says anything else. There are the children: Udea Elizabeth Jane, the eldest beloved child of Thomas and Elizabeth Victoria Raven, died the 23rd of April 1874, aged nine; and Thomas Henry, their only son, died the 10th of January 1873, aged seven weeks. Two children buried within sixteen months of each other, on the richest headstone in the churchyard, facing away from the path.

Wethersfield and Finchingfield could look settled from almost any angle. Both villages had done well out of the middle of the century: mills turning, breweries brewing, straw passing through women's fingers on its way to Luton and Dunstable, farms working land that had fed the county for generations. Both villages reached the highest population the census would ever record for them in the same year, 1851. Wethersfield, 1,770 souls. Finchingfield, 2,594. Neither would see those numbers again.

Whatever pulled people away did not do it quickly. It took twenty years to show itself on a census page, and then it seemed to happen all at once. Between 1871 and 1881, Wethersfield lost 281 people, the single largest change, in either direction, that the parish's records have shown across a hundred and sixty years of counting. Finchingfield lost 434 in the same ten years, the largest change in its own book. A few miles south, at Great Sampford, where a blacksmith named Thomas Gouldstone kept his forge and his beer licence both, the same decade took nearly a third of the village, the steepest fall of the three. By 1881 one house in six there stood empty. By 1901, one in five.

Some of what that loss looked like on the ground survives in the record. The 1871 census at Great Sampford found nine people living in sheds. The village had no public water supply at all through the whole of Thomas Gouldstone's working life there; every household drew its own from a shallow well or a spring, by hand, the way it always had, because nobody had ever built anything better. A village already losing its people was unlikely to be first in line for something better. Wethersfield went without piped water into the 1930s.

And the straw went still in women's hands. For most of the century, plaiting straw into long lengths had kept households solvent when the fields alone could not. Thomas Gouldstone's own sisters had done it: Lydia, twenty-one, and Ruth, sixteen, both recorded as straw plaiters in the family's Little Sampford household in 1851. In Finchingfield the trade had grown proper businesses around it: Joseph Linsell and Son, straw plait manufacturers; Josiah Simms, a plait dealer; Mary White, who made the hats themselves. From about 1870, cheap plait began arriving from Italy, then China, then Japan, worked by hands paid far less than an Essex woman's, and no cottager here could match the price. The trade eroded through the decade and collapsed outright between 1879 and 1881, the same years the census was recording empty houses. The hat manufacturers in Luton were buying the cheap imported plait and prospered through the same years the plaiters went without.

None of this happened as a single event a person could point to and name. A census records twenty years of change as one number, but nobody living through it experienced the decade in a day. It happened in smaller pieces than that, repeated across a hundred households in slightly different order: a daughter going into service in London and not coming home for Christmas; a son taking the train to a building site and writing once, then less; a cottage that had held a family for three generations standing shut through a whole winter because nobody in the parish could afford the rent, let alone anyone outside it. Ten years of erosion, and the enumerator arrives to find the number down by two hundred and eighty-one, and calls it a statistic.

Sometimes though, the change was obvious.

In the spring of 1874 the agricultural labourers of East Anglia, organised under Joseph Arch's National Union, struck for higher wages, and the farmers locked them out in return. The dispute reached Halstead, five miles from Finchingfield and the next market town over: close enough that men from these villages would have known people caught up in it, and perhaps been among them. No record says which. By June the union was paying strike pay to three hundred and thirty-nine men across Essex. By the tenth of August the money had run out, and the men went back to work on the farmers' own terms. The union that had promised to raise wages had proved the opposite, in a single summer: exactly how little bargaining power an Essex labourer actually held.

A few weeks before that, at Witham station, a different kind of defeat had happened. The same union chartered a ship, the St James, to carry three hundred labourers and their families out of Essex and Suffolk to Queensland: free passage, a pound a man, ten shillings a wife, five a child. On the fourteenth of April every station between Suffolk and Witham filled with people come to watch the train go by, women said to be waving flags from cottage doors the whole length of the line into Chelmsford, and at Witham itself, where the crowd was largest, some two hundred working people boarded in one morning while their neighbours cheered them off. It was printed up afterward in the union's own paper as a day nobody there would forget. It was, at least, the loudest and most visible way a person could leave Essex that year: named, dated, celebrated, and still readable now.

Elizabeth Stock was not on that train. By 1874 she had already been gone from Wethersfield for some years, recorded since the 1871 census in domestic service in Stoke Newington, one of the thousands who left the way almost everyone actually left: unphotographed, unreported, her name entered nowhere but a census taker's own hand, a servant among servants in somebody else's home. Adrian Gray, writing of the Sampfords a generation later, gives the whole of it in eleven words: people were "leaving for the towns, especially London, as the ease of travel increased." He might as well have been writing about Wethersfield. He might as well have been writing about her.

She did not know that a blacksmith's family a few miles off had lived through the same thing: daughters sent out to plait straw that no longer paid, a trade dying in the hands that most needed it. Two villages, two collapses that were really one, pulling two people toward the same city without either of them knowing the other existed. They were not chosen by London. It simply had use for them, in a county that no longer did.
