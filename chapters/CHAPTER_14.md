Chapter Fourteen

WILLIAM, ARE YOU WELL?

He was placed at the bar on the Friday morning, before Mr Justice Day, and while the jury were being sworn he stood with his thumbs in the sleeveholes of his waistcoat. He had "looked carelessly round the Court", the Essex Weekly News reported, and he "stood calmly".

There were several indictments against him, and he was tried on one. The court's printed Proceedings named both the roads by which he had come there. He was "indicted for, and charged on the Coroner's Inquisition with, the wilful murder of Charles Gouldstone."

Mr Poland and Mr Montagu Williams prosecuted, on behalf of the Director of Public Prosecutions. Mr Grain led for the defence, with Mr Elliott, who on the Wednesday had asked for the trial to wait until the defence's witnesses could come.

Mr Poland opened for the Crown, and the Times reported him at length. Most of his speech told the jury what the witnesses would say. The circumstances had to be gone into, he said, because the jury needed to know the prisoner's conduct and demeanour at the time. Then he turned to what he called "the real issues involved in the case".

By the law of England, he told them, the wilful killing of a human being was murder, and it was for the person charged to show that he was not guilty of it. Every man was presumed in law to be of sound mind and responsible for his acts, and it was for the prisoner to prove that he was not.

William had known perfectly well what he was about, Poland said, and that the law made it a crime to take a human life, for he had said, "I shall be hung, and I am ready for the rope." Those words showed that he knew he was committing a crime. If he knew the nature and quality of what he was doing, and that it was a crime, he was answerable to the law for it.

Then the Crown called its witnesses. Ada Hamilton was first. She told the afternoon again from the bedroom, Elizabeth asking "What makes you home so early?" and then, through the locked door, "Come in; he is murdering my children." Cross-examined by Mr Grain, she said that after the twins were born he had seemed "absent, and far less cheerful than he had done before". She had heard his wife say that he had come home with a headache that came on suddenly, and she had seen him put his hand to his head a great deal.

After her came Emma Clarke, Cheeseman, Folkard, Wheatley, Gould and Honey. Emma, cross-examined, confirmed what she had told the coroner: that she had heard Mrs Gouldstone say "He has killed my babies", and that at that instant he had opened the bedroom door and said, "You can come in, it is all done." Cheeseman took William again from the kitchen floor to the cell. Folkard had examined the cistern that same night: six feet from the floor to its top, with fourteen inches of water in it. What William had said in the cell that first evening, Wheatley repeated, and Gould swore to the causes of death. Honey had seen the string tied loose round Charles's neck.

Wheatley's cross-examination was a single answer: "He said 'preying,' not 'playing.'" The Times printed "playing" the next morning.

The case for the prosecution was closed.

Mr Grain opened for the defence. This was, he told the jury, "a very solemn and awful inquiry", and he put the defence in a sentence: William had not been in a sane state of mind when he did it, and so was not responsible. The Crown's own evidence was enough to show it. A "kind and affectionate father" and a good husband, he argued, could not have done such a thing to his children, and in so short a time, unless his mind had already given way.

Witness after witness, he promised, would prove "hereditary insanity" on both sides of the family, and Dr Savage of Bethlem would tell them that William had inherited a tendency to a weak mind.

The Essex Weekly News had him go further, and speak of the verdict. He believed the jury would find it their duty, under a new Act of Parliament, to say that William had committed the murders but was insane at the time, and that he would then be "detained during her Majesty's pleasure".

When the defence began to call its witnesses, they were not ready. Mr Justice Day said that he was surprised to find the arrangements in the Central Criminal Court "of this great country" so bad that they would not be allowed in any other large town.

The first witness for the defence was the prisoner's father. Thomas Gouldstone told the court about his wife: the scarf, the knife he had taken from her more than once, the woman he had kept with her. Mr Poland cross-examined him. "I have seven children; the eldest is a daughter—the prisoner is the third—I believe that they are all right but him." His wife had never been in an asylum: "I keep her at home." It was about eighteen years since she had tried to strangle herself, and "she was not taken up for it; I had business troubles about 18 years ago; it was at that time that my wife tried to strangle herself."

His eldest son, Robert, had worked with William at Finchingfield, before William came to London, and remembered him complaining of his head after an accident had left him ruptured. "He wished himself dead at times; I have heard him say so."

He spoke of their mother too. At home, he said, "we used to have to hide the knives and razors from her because we were afraid she would make an end of herself."

Cross-examined, he said that it had been "a good many years ago" that William said he wished himself dead.

The eldest of Thomas's seven children, Emily, was in service in London and had not seen her brother often. "I knew he had been for five years at this firm in Thames Street, working there regularly every day and going home to his wife and children at Walthamstow."

One of the other sisters had married John Byford. On a Saturday afternoon William would call on him "and sit in a chair and play with his hat—if I spoke to him he would invariably answer me the wrong question and take three or four minutes to answer me". Cross-examined, Byford said: "I don't know where he was a few days before this occurred."

For the father's side, the defence called Charles Gouldstone of Epping, a first cousin of Thomas's. His son William, a soldier, had been taken to the Brentford Lunatic Asylum in April 1880 and had died there. Cross-examined, he said his son had been subject to fits: "I think sometimes they were only drink."

At Thaxted, Dr William Sunderland had attended William's mother at intervals for eight years, and in his opinion "she is in an unsound state of mind". Despondency was the form it took, and her sister, Mrs Andrews, suffered "from the same form of insanity, despondency." Cross-examined, he said that neither woman had been certified or confined as a lunatic. Both lived at home with their husbands.

Re-examined, he said: "She is now continually restless and thinks her husband will fail in business, and thinks they will be turned out into the streets, and it makes him move about from place to place."

At the Falkirk Ironworks in Upper Thames Street, William's foreman was William Graves, who had known him since he was born. He had noticed nothing in particular until the second of August, the day after the twins were born. "I then noticed something peculiar, and went from my end of the shop to where he worked and said to him, 'William, are you well, or have you got the toothache?'—he was rubbing his head and face—I asked him whether there was anything the matter with him—he said 'No, only my head is very queer.'"

On the Friday he gave him a job to do in the regular way. "I found he was regularly confused with the work I gave him, and I complained to him he did not get on so well as he had at other times; and he made no reply—he left on that Friday night—it was our beanfeast on Saturday, the next day; then there was Sunday and the Bank Holiday, when we did not work—he ought to have been there on the Tuesday; he was not, nor on the Wednesday."

Cross-examined, he said that William earned twenty-five shillings a week. At about five o'clock on the Friday, Graves had paid him his wages and made him a present of ten shillings, "according to the custom of the firm—he always thanked me for that." He did not know how William had spent the Saturday, the day of the firm's beanfeast. "I went to my own beanfeast; he went where he thought proper, as everybody else did; I cannot tell if he went to a beanfeast."

For some years William's mate at the works had been Charles Cakebread. William had talked of throwing himself in front of a train if he could kill himself instantaneously, and down the lift-hole "if he thought he would kill himself right out without lingering afterwards". He had not made a joke of it: "he spoke seriously." He had been very despondent at times, and when Cakebread asked if there was anything he could do for him, he had complained of pains in his head and asked Cakebread to knock his brains out with a hammer. "That I treated as a joke and took no more notice of." Cross-examined, Cakebread said that it had been about six months before, at work, and that William "seemed to have a determination as if he meant it as a reality".

His brother Bennett worked in the same shop. Cross-examined, Bennett said: "I do not know where he was on the Saturday, the day of the beanfeast; or on the Monday, Tuesday, or Wednesday; he was not at work—he did not tell me where he had been."

Another of the men, John Clark, had heard William on many occasions wish that he were dead, "and also that he wished some one would hit him on the head and kill him out of the way".

When the lift rope broke on the second floor, some months before, George Skelton had been in the lift with him. "I said 'You had better jump out'—we jumped out and I said to the prisoner 'Get a bit of string, we will tie it down to the old iron'—he says 'Never mind, it goes to the bottom; it will only kill me out of the way'—he said it very seriously."

To the manager, William Henry Westbrook, he had been "a quiet man, almost bordering on moroseness—he would go home by himself and come by himself, and never seemed to have a companion with him." Cross-examined, Westbrook said: "He was a very fair worker—the work did not require much intelligence; it is mere fitting up, work which has been fitted before."

For the firm itself, one of the partners, Howard John Kennard, said that he had noticed nothing about the prisoner himself. "The whole feeling of his fellow-workmen is that he is certainly insane."

The last witness was Dr George Henry Savage, physician at Bethlem Hospital. He had examined the prisoner, and "from the examination alone I should consider him rather weak in mind, as evidenced by a slowness of appreciation of questions concerning the crime of which he is accused; when speaking about it he seemed not to appreciate in any way the gravity of the charge—beyond that from my personal examination I can say nothing."

He had heard the evidence in court as well. "I have heard the evidence as to his being a steady workman, quiet, and fond of his wife and children, and the details of the killing of the children—from that I should think his mind was unsound at the time he committed the act." The symptoms William's fellow workmen had described, he said, were "frequently present with persons of unsound mind—I suppose they are to be found in persons of sound mind, but certainly not as a rule."

He was about to be asked what conclusion he had reached, after hearing the evidence of insanity on both sides of the family, when, according to the Times, Mr Justice Day said that this seemed to him to be the very question the jury had to decide. A doctor might give his medical evidence, but not draw the conclusion that was theirs to draw.

In answer to further questions, Savage spoke of the family. "The insanity on the mother's side having been proved, I should say the prisoner has a very great tendency to become insane, and if insanity, however remote, were proved on the male side, it would considerably increase the liability in the offspring." He told the court one thing more: the last patient at Bethlem to die there without being moved to Broadmoor had been a woman who had killed her whole family.

Mr Poland cross-examined him. Savage had seen the prisoner once, on the Thursday, the day before the trial, at the request of his solicitor, "for not more than a quarter or half an hour". He had known that he was to examine him as to the state of his mind, and that he was to be tried for murder. "I spoke about the gravity of his crime, that he had killed five children, but nothing about the trial—speaking alone from the conversation I had with him, and what I saw of him, I had no reason to doubt that he was a man of sound mind; or I might say rather to prove that he was a man of unsound mind; it was compatible with sanity or insanity."

From what he had seen of him, he would not certify him as a lunatic. "I would say that he was not suffering from any active form of mental disease; I could not say he was a lunatic; he did appreciate questions, although rather slowly—his conversation with me was that of a rational man—I should say he knew that the penalty for murder was death, but if he knew I do not think it would have influenced his actions."

The opinion he had given, that William's mind was unsound when he did it, "was formed from sitting in Court and hearing the evidence, and having seen him as well—I heard that he said that he had killed the children, and that he should be hanged for it; I judged from that that at the time he knew he was committing a crime for which he was liable to be sentenced to death."

"I believe that he knew he was killing the children," Savage said, "and that the penalty for that act was death."
