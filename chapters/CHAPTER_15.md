Chapter Fifteen

THE FORM OF THEIR VERDICT

The evidence for the defence was closed. William himself had not given evidence. In 1883 a prisoner could not: he was not put on oath at his own trial, because to swear him was thought a form of compulsion, and it would be another fifteen years before Parliament allowed it. Nor could Elizabeth be called, by either side. The old rule held that a husband and wife could not be witnesses for or against each other, and its few exceptions did not reach the killing of her children. The prisoner himself, though, could still address the court without being sworn. William could have spoken, but apart from his plea, no report has him say a word.

Mr Grain made his closing speech. The next morning's Times reported the speeches, and the summing-up that followed them. Grain's case rested on the man he described: well conducted, five years married, with the esteem of his employers, the confidence of the men he worked with and the love of his family. He asked the jury to accept that such a man could not have done this and been sane. There had been no want in the house. The only threats anyone had heard from him were against his own life, never against the children's. What had happened, he suggested, was "a sudden visitation of Providence, unhinging a brain", whether from the madness in the family, from ill health or from pain.

The Crown's evidence alone, he told them, would justify a finding that William was of unsound mind when he did it. Turning to the evidence he had called himself, he asked whether they could have any reasonable doubt of it.

Mr Poland replied for the Crown. The jury were not there to decide whether William was sane, he said, but whether at the time he knew what he was doing, and knew that it was against the law. Up to the eighth of August he had never shown any sign of insanity. A man in pain who said he wished he were dead was not mad for saying so; to think otherwise was, in the words the Illustrated Police News gave him, "ridiculous".

Against a sudden visitation, Poland set deliberation: the revolver William had thought of buying a week before and given up, the drink he had taken, by his own account, to nerve himself, and what he had said to the constable, "I am ready for the rope." If the jury took the brutality of what had been done as proof of madness, he said, it would be "a very dangerous precedent".

If they were satisfied that William was responsible for the deed, then, and only then, he would ask them for a verdict for the Crown.

Mr Justice Day then summed up. That William had killed his children, he said, was beyond all question. What the jury had to decide was whether the killing amounted to wilful murder. The killing of a human being was presumed to be murder, but the circumstances might show it to be something less: manslaughter, justifiable homicide, misadventure, or "the act of not a reasonable being". The only question for them was whether this had been the act of a reasonable being.

The law was for him to state, and they were bound to take it from him, incurring no responsibility of their own in doing so. If the prisoner, when he killed the children, "knew the nature and quality of the act he was committing, and knew that he was doing wrong, then he was guilty of wilful murder." By the nature and quality of the act he meant only that a man understood what he was doing: that he knew he was "killing a fellow creature".

The defence, he said, rested on one ground alone, that William had been of unsound mind, and Mr Grain had put it with the utmost skill. Going through the evidence called for it, Day remarked that "previous to the 8th of August no one would have said the prisoner was a man of unsound mind", and that, after what William had said himself, it was difficult to say he had not known what he was doing. But the matter was solely for them. He asked them to come to their verdict calmly and impartially, without thought of the consequences, according to their oaths and their consciences.

Last of all, he turned to an Act of Parliament. Counsel for the prosecution had handed it to him, an Act passed towards the end of the previous month, "of the existence of which he was not previously aware, as no notice had been given to him". It might affect the form of their verdict. Under what he called "the former and logical law", wilful murder required malice aforethought, which a person of unsound mind could not form. Such a person could not commit murder, and in that case a jury returned a verdict of "Not Guilty on the ground of insanity". The law had been altered. A jury that found the prisoner insane when he did it must now return a special verdict instead: that he had done it, but had been insane in doing it. But if they found that he had known the nature and quality of the act when he killed his children, and was of sound mind, their verdict was guilty, and the new Act changed nothing.

The Daily Telegraph's shorter report of the summing-up put the direction another way. The point the jury had to decide was "not whether the prisoner was sane or insane", but whether at the time he was in such a condition of mind as not to know that he was killing his children, or that he was doing wrong. That, Day told them, had been solemnly decided by the judges many years before. He directed them to the medical evidence, the Telegraph went on, and to one doctor's admission that he had no doubt William knew at the time that he was committing murder, and that death was the penalty.

The jury retired at twenty minutes past five. They were out for about a quarter of an hour, and when they came back into court the foreman gave their verdict: guilty of wilful murder.

Asked in the usual way whether he had anything to say why sentence of death should not be passed on him, William, according to the Times, said that he had not. The Essex Weekly News reported him differently, in a line of its own: "The Prisoner: I am not guilty."

Silence was proclaimed, and Mr Justice Day put on the black cap.

"William Gouldstone, you have been convicted upon overwhelming evidence of the crime of wilful murder. The jury could not consistently with their duty have found any other verdict in your case. It is my invariable rule never to comment upon the facts of a case where it is my painful duty to sentence a prisoner to such a painful death as that which awaits you. I wish to say nothing to add to your sufferings, but merely to beg of you, as a fellow man, to make good use of the short time which is allowed you by law to prepare for your last day. Seek mercy where mercy alone is certain to be found. Seek it at the feet of your Redeemer. I beseech you make good use of your time. Every facility and every help will be afforded you. Do, I beseech you again, make use of that short time which is allowed you."

He passed sentence of death in the usual form, and ordered that William be handed over to the Sheriff of Essex for the sentence of the law to be carried out. The Telegraph described the judge as "a good deal affected".

William was removed to the cells. The Times reported that he had kept "a calm and undisturbed demeanour" all through the trial, and the Essex Weekly News that he "appeared to be quite unconcerned" as he went.

That night the 8.12 train took him to Chelmsford, in the charge of a clerk from the office of Gepp and Sons, the Acting Under Sheriffs, and two warders from Springfield Gaol.

At Chelmsford he was put into a cab and driven straight to the prison. He was wearing a dark tweed suit. He was placed in the condemned cell.
