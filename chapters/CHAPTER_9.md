Chapter Nine

GONE TO GLORY

On the road to the police station, William talked. Cheeseman did not have to ask him anything.

"When I took my money last week I thought of buying a revolver to do it with, but I altered my mind, because I thought it would make too much noise. I had a hard job with the two biggest, but the other little bugger, I soon settled him. I thought it was getting too hot to have five bloody kids in about three years and a half."

And then: "Now I am happy. I thought it was time to put a stop to it."

Three or four minutes afterwards, Cheeseman wrote it down. He kept the note, and five weeks later at the Old Bailey he had it with him.

Inspector George Folkard was at the Lea Bridge Road station when they came in, about six o'clock. "This is a charge of murder, sir," Cheeseman told him. "This man has murdered his five children." Two of them were still alive.

"That is right, sir. I did it, and now I am happy. I did it like a man too."

He braced himself up as he said it, Folkard noticed. Folkard left him in custody and went to the house with Dr Gould, and William was put into a cell. Henry Wheatley, police constable 208 N, had charge of him, keeping watch from the passage outside. At about a quarter to seven, of his own accord, William spoke to him.

"I have had an extra drop of drink to accomplish the job; there is five of them gone to glory, and it is a good job."

Wheatley observed no traces of drink about him. At about half past seven William spoke again.

"I am sorry for my wife and parents, but the children will be better off in heaven, if there is such a place; it is better than leaving them to the mercy of the world."

At about eight o'clock Folkard came back from the house and charged him with the wilful murder of his three children, Charles, Herbert and Frederick, and with attempting to murder his two male infants. By then, it had been about two hours since William had last seen his wife.

"Quite right, sir. What condition is the missis in? I suppose some of you gentlemen have been round?"

Dr Gould was there. "Tolerable," he said.

"Are the two young ones dead?"

"No," Folkard told him.

Shortly after eight, while he was still at the station, Dr Gould received word that one of the infants had died, and went back to Courtenay Place.

At about nine o'clock William spoke to Wheatley for the third time.

"I wish I had killed the little ones out of the way; I don't know whether I hit them once or twice; I have had this preying on my mind for a long time."

He did not ramble or talk to himself. He was perfectly cool, Wheatley thought, and not showy in the least.

At half past eight the next morning, Thursday the ninth of August, Folkard went to him again and told him that he was now charged with the murder of all five of his children.

"Yes, sir. The other two are dead then?"

"Yes."
