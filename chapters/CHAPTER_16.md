Chapter Sixteen

AS HAPPY AS A CHILD AT PLAY

On the day of the trial, Dr Savage wrote to the Lancet from Bethlem Hospital. To him the case was one more instance of a court making poor use of a specialist, and he went through it for the journal's readers: the steady workman and family man, the pains in his head, the rupture, the talk at work of wanting to be dead, the mother who was insane and the aunt who was the same.

He added something the court had not heard. When he examined William on the Thursday, William had told him "that he had very bad dreams and had seen heaven opened and had heard the voice of God, but, he added, he knew it was only dreaming." That, Savage wrote, had not been brought out in evidence.

His complaint was with the judge, who had scoffed, he said, at the cousins on the father's side and "even threw doubt upon my bona fides". He had never been given the chance to say what he thought of William's sanity, so all the jury had from him was "the very bald statement that at the time I saw the man he was not a marked case of insanity". He finished: "If this man is hanged, I think it will be a scandal to law, or rather justice." The Lancet printed the letter eight days later.

The next morning the Times gave the case one of its leaders and backed the jury. Insanity was the only defence William had, and the paper judged the jury right to reject it. In its eyes there had been no delusion in him, then or ever. It pitied him, and it wanted him hanged: "we couple our pity with abhorrence". The medical evidence, it told its readers, had been an opinion that he "did not know what he was doing, and did not know that the penalty for the act was death". In the box, Savage had said: "I believe that he knew he was killing the children, and that the penalty for that act was death."

The Daily Telegraph wanted him examined first. It had no softer word for him than monster, but a crime with no motive behind it looked to the paper like madness, and Mr Justice Day's question, whether William knew what he was doing, seemed to it too narrow a test, since "a raving maniac" could know that much. Before the sentence was allowed to stand, it said, he should be seen by a specialist or two, and it suggested Dr Maudsley or Dr Bucknill.

One of the jurors answered it that day, writing to the Telegraph from Tottenham and signing himself One of the Jury. According to him, Savage had seen William once, on the Thursday, for twenty minutes to half an hour; when Mr Poland asked whether he would certify William insane, Savage "distinctly stated 'No'"; and Savage had said he believed William knew, when he did it, that it was murder.

The men William had worked beside wrote to the Telegraph too, the whole smiths' shop, through their foreman, William Graves. They had worked with him every day for four and a half years, and they had no doubt he was out of his mind. "We consider it a great hardship that we were not allowed to put this evidence before the jury."

Savage sent a second letter that Saturday, this time to the Times: "Justice demands further investigation of the case." He gave the paper's readers an account of the crime the court had not heard from him. A steady young man had become terrified that he and his family were heading for ruin, and had killed the children "to send them to heaven"; his mother and aunt had lived with the same dread. He passed on one thing more: the medical officer at the House of Detention had told him he thought William was suffering from melancholia. "I trust this prisoner will not be allowed to be hanged."

In the prison at Chelmsford, on the Saturday, William wrote to his wife. As the Essex Weekly News printed it, the letter read:

"My Dear Wife—I feel it is a Pleasure to Write a few Lines to you and trusting to God that it will find you as well as Can Be Expected after the Dreadful news but I am thankful to God that I feel Happey as I may Expect and I Hope you will Look to God to Help you and He Will Comfort you as well as myselfe Dearest Wife I Hope you will Recive this Letter this Evening and than I Hope you wont fret much about me as what God Doest is for the Best and I Hope you will Be able to Come and see Me Over a Short Time and I Hope Aunt and all at Home are quite Well and all Cousins and Rember me to all that know me, and Let Cousin Gouldstone in Mash-street know How I am and I am going to Send a few Lines to Mr. G. Sampford and I Hope all Will forgive me for I am thinking about you all But I Hope you all Will Pray to God to Comfort One and all of you and Bear that in mind that God Will forgive all Dear Wife do not fret about what I Have to Live Upon Because I Can Have What I Wish for in my Liveing from the Prison and you may Belive that the Chappeling will Give me all I Require and I thank God that I Have Been Preparing for the Other World and which I shell do more so now and Live in Hopes to meet you all In Heaven. So Good By God Bless you

"WILLIAM GOULDSTONE.
"Her Majesty's Prison, Chelmsford, Essex.
"Sept. 15, 1883."

The prison chaplain was the Rev. W. F. Lumley, and on the Sunday William was in his chapel. It was the sixteenth of the month, and the psalms appointed in the Prayer Book for that day began with the seventy-ninth. "Singularly enough," the Essex Weekly News noted, it contained the verse: "Oh let the sorrowful sighing of the prisoners come before thee; according to the greatness of Thy power preserve thou those that are appointed to die." It was the twelfth verse. The clergyman took the odd verses and the prisoners the even, so by the paper's reckoning the words fell to William and the men around him.

By the end of that week he was out of the dark tweed suit he had arrived in and into prison clothes, and his curls had been cut off. He was never left alone: one officer sat with him through the day and another through the night, and anything he asked for, he got. The same paper found him stolid and indifferent: he ate and drank heartily, it said, and "takes his rest with the felicity of a child".

The juror's and the workmen's letters appeared in the Telegraph on the Monday, and Savage's in the Times. Savage answered the juror before the day was out. His worry was that a misreading of his evidence could still go against William. He blamed Poland, whose questioning he called skilful, for keeping his real opinion out of the box. He had conceded one thing only: that one short meeting had not given him enough to put his name to a certificate of insanity. Had he offered to, he wrote, the hurry of it would have been turned against him. What he had said in court, he insisted, taking the interview together with the man's history and the crime, was that he had "no doubt that he was of unsound mind". He granted that William had known he killed his children and knew he might hang. To Savage that settled nothing about his sanity: "I have patients of the most insanely dangerous class here who have said the same things which Gouldstone said, and who know as much as he does. Yet they are mad."

Two more men wrote to the Times that Monday. William Tallack, of the Howard Association, did not dispute that William knew what he was doing, or that in law it was murder. But in 1864 the doctors of the asylums had resolved, without a dissenting voice, that the old test of right and wrong ignored what every one of them knew: that many people who were plainly insane could still tell the difference. That, together with the Home Office's own habit of stepping in, seemed to Tallack reason enough for the Home Secretary to think again. The difficulty, as he saw it, was the gallows: "A sentence to permanent detention would meet the claims of justice and science and public security, whether the man be insane or not." Dr W. Wood, physician to St Luke's Hospital, believed that "what he wanted was to compass his own death", and that William had chosen a madman's way of going about it.

Elizabeth went to Chelmsford on the Tuesday to see him, with his sister and his aunt. The High Sheriff of Essex, J. Oxley Parker, visited him the same day.

She wrote to the papers the next day. The fullest text to survive is the one the Essex Weekly News printed on the Friday.

"During our five years married life I frequently noticed that my husband was absent-minded and given to brooding. If he were asked a question he would not, no matter how simple it was, or however easy to answer, reply at once. He would think over it, and sometimes say something not concerning the question. We commenced housekeeping on 21s. a week, and latterly he had 25s. It was sometimes difficult to make ends meet when the children came so rapidly, and this weighed on his mind, I fear, although he never spoke of it to me—except in the way of saying how sorry he was that my hands were so full, and that we could not go out together as we used to do. That was the only way in which he ever spoke of the burden of the family. Before we had the first child, and right on till the birth of the second, we regularly went to Church. The second child was weakly, and the first was still only a baby, so that we could not leave the house together. For all his occasional absent ways he was very lively, and as I had all to do with the spending of the money, and did not let him know if there was any trouble about payments when there was a confinement or sickness in the house, I always found it very easy to turn his attention to something brighter. He was very kindly with me and the children, handy and willing if they were ailing, and he constantly helped me in household duties. He was a man who had few acquaintances except his own relatives and his fellow-workmen, and he was nearly always at home when not at work, except on the Sunday morning when he occasionally took a walk alone. But for hours together he would keep the children going with his concertina, which he was very fond of playing, while I was engaged in household duties. The children were very fond of him, and never tired listening to the concertina. If he seemed worried, amusing the children—at which he was very good—or a remark from me would at once change his mood. He was painfully aware of how easy it was for him to get worried, and he has often said to me that it was a good job he had not much to think of, as if he had he believed it would turn his brain. Nothing that ever he said or did before the loss of the little ones gave me the least fear that he would take his own life or that of anyone else. But shortly before my last babies were born he said to me that he believed he had not many weeks to live, which was a strange remark that I have often thought of since. He never complained, except that he did not know how I should manage with my hands already so full, and soon to be even fuller. But then lots of people manage on less, and we did very well, and when I put this before him he ceased to be moody and absent. I never knew till very lately that there was any insanity in the family. Knowing that now, and looking back on all that has happened, I am certain he was insane, and that the times when I found him sitting thinking lately he was distressing himself about our growing family. You see the eldest was 3½, bright and healthy, but the next, 2½, was sickly and could not walk, and the third, being only 16 months, I had practically two babies to mind when the twins were born. But for all this there was no change in his kindly ways to me and the children. He got up early in the morning of the day when the twins were born, and got me a cup of tea, as he often did before, and did every morning from their birth till their death. The thing that struck me most was that during all this time he never took any notice of the babies. They were born about half-past ten, and he immediately went out of the house, and did not return till after 12. He then came into the room to me, but did not speak. His appearance seemed changed, and his eyes were strange and wandering. He came home early on the day he attacked the poor things, and I asked him if he had told his foreman about the twins, as he had lived with him once, and we looked upon him as one of our own family, but he said he had not. Since the occurrence I have seen my husband three times at the House of Detention, once at the Old Bailey, and on Tuesday last in the Prison at Chelmsford. On each occasion he has been as happy as a child at play. At Chelmsford he said he had seen several relatives whom he had not met for a long time, but neither he nor I made any reference to the fate of the children. His sister and aunt accompanied me yesterday. He asked me to take care of myself, and, turning to his relatives, said, 'You will take care of her, won't you?' Then he said, 'I have broken God's commandment, and I must expect to suffer, but He will forgive me for what I have done.' He realises his position in so far as he believes he will die, but he seems to have no notion of the enormity of killing the children. In a letter I had from him he spoke of them as being in heaven, where he hopes soon to join them. Here is a letter I have from him, dated the 15th, in the same strain:—

[Here the paper printed his letter of the fifteenth.]

"[I] have no doubt he did not know and could not help what he did. He was always most affectionate to me and the children, and I cannot think that he should suffer a shameful death for what would be to him, if he only understood it, the most awful distress."

Grantham Robinson, the vicar of St Saviour's, saw her letter on the Thursday and wrote in support of it. He was not taking her word alone. He had asked about William for himself, and had spent a long time with him at the House of Detention, and he had come to the same conclusion: "I firmly believe that he was not in his right mind when he committed the fearful deed." To him the insanity in the family was proved, on his mother's side and his father's, and a jury that had properly heard it would, he believed, have reached another verdict. He had, he added, "not seen his wife since her husband's conviction". "We are getting up a petition in Walthamstow, praying for a reprieve."

The Law Times, the lawyers' journal, did not fault the jury, and on the Friday the Times reprinted what it said. Measured against M'Naghten's case, the test the judges had set out forty years before, William was guilty: he had known what he was doing and had said he was ready to pay for it. Its complaint was with the test. Knowing what you are doing, it argued, is not the same as being able to stop, and a man who kills for none of the usual reasons, with madness in his family, may be in the grip of an impulse he cannot resist. It urged Parliament to widen the law to take such a man into account, and it ended on a choice of remedies: "surely the prevention of a lunatic asylum is better suited to his case than the cure of the gallows."

The Leeds Mercury's London correspondent predicted a campaign to save him. Medical men, he reported, resented how little weight the court had given their evidence. A man condemned to death in England had no court to appeal to. In April the Attorney General, Sir Henry James, had brought a bill before the Commons to set one up, with an appeal as of right in capital cases, and it had come through committee, but on the twenty-first of August, with the session running out, Gladstone's government withdrew it. The correspondent blamed Parliament for that, and was certain this case would have gone to such a court if one had existed. As things stood, only the Home Secretary could reopen it.

Dr Sunderland's letter, from Thaxted, was in the Times on the Saturday. He had given evidence at the trial about William's mother. He was sure the jury had taken Savage's "No" to mean that Savage thought William sane. In fact, he went on, Savage's opinion had been the opposite. Signing a certificate of insanity meant satisfying the Lunacy Commissioners, who required hard facts as well as a firm opinion, and Savage had not had the facts. To the medical profession, he wrote, the killings had made William as plainly insane "as if he had sat in a corner for a month with a paper crown and called himself the Emperor of all the Russias". The verdict seemed to him to mean that, in law, a doctor would have had to witness them.

The British Medical Journal of that Saturday carried a long article of its own. It accepted that William had known he was killing his children and that he would hang for it. What it denied was that he had known it was wrong: he had been "as fully persuaded of the righteousness of his proceedings as is the lunatic who kills at the command of God." Mr Justice Day, in its view, was at fault for giving the jury only the strict test of 1843, when Mr Justice Stephen had lately argued that a man whom disease had robbed of control over his own conduct should not be held guilty of a crime. It called on the Home Secretary, Sir William Harcourt, to take specialist medical advice before deciding. A single meeting of a quarter to half an hour, it said of Savage's examination, was no foundation for a judgement on a man's mind.

The Walthamstow and Leyton Guardian, the local weekly, set out what the London papers had said about the verdict and counted them up. By its reckoning they were split evenly. The Times, the Daily Chronicle and the Morning Advertiser were for the sentence; the Advertiser expected that "the mad doctors will be called in". The Daily Telegraph, the Morning Post and the Daily News called for his state of mind to be further examined. The Morning Post granted that by the M'Naghten rule he was guilty beyond question, but expected public feeling to turn against hanging him. The Daily News, writing after the letters of the Monday, held that the workmen should have been heard, and found it "very singular" that the evidence of a doctor so sure of William's insanity had told so heavily against him.

The execution had been fixed for Monday the first of October, at Springfield Gaol. It fell to the High Sheriff of Essex to find a hangman, for Marwood, the executioner, was dead. Men had been writing to the gaol and to the High Sheriff asking for the job.

That Saturday the Daily News carried a line from the Central News agency: the Home Secretary "is about to cause a medical inquiry to be held into the mental condition of William Gouldstone".
