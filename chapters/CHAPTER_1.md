Chapter One

LATE ANSELL FORMERLY SUCKLING

On the thirtieth of March 1855, Thomas Stock registered his daughter's birth, and where he should have signed, he made his mark. Beside it the registrar for the Finchingfield district wrote "the mark of Thomas Stock, Father". The girl was Elizabeth, born six weeks before, on the eighteenth of February, at "The Village, Weathersfield", in Essex. Her father was entered as an agricultural labourer.

Her mother's name went down in full, three surnames long: "Jemima Stock, late Ansell formerly Suckling".

Suckling was the name Jemima was born with. She was baptised at Hornchurch, on the London side of the county, on the fifteenth of December 1811, the daughter of Edward and Sarah Suckling. Ansell was her first husband's. On Christmas Day 1833, at twenty-two, she married John Ansell at St Mary Magdalene, Wethersfield, and the parish clerk, Frederick Smee, was one of the witnesses. John was the son of a Wethersfield brewer, also John, and the couple began their married life in his father's house on Dog Chase.

The children came steadily. When the census was taken in June 1841 there were six of them under the brewer's roof: John Nathan, seven; the twins, Sarah and Eliza, six, whose births had gone into the register of the Independent chapel under a single date, the fourth of August 1835; Alfred, three; George, two; and Emma, eight months. When the parish was surveyed for its tithes the following year, the brewer was the occupier of a cottage and, with two other men, of a row of tenements, both in the same lane. Arthur followed in the summer of 1843, and was baptised at St Mary Magdalene on the ninth of July.

Then, across a little over three years, Jemima buried nearly all of it. Sarah died on the twenty-ninth of February 1844, aged eight. Eight weeks later Arthur died, at ten months, of influenza. Their father died on the fifteenth of April 1845, thirty-two years old. Eliza died that August, aged ten, and was entered in the burial register under her full name, Elizabeth. Alfred died the following May, aged eight, after six months of consumption. A daughter, Edith, born after her father's death, was dead by the February of 1847, not yet two.

The certificates give the cause, and for the most part it is the same one. Sarah, her father, Eliza and Edith died of decline, the everyday word for consumption, and Alfred's certificate used that word itself. Consumption is what is now called tuberculosis: a slow wasting of the lungs that passed easily from one person to the next in a crowded house, and could take years to work its way through a family. Four of them died at Hudson's Hill, where the High Street runs out of the village into the fields. Five times the woman in attendance at the end was Elizabeth Carter, who went to the registrar afterwards and made her mark.

By the census of 1851 Jemima was a widow and a straw plaiter, back on Dog Chase, and the household had turned round. Her father-in-law, the brewer of ten years before, was living in her house now, a widower and an agricultural labourer. Only three of her children remained: John Nathan, sixteen, an errand boy, and George and Emma, eleven and ten, at school.

Plaiting meant splitting the straw and plaiting it into long strips, to be sold on and made up into hats and bonnets. At Finchingfield, her parents' village, there was a trade built on it: White's directory of 1863 lists Joseph Linsell and Son, straw plait manufacturers, a plait dealer and a straw hat maker there.

She married again on the sixteenth of June 1854, after banns, at the church where she had buried five of her children. She was forty-two. Her new husband, Thomas Stock, an agricultural labourer and a widower, had been baptised at Finchingfield in 1802. Both made their marks. The witnesses were Jemima's brother, Robert Suckling, and the parish clerk, Frederick Smee, who had signed the register at her first wedding twenty years before.

The following February, at forty-three, Jemima had a daughter, and they named her Elizabeth.

She was seventeen months old when the eldest of her mother's first family died. John Nathan had had phthisis, the doctors' word for consumption, for eight months. He was twenty-two, and on the nineteenth of July 1856 he was buried under the name Nathan alone.

Three months later, a few miles to the north at Great Sampford, the blacksmith Thomas Gouldstone and his wife, Emily, had a son. Thomas had married Emily Willett, a Wimbish girl, in January 1851. Eight months before, at Saffron Walden, she had stood witness at her sister Ann's wedding and signed the register. William, their third child, was born on the tenth of October 1856. Emily was ill in her mind when he was born. She registered the birth herself, on the third of November, and signed her own name.

Thomas was a smith's son, and the Gouldstones had been in the village for at least four generations. When the parish was surveyed for its tithes in 1842, his father, Joseph, had the plot beside a smithy near the Bull Inn. By the spring of 1851 Thomas was working as a smith on his own account, and on the census page his household comes between a journeyman miller's and a blacksmith's. His sisters, Lydia and Ruth, were straw plaiters.

In March 1857 Joseph was a witness at the Lent Assizes at Chelmsford. The question before the jury in the Nisi Prius court, before Mr Baron Channell, was whether Henry Hibblethwaite, a farmer, had been of sound mind when he made his will. Joseph, the Chelmsford Chronicle reported, "excited much amusement by replying to the counsel as 'My learned counsel,' which Mr. James returned by addressing him as 'My learned blacksmith.'"

"I am 65, and knew Henry Hibblethwaite perfectly well; he was a man of weak intellect, and knew nothing and no business whatever; did not play with him as a boy, as I did not like to play with such a weak intellect, I knew myself better; he brought things to my shop to be repaired, but he never could explain what they were to be done to."

Cross-examined, he said the boys had called Hibblethwaite "Five-clawed", because of a chicken he claimed had five claws on one leg. He could not tell a Dorking from a Cochin-China, but he did "know a cock from a hen by the comb", and was "not an ornithologist as well as a learned blacksmith". The court laughed.

The next witness was Thomas. "I knew the testator 20 years; the Christmas before he died he came to me and asked me if I was not going to give him a Christmas-box; I said it was not a common thing for farmers to come to tradesmen for Christmas-boxes; he laughed; I asked him, and he had a cup of elder-wine; the year before that he asked for a Christmas-box, and I gave him sixpence."

In less than five minutes the jury found for the defendant, "and said they thought the testator was in a sound state of mind when the will was executed."

At Wethersfield, in the summer of 1858, George Ansell died of typhus fever, aged nineteen. He was buried on the eleventh of July. Elizabeth was three. Of the eight children Jemima had borne John Ansell, only Emma was left.
