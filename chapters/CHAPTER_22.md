Chapter Twenty-Two

THE LENGTH OF A ROPE

William Gouldstone died in the infirmary at Broadmoor at five minutes past midnight on Saturday the nineteenth of January 1935. He was seventy-eight. On the Monday a coroner for Berkshire, Robert Sidney Payne, held an inquest at the asylum with a jury of eight. The first witness was the assistant medical officer, Kenneth Milne. In his deposition he began to write the name as "Goldstone", struck it through and wrote "Gouldstone". He said on oath that William had been admitted on the fifteenth of October 1883 from Chelmsford Prison, "a married man & a blacksmith by occupation", that he had been tried for wilful murder, found guilty and sentenced to death, and that the sentence had been "respited". The examination after death found hypostatic pneumonia following myocardial degeneration, and the jury returned a verdict of death from natural causes.

The argument over minds like his had begun before anyone outside Walthamstow knew his name.

***

When Mr Justice Day told William's jury that he was guilty of wilful murder if he had known what he was doing and known that it was wrong, he was using words the judges had settled in 1843. For most of the forty years since, the doctors who ran the country's asylums had been saying that those words asked the wrong question. A man could know exactly what he was doing, they argued, and still be mad.

Seven weeks before William's trial, on Friday the twenty-seventh of July 1883, they said so again, at the annual meeting of their association in the Royal College of Physicians in London. The president that year was Dr William Orange, superintendent of Broadmoor, the criminal lunatic asylum in Berkshire. He opened the afternoon by reading the apologies of those who could not come. The first was from the Home Secretary, Sir William Harcourt.

Orange's own concern was the order in which things were done. Many of the patients who came to him from the prisons, he told the meeting, had been insane when they were sentenced. He was speaking of men sent to penal servitude, not to the gallows, but the remedy he proposed was plain: in doubtful cases it would be better "not to pass sentence until after a satisfactory examination of the mental condition of the prisoner had been made."

Dr Daniel Hack Tuke, whose disciple Orange said he was, doubted that the judges would bend their rule. His fear was that a man whose disease had taken away his control of himself could still, in a judge's eyes, know right from wrong, and be punished for it.

The meeting voted for what Orange proposed. A prisoner suspected of insanity should be examined as soon as possible after his crime, by three doctors, and the cost should fall on the public purse, "inasmuch as it is useless to tell an insane man that the burden of proving himself insane lies upon himself." Tuke asked who would appoint the doctors. The Treasury's solicitors, said Orange, or the Public Prosecutor; something of the kind was already done "in the more notorious cases". Then the members went to dine at the Ship, at Greenwich.

Twelve days later William killed his children. When he was tried, the one specialist in madness the jury heard was Dr George Savage of Bethlem Hospital, who had been at the July meeting. He had seen William for half an hour the day before, and he agreed, under cross-examination, that William knew he was killing the children and knew that the penalty was death. The jury took a quarter of an hour. The examination came after the sentence, at Harcourt's order, and Orange was one of the two doctors who made it.

Three days after William reached Broadmoor, another father was condemned at the Old Bailey. James Cole, a labourer from West Croydon, had killed his small son, believing that his wife had hidden people under the floor to poison him. In passing sentence, the judge, Mr Justice Denman, accepted that Cole had been suffering from delusions, and still told him: "I cannot entertain a doubt that on the occasion on which you violently caused the death of your child, you knew you were doing wrong". The British Medical Journal's leader began with William: "The echoes of the Gouldstone case have scarcely died away, when the necessity arises for another loud protest against the manner in which trials for murder, in which the defence of insanity is set up, are conducted in this country."

The Times had taken the other side. Its leader the day after Cole's trial mocked the "medical theorists" and their "*quasi*-scientific terminology or jargon", praised the plain men of the jury box who would not believe that a man must be mad because his crime was monstrous, and then turned to William. A jury, it said, had found the evidence of his "mental weakness" too vague to act on, "But for reasons satisfactory to the Home Secretary and his advisers, the jury were overruled, and Gouldstone was sent to Broadmoor." In the last resort, the paper went on, "the Home Secretary is the final Court of Appeal as to these questions", and it was not a satisfactory court. The Journal of Mental Science reprinted the leader that winter, as a "discreditable article", so that it should be on record.

***

On the twenty-fourth of October, nine days after William reached Broadmoor, Savage read a paper on his case to the South London doctors of the British Medical Association, at St Thomas's Hospital. Dr Nicolson spoke after him. He described William's present condition in the asylum and agreed with Savage: no doubt he was insane. Tuke followed with Orange's resolution of July, almost word for word. Nothing, he said, could be more absurd than that the principal medical witness should have examined the prisoner at the eleventh hour, for twenty minutes. What had been done after the trial should have been done before.

Not every doctor in the room agreed. Dr John Bucknill, who had once been superintendent of a county asylum, would not support the resolution. He sympathised with Savage, but he did not think William's mad relatives proved anything about William: if they were the cause, all his brothers and sisters ought to have been insane too. He had hoped Nicolson would give some facts about the man as he was now, and Nicolson had given none. And he wanted to see what Orange had reported to the Home Secretary. Reports like that, he said, ought to be published. The meeting carried Tuke's resolution anyway.

In January 1884 Savage gave his own account in the Association's journal. He had been asked in court whether William knew he had killed his children and whether he knew the punishment, and he had answered yes, because it was true. He had added that he believed William insane when he did it, and had been told by the judge that this was for the jury. "Surely the jury have a right to be instructed by experts as well as by lawyers," he wrote. In his view the skill of the prosecuting counsel and the ruling of the judge had made his opinion appear to be the opposite of what it was.

Tuke wrote alongside him about Cole, and about both trials. It could not help the respect owed to the courts, he wrote, to go on "convicting and sentencing lunatics to the gallows, and then reprieving them—a game which may be all very well for cats and mice". He pointed out that the person who knew most about Cole's madness, his wife, could not give evidence at his trial. The law was the same at William's. Elizabeth had watched her husband for five years, and nobody could put her on oath.

Bucknill set out his own case at the London Institution at the end of February, in a lecture printed by the British Medical Journal in two parts. The first was about the law. He quoted the words Mr Justice Day had used to William's jury, and the words Mr Justice Denman had used to Cole, as proof that the rule of 1843 was being applied "in its most unconditional simplicity". He wanted it replaced by one plain test: no act should be a crime if the person who did it was at the time "incapable of not doing it" because of disease of the mind.

The second part was about what happened after the verdict, and here William's case was his example. Savage had written to the Times about Gouldstone, and a Croydon doctor about Cole. The newspapers had taken it up, and the Home Secretary had been moved to send doctors. Had either man been tried in some remote county, without eminent doctors to speak for him, Bucknill thought he would have been hanged. The reprieves were, "to a great extent, accidental."

The doctors the Home Secretary sent, Bucknill went on, did not simply advise on mercy. They looked again at the whole case: the crime, the evidence, how well the man had been defended, even the words in which the judge had put the law to the jury. "And these terms may vary greatly, even to the length of a rope." In effect the Home Secretary and his two doctors were "an informal court of appeal, or an appeal which is not a court", and it was "monstrous" that their reports were kept secret. He wanted examiners on oath, in public, open to cross-examination, under a judge.

Five months later Parliament wrote the secret procedure into law. The Criminal Lunatics Act of August 1884 provided that where there was reason to believe a prisoner under sentence of death to be insane, the Secretary of State "shall appoint two or more legally qualified medical practitioners" to examine him and report in writing. The Act said nothing about oaths, cross-examination or publication. What Harcourt had chosen to do for William became what every Home Secretary must do in such a case.

The argument did not stop. Ten years later, in August 1894, the British Medical Association met at Bristol and spent a session on the insanity rule. Charles Mercier, a lecturer on insanity at the Westminster Hospital, used William's trial to show how differently judges applied it: Mr Justice Day had let William's fellow workmen tell the jury they believed him mad, and had admitted evidence of madness in his family, while another judge had refused to let any doctor give an opinion at all. Mercier also described, without naming William, what happened next in such cases. A man was convicted, sentenced to death and then examined by two doctors in private, so that the case was "tried by an unsworn jury of two, without a judge, without counsel, without witnesses". He called it "this amateur court". The meeting resolved that the law was not in accord with modern mental science, and named a committee to press for change. Orange and Savage were both on it.

In January 1895 Charles Cullingworth, a physician at St Thomas's Hospital, took William and Cole as his examples in a paper to the St Thomas's Hospital society. Both men, in his opinion, had been "undoubtedly insane"; both had been convicted under a test they could not meet, and saved only by "a secret and informal inquiry" after sentence. He had once given evidence at a trial in which the prisoner was convicted and hanged, and he had read how the man went to the scaffold "with the surprised look of a man awaking from a dream". His remedy was the doctors' remedy of 1883, made sharper: two independent physicians appointed by the state, examining before the trial, giving their evidence on oath in open court, as the Home Office's own analysts already did in cases of poisoning.

***

When William was tried there had been no court in England to which a man condemned to death could appeal. A bill to create one had been withdrawn three weeks before his trial. The Criminal Appeal Act passed in August 1907, by which time William had been in Broadmoor for nearly twenty-four years. The new court could even find, on appeal, that a man had done the act but been insane, as though the jury had returned the special verdict. But the Act left the Home Secretary's mercy where it was, and with it the inquiry into condemned men's minds.

In March 1922 a former airman named Ronald True killed Gertrude Yates in London. He was tried at the Old Bailey from the first of May, found guilty on the fifth and sentenced to death. On the twenty-fifth the new Court of Criminal Appeal dismissed his appeal. The Home Secretary, Edward Shortt, then appointed doctors under the Act of 1884, and on the eighth of June, as the printed record of his trial puts it, the "Sentence respited on the ground of insanity. True removed to Broadmoor."

There was an outcry, and on the thirteenth Shortt defended himself in the House of Commons. He read out the section. It was, he said, "peremptory in its terms". The jury had answered one question, whether True was responsible when he killed; the doctors had answered another, whether he was insane now. They were "two issues which are quite distinct". One of the doctors, he told the House, "had been for many years superintendent of Broadmoor Asylum". Once they had certified True insane, "I was bound by the law of the land to reprieve him."

What Harcourt had done in 1883 by choice, Shortt said he had done by law. True went to Broadmoor, where William had been for thirty-nine years, and he was still there nine years later.

A month after True's reprieve the Lord Chancellor appointed a committee under Lord Justice Atkin to consider the insanity defence and the procedure under the Act of 1884. It reported in November 1923. It proposed one change to the rule of 1843: that a man should not be held responsible for an act committed under an impulse which mental disease had "in substance deprived" him of any power to resist. That never became law. On the procedure, it took the opposite side from Bucknill, Mercier and Cullingworth. That the Home Secretary's inquiry might reverse the finding of a jury was, in the committee's words, "So far from this being objectionable we think it essential". The procedure was "satisfactory and does not require amendment".

The committee found support for that in the doctors' own association. In 1896, it said, a committee of the Medico-Psychological Association had looked into the same question and reported that "care is taken that justice should be done". The report of 1923 closed its argument with the doctors' phrase: "In our opinion, in 1923, as in 1896, 'to the insane person justice is done.'"

***

Outside the argument, the record of William's own years in Broadmoor is thin.

The census found him there four times. In April 1891 the clerk entered him in full, William Gouldstone, thirty-four, married, a blacksmith, born at "Great Sanford" in Essex. In 1901 and 1911 every patient on the page was entered by initials only, and he was "W. G.", still married, still a blacksmith. In June 1921 he was William Gouldstone again, sixty-four, married. Elizabeth had died at Whitstable in June 1906.

The same men appear around him, page after page. Thomas Luck, a commercial clerk from Weston Favell in Northamptonshire, and Henry White, a hawker and former soldier from Bethersden in Kent, are listed with him in all four returns. Whether they ever spoke to one another, no record says.

From 1884 the law required the Home Secretary to consider his case at least once every three years and to decide whether he should be discharged.

In fifty-one years the open record gives his mind a single line, in Milne's deposition: "on admission he was suffering from confusional insanity & later became demented."

When Milne gave his evidence in 1935 he called William "a married man". The typed slip among the coroner's papers says "Widower". That Monday the Evening Standard reported the death under the heading "Death After 52 Years in Broadmoor". It said that he "had been described as an ideal patient. He worked in the various wards."

In the register of criminal lunatics, William's admission had been entered in October 1883 against number 4953. The page listed patients sent to asylums across the country, and nearly every other entry on it was closed within ten years.

William's remained open.

Someone returned to the register after his death and entered the date, 19.1.35, in a different hand from the original entry.

The legal test applied by Mr Justice Day in September 1883 was still in force.

He was never discharged.
