Chapter Twenty

INVALIDED

Three months after the wedding Albert left the barracks at Chatham for HMS Dominion, a first-class battleship of the Second Division of the Home Fleet. He joined her on the seventh of June 1910, and stayed for almost a year.

The Dominion was lying in Glengariff Harbour, Ireland, with seven hundred and sixty-nine people on board, when the census was taken at midnight on Sunday the second of April 1911. Her captain, Morgan Singer, signed the totals. The census gives Albert as "Albert Goldstone", "24", "Married", "aB", born at "Hukening, Honyllon, Germany".

He went back to the barracks in May. On the eighth of August he was sent to HMS Crescent, a cruiser that was taking out relief crews for ships on the Australia and China stations. Two days later she came round from Portsmouth to Plymouth, and that evening she sailed for Malta, on her way to Colombo. By way of Malta, Port Said and Suez she reached Aden, and left it on the fourth of September.

Four cruisers of the Australian station, the Cambrian, Psyche, Pyramus and Pioneer, were waiting for her, and two of them were to take the men she carried. They had come up together from Sydney to pay off at Colombo and take on new crews, and on Sunday the third of September they were sighted at about noon and steamed into the harbour in line, flying the paying-off pennant. The Times of Ceylon reported that "thousands of residents along the sea shore turned out of their bungalows to watch the stately procession of the ironclads approaching the port."

The Crescent came in shortly after four o'clock on the afternoon of the twelfth. She had been in touch by wireless with the Cambrian, the largest of the four, for three days, and she took her place beside them along the south-west arm of the breakwater, with ten warships now in the harbour.

The ships were recommissioned on Friday the fifteenth, and the crews changed over. Albert went into the Pioneer, a third-class cruiser, and on Sunday the seventeenth she sailed for Australia with the Cambrian and the Psyche. He would not see England again for eighteen months.

In the new year the Pioneer crossed to New Zealand as the drillship of the Royal Naval Reserve there, and she spent the southern summer working her way along the South Island: the sounds of the west coast, Bluff, and then Timaru, where she came in on the night of the thirteenth of February. At the start of March she lay at Lyttelton, where she was to stay until after Easter, training the men of the reserve.

The Himitangi, a steamer of the Chatham Island Fishing Company, had left Lyttelton for the Chatham Islands on the thirty-first of January, and by March nothing had been heard of her. Her owners asked the Prime Minister, Sir Joseph Ward, to have the Pioneer sent to look for her. She had wireless, and in the words of the Lyttelton Times "her exceptionally long radius (about 2000 miles) will enable her to keep in constant touch with passing vessels, and also with the Wellington post office." On Monday the fourth of March she took on three hundred and fifty tons of Westport coal, and at two o'clock the next afternoon she left her moorings at Gladstone Pier and sailed under easy steam for the Chathams, four hundred and ninety miles away.

She never reached them. The Gertie, a steamer already out on the same search, came back to Wellington on the Wednesday with the news that the Himitangi was aground at the Chathams, undamaged, with her crew still aboard. The Pioneer had been in touch with the wireless station at the General Post Office in Wellington all day, and that night, in mid-ocean, a message reached Commander Stopford calling off the search. She turned back, and on the Friday morning she was berthed at Gladstone Pier again.

Albert left the Pioneer in May for HMS Drake, the new flagship of the Australian squadron. Each month the naval authorities sent the Navy League at Wellington a statement of where the ships were and where they were going, "for the information of friends and relatives of men aboard the different ships", and the Lyttelton Times printed it. The statement for July had the Drake leaving Sydney for Noumea, Vila, Suva and Auckland. She sailed on the fifth with the Admiral aboard, for six weeks among the islands, and came in to Auckland from Suva at a quarter past six on the morning of the twenty-fourth. In August she lay in Moreton Bay, in Queensland, with the Cambrian and the Psyche, the two cruisers that had sailed with the Pioneer from Colombo.

A cable from Sydney announced in September that the Drake would go home in January. A new crew was waiting in England, and the Admiralty meant to pick from it the men for the Commonwealth's new cruiser, the Australia. At the end of the year she was in New Zealand waters again. On the third of December 1912, a year after his first good conduct badge, he passed the educational examination for petty officer.

She left Sydney on New Year's Day 1913, a holiday, and thousands came down to the foreshores around Farm Cove to see her go. At ten minutes past ten she slipped her moorings, with her band playing and the crowd on shore cheering, and her seamen returned the cheers of the men in the Psyche and the Pyramus. The Naval and Military Record's correspondent wrote: "Amidst the strains of 'Home, sweet home,' 'Auld lang syne,' and 'The girl I left behind me,' the Drake shaped her course for the Heads." She carried about a hundred and forty men as a draft for the new Australian ships.

Fremantle was her last Australian port, and she sailed from it on the fourteenth of January. The heat on the run across the Indian Ocean was very great, and according to the Ceylon Observer "several stokers fainted." She came into Colombo at half past nine on the morning of the thirtieth, flying the homeward-bound pennant, and on the twenty-first of February she reached Plymouth and went on to Portsmouth to pay off. Five days later Albert was back in the barracks at Chatham.

He qualified there as a seaman gunner on the fourteenth of October 1913, and a fortnight later he was sent to a torpedo boat. The sheet gives her as "Tyne (T.B.31)": torpedo boat No. 31, whose men were carried on the books of HMS Tyne. On the fourteenth of December Clarie gave birth to their daughter, Lily Alma Rose, and in January he was given his second good conduct badge.

The boat was at Parkeston, by Harwich, in the spring of 1914. Britain went to war on the fourth of August, and by the end of the month she was in the Firth of Forth. At a quarter past nine on the night of the twenty-ninth she went aground on Iron Craig, off Inchkeith. Boats came out to her from the other torpedo boats and the trawlers, anchors and lines were laid out to steady her, and searchlights were kept on her all night. At five in the morning the battle cruisers New Zealand and Invincible came in from the battle in the Heligoland Bight two days before. Two of her torpedoes were taken off, and at ten o'clock, just after high water, tugs towed her off and took her to Leith.

The Mayoress of Exeter sent the boat a present of warm clothing that autumn, and on the twenty-sixth of October one of her officers, Harry M. L. Scott, wrote to thank her. The Western Times printed his letter. A good proportion of the boat's company, he wrote, knew "from former experience what winter in the North Sea means to those serving in torpedo craft, even in peace time, when the work is naturally somewhat less arduous than at present." He sent her "the very best wishes of all '31's'".

One of Albert's good conduct badges was taken from him on the fifteenth of October 1914 and given back the following April, and at the end of 1914 his character, "V.G." every year since 1910, was marked "G".

His twelve years ran out on the nineteenth of June 1916. Under the King's proclamation of 1914 he was kept on, and paid the gratuity of three pounds ten shillings for clothing and bedding that went to every man so detained. Two nights later the boat was out on night patrol in the Firth with Nos. 32 and 35, and on the first of July he was rated leading seaman. At the end of the month he left her.

A little over three months in the barracks followed, in which he passed the professional examination for petty officer, and on the fourth of November 1916 Albert joined HMS Courageous, a cruiser carrying fifteen-inch guns. One of his badges was taken away again on the last day of May 1917, and on the first of November he was made acting gunlayer, second class.

The Courageous put to sea a fortnight later, flying the flag of Admiral Napier, with the cruisers, the battle cruisers and a squadron of battleships, and early on the morning of the seventeenth of November they were nearing the German minefields in the Heligoland Bight. At half past seven, according to the official history of the war at sea, "The look-out men in the Courageous were the first to sight the enemy": German minesweepers and destroyers at work on the minefield, with four light cruisers in support. Seven minutes later the Courageous opened fire with her fifteen-inch guns. The Germans covered themselves with smoke and drew the British after them into the minefields, and for two hours the cruisers chased them through the smoke, firing whenever they could see them. At half past nine Admiral Napier, at what he took to be the limit of safe water, turned away, and "from this moment the Courageous and Glorious were out of the action." She was Albert's ship until the end of the year, and his character for 1917 was marked "Good".

Two months later he was in a very different ship. HMS Alexander was an armed icebreaker, and in the spring of 1918 she helped two ships of food, sent out from England, through the ice to Archangel, on the White Sea. The food was to be bartered for the Allied war stores at Archangel, to keep them out of German hands, but the government in Moscow would not let the stores go, and by June most of them had been sent away inland by rail. The Alexander stayed to protect the food from raids, and the Soviet resented her being there.

The official history records that early in July there was "an ugly demonstration against the Alexander by all the armed vessels in the port, and even field-guns ashore", and that it was the "cool and firm conduct" of her captain, Henry Hurt, that averted the danger. Rear-Admiral Kemp, who reached Archangel two days later, sent her away to Murmansk.

He stayed in the Alexander for the rest of the war, and in September 1918, with his lost badge restored, he was made a petty officer. On the twenty-first of October Clarie gave birth to their second daughter, Clarie Irene. Three weeks later the war was over.

He came back to the barracks at Chatham in January 1919, for what would be his last weeks in the Navy. On the twenty-sixth of March he was discharged, and the last column of his service sheet gives the reason: "Invalided", and beneath it, "Org Heart Disease".
