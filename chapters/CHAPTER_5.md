Chapter Five

THE HOUSEHOLD

In the spring of 1871 the census found Elizabeth Stock at 115 Albion Road, Stoke Newington, in the household of George Horace David Chilton, a solicitor. She was recorded as a general servant, aged eighteen. She was sixteen. Most girls from her sort of background went to their first place at twelve or thirteen, often somewhere close to home, and moved on from there. She was in a stranger's house, a long way from the village she was born in, with both her parents back in Essex.

It was not a large household. George Chilton was thirty-five and London-born. His wife, Isabella, had been a governess herself before her marriage, living in somebody else's house. There were three boys: Guy, five, Stanley, four, and Reginald, not yet a year old. There were two servants to run all of it.

The second was Susan Newman, recorded as a nursemaid, sixteen, born in Wethersfield. She was the girl from two doors down from the Red Lion, whose widowed mother had gone into service, and who had been at school in the village in the same year as Elizabeth. Places like these were usually found by recommendation, one mistress asking another, or a servant speaking up for a girl from home. However it was done, for a while, in a city that owed neither of them anything, Elizabeth had somebody beside her who did not need her history explained.

Service, for a girl in her position, meant being up before the household woke and in bed long after it slept: grates to lay and clear, water to carry, linen to wash, whatever the mistress wanted done exactly the way that mistress wanted it done, learned afresh in every new place. A general servant did whatever was not the nursemaid's. Between them, the two girls from Wethersfield ran a household and minded another woman's three sons, the youngest still a baby. The boys would do well. Guy grew up to follow his father into the law, and in time held the office of Under Sheriff of Bristol that his father had held before him. Reginald, the baby, became a doctor.

In the autumn of 1873 her mother died, at Dog Chase, back in Wethersfield. Jemima was sixty-one. The certificate gave neuralgia, of six weeks, and three weeks of congestion of the brain. A neighbour from Hudson's Hill, Hannah Passfield, was with her at the end, and went to the registrar afterwards and made her mark. Thomas followed her eight weeks later, on the twenty-second of December, of the chronic bronchitis he had carried for a year. He was buried at St Mary Magdalene three days after Christmas.

Elizabeth was eighteen and suddenly alone. There were no brothers or sisters left and, with both her parents gone, no home in the village to return to, even for a visit.

By then the Chiltons were leaving London. By 1874 they had settled at Eltham House, in Westbury on Trym outside Bristol, where two more children were born. When the census found them there in 1881 they kept three servants. Neither Elizabeth nor Susan was among them. Susan's life went on to a marriage on Christmas Day 1880, at Penge, to a man named Arthur Davis, and rooms in Lambeth. Elizabeth's simply goes quiet.

For the next five years there was nobody left whose business it was to keep track of her. She managed, because women in her position generally had to manage, moving from situation to situation as they came up, learning a house, learning its rhythms, and then, sooner or later, having to learn another one, carrying with her only the small store of possessions a servant accumulates. She had no parents to disappoint and none to please, no sister to write to when a place turned out badly, no brother to fall back on if the work ran out. When the newspapers came to write about her, ten years later, they put the whole of it in a line: her father and mother were both dead, and she had no near relatives whatever.

When she surfaces again she is twenty-four, and living in Hackney, in a house belonging to a whitesmith called William Graves.

Graves was about fifty, and an Essex man himself, born at Radwinter, the next parish to Great Sampford. He had been a whitesmith in London since at least his early twenties, and was now foreman of a department at the Falkirk Ironworks in Upper Thames Street. Of the children listed with him in Bethnal Green in 1861, two boys, William and George, were gone ten years later, and a new William, aged four, had taken the name. His first wife, Harriet, was gone too, and he had married again. Martha, eleven years younger, had kept a chandler's shop when the family lived in Mare Street. The house at 88 Frampton Park Road was full of his children: a grown son, Charles, already a whitesmith like his father, and younger ones still at school. It was nothing like the solicitor's house in Albion Road.

It was under Graves that William Gouldstone had started at the Falkirk works the autumn before, the young smith from Sampford whom Graves had known since the day he was born. He was twenty-two, not quite two years younger than Elizabeth, fair and stoutly built, and not tall. He said little unless he was spoken to. He had come as a blacksmith, and in time the firm made him a whitesmith, the careful cousin of the trade he already knew: less fire and hammer, more file and polish. He had lived with Graves, and Elizabeth would later say that they looked upon Graves as one of their own family.

Graves remembered only that William married some four or five months after he arrived. They were married after banns, which meant Elizabeth had been living in the parish for the weeks beforehand, and on the certificate both of them gave the same address: 88 Frampton Park Road.

The wedding was on the fifteenth of March 1879, at St Luke's, Hackney. William gave his age as twenty-two. Elizabeth gave hers as twenty-four, which it was. In the column for the father's occupation, William's entry read "Smith". Elizabeth's father was named, Thomas Stock, and beside his name, where his work should have been, the register read "(dead)". William's parents had come up from Sampford to witness it. They were the only witnesses there were. Emily Gouldstone signed her name. Thomas made his mark. Elizabeth signed hers, and so did William. She stood at the front of that church with her own side of it empty.

Four and a half years later, Elizabeth would write that she had never known, until very lately, that there was any insanity in the family.

Graves and his family stayed on in the house, and William and Elizabeth had rooms of their own in it. "We commenced housekeeping on 21s. a week," she wrote, "and latterly he had 25s." It was sometimes difficult to make ends meet. She did all the spending of the money, and when there was a confinement or a sickness in the house and the payments were a trouble, she did not let him know.

The children came quickly. Charles Thomas was born early in 1880, bright and healthy. Herbert Ernest came at the turn of the next year, and was weakly. Frederick William, with his father's name, followed in the spring of 1882. By then Herbert still could not walk, and she had, as she put it, practically two babies to mind.

When the census came round in April 1881, the enumerator took the Graveses at 88 Frampton Park Road, recording Graves and his son Charles as whitesmiths, and then, on the next schedule, the Gouldstones, though he wrote the name down as "G. Stone": William, twenty-four, blacksmith; Elizabeth, twenty-six; Charles, one; Herbert, four months; and William's younger brother Bennett, nineteen, also a blacksmith, up from Sampford and boarding with them. On the schedule before, Martha Graves had a son of five months. There were two babies in the house that winter, born a month apart.

"Before we had the first child, and right on till the birth of the second," Elizabeth wrote, "we regularly went to Church." After that they could not leave the house together. This bothered William. The strain of making ends meet weighed on his mind, she feared, though he never spoke of it to her, "except in the way of saying how sorry he was that my hands were so full, and that we could not go out together as we used to do. That was the only way in which he ever spoke of the burden of the family."

For all his absent ways, she wrote, he was very lively. He was kind with her and with the boys, handy and willing when they were ailing, and he constantly helped her about the house. He had few acquaintances beyond his own relatives and the men he worked with, and he was nearly always at home when he was not at work, except on a Sunday morning, when he sometimes took a walk alone. He played the cornet and the flute. But it was the concertina he was fond of, and for hours together, while she got on with the housework, he would keep the children going with it. They never tired of listening.

If he seemed worried, amusing the children, at which he was very good, or a word from her would change his mood at once. They lived with Graves for a little under three years, and all three boys were born in his house. Elizabeth remembered one thing William said to her in those years, and said often.

"He was painfully aware of how easy it was for him to get worried, and he has often said to me that it was a good job he had not much to think of, as if he had he believed it would turn his brain."
