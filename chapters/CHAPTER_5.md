Chapter Five
THE HOUSEHOLD

By the spring of 1871, Elizabeth Stock was sixteen years old and already in service in London, in the household of one George Horace David Chilton, a solicitor in Stoke Newington. How she got there from Wethersfield, and at what age she first left home, is unknown. She was aged sixteen, working in a stranger's house a long way from the village she was born in, with both her parents back in Essex.

Service, for a girl in her position, was not one long grey stretch, day after day. It was up before the household woke and to bed long after it slept, grates to lay and clear, water to carry, linen to wash, whatever the mistress wanted done exactly the way that mistress wanted it done, learned afresh in every new place. It was not a large household, and it kept no great staff: just two servants for the whole of it, Elizabeth and one other girl, between them responsible for three young boys, Guy, five, and Stanley, four, and an infant not yet a year old named Reginald, born into the house itself.

The other girl was, for once, someone who already understood where she came from, without a word of explanation needed. Susan Newman, the same age as Elizabeth, had grown up in Wethersfield too, and the two of them had more history between them than the household itself likely ever knew. Susan's own mother had been a widow years before either girl left the village, yet both had been kept at school against the odds while it lasted, two girls from two households already shaped by loss, growing up within easy walking distance of each other long before either one ever set foot in London. It hardly matters how the two of them found their way into the same house so many miles from home. They did, and for a while, in a city that owed neither of them anything, Elizabeth had somebody beside her who did not need the whole of her history explained. Between them they ran a household and raised another woman's three sons, including her youngest, almost entirely on their own.

Then, within two years, everything she still had behind her in Wethersfield was gone. Her mother died in the autumn of 1873. Her father, Thomas, followed her into the grave within two months, in the last hard weeks of that year, the year Elizabeth turned eighteen. There was, after that, almost no one left at all. No brothers or sisters. No household in the village to go home to, even for a visit. Whatever Elizabeth Stock was going to become from that point on, she was going to become it without anyone behind her watching, or waiting, or caring very much either way.

That companionship did not survive the Chiltons' move. They left London a few years later, out to a fine house near Bristol, and took on new servants once they got there, people with no connection to Wethersfield and no reason to have one. Elizabeth was left behind. Susan's own life carried her away from there too, a marriage, a family, and a small greengrocer's shop. Elizabeth's life, from that point, simply goes quiet. There was nobody left whose business it was to notice, and the one piece of comfort she had left by then, that one particular friend in that one particular home, was gone too.

She was, by any ordinary reckoning, entirely on her own for the better part of five years. The truest thing anyone can say about Elizabeth Stock by the time William Gouldstone met her is that she had no parents to disappoint and none to please. No sister to write to when a place turned out badly, no brother to fall back on if the work ran out. She managed, because women in her position generally had to manage, moving from situation to situation as they came up, learning a house, learning its rhythms, and then, sooner or later, having to learn another one. Carrying with her only the small store of possessions a servant accumulates and nothing else besides. Those years must have cost her something, though she never once described them afterward. Where they left her is plainer: twenty-three years old, unattached to any living family, entirely accustomed to managing alone, and, at the far end of them, arriving at a house in Hackney belonging to a whitesmith called William Graves.

It was there that everything changed for her, and it changed quickly.

It was nothing like the quiet, ordered solicitor's house she had known in Stoke Newington. Graves kept a busy household of his own in that same building, above the chandler's shop. Mrs Graves ran the shop while her husband spent his days overseeing the smiths at the Falkirk Iron Works near Blackfriars, foreman over men who did the hands-on work he himself had once done. Sometime in the autumn of 1878, he had taken on a young Essex smith, a quiet young man, fair-haired, stoutly built, not tall, from a village called Great Sampford where his own father kept a forge.

William Gouldstone was twenty-two years old, not quite two years younger than Elizabeth. He said little unless spoken to. He worked hard at the whitesmith trade Graves was teaching him, the careful cousin of the blacksmithing he already knew from his father, less fire and hammer now and more file and polish, and came home most evenings with the tiredness of a man who had spent the day bent close over careful, exacting work, glad enough of a full table and a house with some comfort in it.

How Elizabeth Stock and William Gouldstone met is not recorded. On the day they were married both gave the same address on the certificate, 88 Frampton Park Road, a subdivision of Graves's own home. William had only arrived the previous autumn, four or five months before the wedding, by Graves's own account. Two strangers meeting for the first time in a busy lodging house and marrying within months of the first hello seems unlikely, though not impossible. It is more probable that William was the one with a reason to be there, Graves having known him since he was born, and that Elizabeth's own connection to Frampton Park Road came through the relationship itself, whether she already knew him before he left Essex or came to know him only once he'd arrived. Whatever it was Elizabeth saw in him, a steady, hardworking man with a trade and a temper nobody ever heard raised, was enough. Within a year of William's arrival in Hackney, they married.

The wedding took place on the fifteenth of March 1879, at St Luke's Church, Hackney. It was a plain enough affair: no wedding breakfast to speak of, no photographs, nothing beyond the register itself to mark that it had happened at all. William's parents, Thomas and Emily, had travelled up from Essex to stand as witnesses, the only witnesses either of them had, Elizabeth having no one of her own left to ask. She stood at the front of that church, twenty-four years old, with her whole side of it empty, and married a man whose life, for all else that might be said of it, still included a family, present, solid, willing to make the journey to see the thing done properly. Whatever she felt about the emptiness beside her, she kept it to herself, the way she had learned to keep most things private over the preceding five years. It did not stop her marrying him. It may have made the offer of a family, however borrowed, harder to refuse.

Graves's own family stayed on at the shop on Frampton Park Road, and William and Elizabeth, once married, had rooms of their own besides, and set about making of them, as best two young people with very little between them could, something that felt like theirs.

It did not stay quiet, or empty, for long. Within the first year Charles was born, and Elizabeth, for the first time since her own childhood, had somebody who belonged to her and to no one else. He grew into a strong, healthy child, walking early, never still for long, the kind of boy who filled a small room simply by being in it.

Herbert followed not long after, and gave his mother a different, harder kind of work. Past his second birthday he still could not stand without help, let alone walk, and stayed, for all practical purposes, a baby in arms long after his older brother had been running about the yard. He still had to be carried everywhere his brother now walked, watched a little more closely, worried over a little more than a mother generally worries over a thriving child. Frederick came last, before either of the older two was truly out of clouts, and by the time he arrived Elizabeth had, in the space of barely three years, gone from a woman with nobody in the world to a woman with three sons, a husband, and scarcely a moment in the day that belonged to her alone.

William's younger brother Bennett arrived from Essex around this time too, seventeen or eighteen and looking for work of his own. He moved in with William and Elizabeth in their own rooms, not with Graves, sleeping wherever there was space to sleep, one more person for Elizabeth to feed and mind in a household already full. Three small boys and a teenage boarder, with Graves's own large family living an equally crowded life of its own close by in the same building. Neither of them had ever known much else. Elizabeth had spent her whole adult life sharing other people's houses. William had grown up one of many in a small Essex cottage. Rooms in a subdivided house that was never quiet, with a landlord next door who had known the groom his whole life and had, by now, come to know the bride nearly as well.

For the first while, before the children came thick and fast, Elizabeth and William went to church together most Sundays. It stopped once Herbert was born and could not be left. William minded the change more than he ever admitted outright. The nearest he ever came to complaining, in the whole of their marriage, was to tell her once, quietly, how sorry he was that they could not go out together the way they used to. He never named the money as the reason. He never named the crowding either. Just that one small regret, said once, and never repeated.

What time he could not spend stepping out with his wife, he gave to his sons instead. He kept a concertina, and was fond of it, and some evenings, once the day's work was done and the house had settled into the relative calm of the evening, he would play it for an hour or more while Elizabeth got on with the mending. The boys would gather at his feet on the floor, never once tired of the sound of it, three small faces turned up toward their father in the lamplight. On Sunday mornings, when he was neither working nor at church, he sometimes walked out alone for an hour or two along no route he ever explained and came home again exactly the man he had left as, fond of his own small habits, uninterested in company beyond his own family. He earned twenty-one shillings a week when the marriage began, twenty-five by the time it was properly under way, and it was not always an easy sum to stretch across three young children, but Elizabeth found the way to make it stretch, mending what could be mended rather than replaced, feeding a full table on very little, and kept the worst of the strain from him besides. A word from her, more often than not, was enough to lift him when the weight of it showed on his face. He told her once, almost as a joke, that "it was a good job he had not much to think of as, if he had, he believed it would turn his brain".

She thought little of it at the time. She had a household to run, three boys to mind, and very little space in a full day to dwell on one offhand remark. She would think of almost nothing else, later.

None of this comes from William, who left no account of these years at all until Chelmsford, much later, when there was nothing left to salvage and nothing left to build, only what remained to be said. The marriage as it stands here is Elizabeth's alone: plain, steady, and unsoftened, given at the one moment when telling the truth about him mattered more to her than making him sound better than he was.

They stayed at Frampton Park Road a little under three years: long enough for three sons to be born and to start growing into themselves, long enough for Bennett to settle in as though he had always been there, long enough for whatever Elizabeth had carried into that house from her own hidden years to soften, at last, into something that looked, from any ordinary vantage point, exactly like happiness. A husband. Three children. A trade that paid, however modestly. A household, borrowed at first and by now simply her own. For a woman who had arrived at Graves's door with nothing behind her and nobody to answer to, it must have felt, for a while, like more than she had any right to expect.

Then, in the autumn of 1882, they left Graves's roof for the first time since the wedding, for a house of their own at last, in Walthamstow. It should have been the beginning of the life they had been quietly building toward all along. Instead, in ways nobody around them yet understood, it was the beginning of the end of it.
