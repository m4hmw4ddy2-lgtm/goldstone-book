Chapter Twenty-Three

Of 35, Dagmar Road

Within a month of Brian's burial, Vera's name was in the Chatham Observer. In June 1937 the paper printed, grade by grade, the girls of the County School for Girls who had passed the Civil Service Commissioners' latest examination, all of whom would be offered posts, and under the clerical assistant grade was "Vera Goldstone". She was sixteen. At the end of July she was in the paper again, among the school's passes in the University of London's general school examination: "Vera Goldstone, v.g. spoken French".

Her father was the secretary of the Luton Working Men's Club, which had opened in 1931. In January 1939 the club held its first ladies' night, a dinner in Luton Parochial Hall for about forty of the members' wives, and Clarie was on the committee that arranged it. Albert spoke. He took them back over the club's eight years and told them it would have premises of its own within two years. It had eighty members, and he wanted more. "In view of the unique position of the club we believe that membership should be far greater than it actually is," he said, as the Chatham Observer reported him; the Chatham News had him saying that, small as the club was, its spirit "might well be envied by much larger organisations". The chairman, G. A. Field, said that this was the first evening the ladies had had to themselves since the club was formed, and that he hoped to make the dinner a yearly event. After it there was a dance, and Albert and Mrs Blatchford were the masters of ceremonies.

Five days later Albert spoke at the first dinner of the club's "Tough Guys", members who paid a penny a week into a fund for any of them out of work at Christmas, and in the musical evening that followed he was one of those who sang.

By the autumn the country was at war. On the twenty-ninth of September 1939 every household was entered in a national register, from which everyone would be issued with an identity card and, later, a ration book. At 33A Connaught Road, Chatham, the Register entered Albert as a ships rigger, his year of birth as "86". Clarie was a housewife. Lily, who was twenty-five, was a civil servant, and the younger Clarie a shop assistant in a grocer's. Doris, eleven, was at school.

By this time Vera was not with them. She had moved to 3 Marlborough Street, Bath, and was a civil servant, residing in the household of Fanny R. Cole, a single woman of sixty-eight with means of her own.

On the same night George William Ferguson was recorded as a single man of nineteen, at 79 Park Street, Pembroke, an aircraft riveter. He was lodging with a crane driver's family, in a household of aircraft riveters, fitters and steel benders.

The first of the sisters to marry was the younger Clarie. Her wedding to John T. Ainsley was registered in the Medway district in the summer of 1940.

By the spring of 1942 Vera was back in Chatham, working as a clerk and living at 35 Dagmar Road, and when she and George were married at the parish church of Luton on Saturday the twenty-eighth of March, he gave the same address. Both were twenty-one. He was an aircraft fitter now, and his father, John Henry Ferguson, was a labourer in the dockyard. Albert signed the register as a witness, with Sidney Rose and J. H. Ferguson.

Their first child, Michael George, was born on the twenty-first of April 1943 at 42 Magpie Hall Road, the Medway Hospital, which became All Saints Hospital in 1948. Vera registered the birth herself, on the seventh of May, as of 35 Dagmar Road. Lily married that year too; her husband was Frank G. Godbold. In 1944 Vera and George had a second son, Stephen Albert John.

In March 1945 a delivery of coal brought Albert and Clarie into court at Chatham as witnesses. A coalman was charged with stealing coal from his employer and delivering it to the club on the afternoon of the twelfth of February. Albert, as secretary, said that no coal had been ordered since the seventh, that the gates were kept locked and opened only for the brewer and the coalman, and that the coalman could get the key only from the bar. He had fetched coal from the cellar on the eleventh, when there were five or six hundredweight there, and gone down again with a police constable the next evening, and seen no difference. Clarie said that there had been no one in the club when she left at half past two; the bar was locked, and the key of the cellar was in the bar. The Chatham Observer reported that the bench dismissed the case without calling on the defence.

Doris, the youngest, was the last of the sisters to marry. In November 1948 the East Kent Gazette announced her engagement to Charles George Neaves of Sittingbourne; her parents were "Mr. and Mrs. A. Goldstone, of 35, Dagmar Road, Chatham". The marriage was registered at Chatham the following summer.

Albert was still the club's secretary in January 1953, when it held its annual dinner and dance on Wednesday the fourteenth. Councillor G. W. Rhodes, in the chair, told the members that trade was hard for clubs and pubs, that the easy money of the war years had gone, and that each of them should try to bring in one new member before the year was out.

When Albert's turn came, the Chatham Observer reported, he was speaking "more to the younger men". "We now have a vast amount of ground at our disposal and I see no reason why we could not build our own dance hall and billiards hall," he said. "I shall not worry one iota so long as Luton Working Men's Club is going ahead." And then: "We have got to pull together; only then will we be able to pay off the debt owing now, and then call the club our own."

Clarie died on the second of January 1958, at sixty-eight. Five days later she was buried in Palmerston Road Cemetery, in the grave where Brian had been buried twenty years before, the second burial in it, at eight feet. The cemetery's register entered her as "Wife".

Vera's husband George died at All Saints Hospital, Chatham, on the eighth of February 1962. He was forty-one. A week later he was buried in Palmerston Road Cemetery, in a grave of his own, and the register gave his trade as "Sheet metal Worker". His stone has the words "husband & father".

Four months later, on the twenty-first of June 1962, Albert died at All Saints Hospital, Chatham. Within months of each other, Vera had lost her husband and her father. Albert was buried on the twenty-sixth with his wife and son, Clarie and Brian, the third burial, at six feet, in the grave he had bought. The register entered him as "Retired", aged "76".

On the headstone the three of them are Albert, Brian R. A. and Rennie, "wife & mother". It gives none of them a surname.
