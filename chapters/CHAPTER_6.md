# Chapter Six

## THE DETERIORATION

They left Frampton Park Road in September 1882, three years into their marriage, for a terrace of thirteen bay-fronted cottages a few miles east, on a new development called Courtenay Place. The houses had gone up the year before, on streets that had not even existed when William and Elizabeth married. Tuppence covered the return fare on the workmen's train that had made the whole area possible. On twenty-five shillings a week, a whitesmith could live at the end of a line and still reach Upper Thames Street by half past seven. Number 8 stood near the station end, close enough that the platforms of St James's Street were the first thing visible from the front step. The sound of the steam trains was something the household simply lived within.

They had perhaps a year of it before anything changed.

Elizabeth would later put a figure on it. Twelve months, she was reported as saying, though nothing draws a line at any particular month, or names a moment things turned. Her own words were older and more vague: a man who had always taken too long to answer a simple question, always carried more than he said, for as long as she had known him. Whether that had sharpened into something worse in the year before the murders, or had simply been there the whole time, waiting to be reinterpreted once she had reason to, is not something she ever made clear.

Three boys already filled the back room by then. Charles almost three years old, Herbert not yet two, sickly and still unable to walk, and Frederick barely out of her arms. Another child should have been nothing remarkable in a marriage that had produced one almost every year since the wedding. What nobody in the house yet knew was that this time it would be two.

On the seventh of June 1883 another couple came to lodge across the landing, in the front room on the same first floor: Ada Hamilton, a monthly nurse who would later see Elizabeth through her confinement, and her husband William, a jeweller. Ada noticed what a lodger and nurse might notice about the family on the other side of a thin wall: William sometimes came home with a headache that had come on all at once, his hand going to his head, complaining of a pain that made him stop what he was doing and sit still until it passed. Elizabeth had told her of it herself. An old trouble, she said, a rupture from years back before she had known him, that still troubled him now and again.

On Wednesday the first of August, William had been up early, bringing her a cup of tea the way he often did. Elizabeth was confined later that night at half past ten, and delivered of two boys, twins. An unpleasant surprise, perhaps, in a household already stretched thin. When Ada informed him, he gave one look, said nothing, put on his coat and hat, and went straight out. He did not come back until gone midnight.

When he eventually did return, he came into Elizabeth's room but said nothing. His face looked changed to her. His eyes were strange and wandering, despondent, Ada would later call it. He had not so much as looked at the twins, and would not, not that night nor any of the nights that followed.

He went back to work on the Thursday and the Friday. Friday was pay day, with the customary extra ten shillings the firm gave its men, money in hand for a household that badly needed it. That night William came home the worse for drink, the only time in her ten weeks that Ada ever saw him so, a man who, by every account, had never once been anything but sober and steady. Monday was a Bank Holiday. Tuesday, so far as the household understood it, was holiday too. Twice over the following few days, Ada found him strange and quiet, sitting somewhere in the house, crying. With Elizabeth still not up from childbed, the household was running on whatever Ada could manage.

Wednesday the eighth of August began like any other since the twins were born. William left for his 6:04 train as always; she did not expect to see him again until half past seven.
