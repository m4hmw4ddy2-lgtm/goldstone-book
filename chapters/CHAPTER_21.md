Chapter Twenty-One

I SHALL LIVE TO FIGHT AGAIN

Three weeks after his discharge, on the seventeenth of April 1919, Albert was issued with the King's Certificate of Discharge. The last entry in the character column of his service sheet, beside his final weeks in the barracks, reads "V.G." For his war service he was awarded the 1914-15 Star, the British War Medal and the Victory Medal.

His third daughter, Vera Georgina, was born at 46 Edinburgh Road, Chatham, on the first of March 1921. Clarie registered her in April, and against the father's occupation the registrar wrote "Seaman Yard Craft H.M.D. Ex. R.N". The Yard Craft Service was the Admiralty's civilian service for the tugs, lighters, launches and harbour ferries working in and around the dockyards.

The census that year was to have been taken in April, but because of industrial unrest it was put back to Sunday the nineteenth of June, which was Albert's birthday. That night he signed the household's schedule. He gave his occupation as "Seaman Yard craft" and his employer as Chatham Dockyard. Clarie was "At Home" with the two younger girls, their second daughter, Clarie, who was two, and the baby; Lily, at seven, was at school. Clarie's sister, Alma Rose Setterington, a general servant, was living with them, and the six of them had six rooms. Ten years before, on the night the Dominion lay in Glengariff Harbour, Clarie had been alone at the same address. She had signed the schedule as "Rennie Goldstone", tailoress.

Lily sat the entrance examination for Chatham County School for Girls, and in the summer of 1924, aged ten, won one of the Medway Education Board's free places; the Chatham Observer printed her name in the list as "Lily A. R. Goldstone, Luton Council". The local authority maintained the school, but it charged fees, and with a free place the family paid none. Four years later Clarie gave birth to their fourth daughter, Doris May.

In July 1930 Albert came home from Bermuda. The Orbita, of the Pacific Steam Navigation Company, was on her way from Talcahuano, in Chile, to Plymouth, and he went aboard her at Bermuda and travelled second class. On the same page of the passenger list, also from Bermuda, were an actress, Florence Glossop-Harris, an actor-manager and an actor, a butler and a nurse. The list enters Albert as a rigger, of 46 Edinburgh Road, aged "44". The Orbita landed them at Plymouth on the fourteenth of July.

Clarie and Albert's last child was a boy, Brian Ronald Albert, born in 1932. In her turn Vera, who was eleven, sat the entrance examination for Chatham County School for Girls, and in June the Chatham Education Committee awarded her one of its scholarships there. The school had been extended the year before, nearly doubling in size.

By the autumn of 1933 Albert was standing for the council. The municipal elections were to be held on Wednesday the first of November, and at the end of September the Chatham Observer reported that the only Labour candidate yet chosen in Chatham was "Mr A. Goldstone, the propaganda secretary", who would oppose Councillor Mark Packer in Christ Church ward. A fortnight later Packer was Mayor-elect, and Albert was switched to Luton ward, against Councillor G. B. Silver. In the list of nominations his address was 46 Edinburgh Road and his work "ship rigger in H.M. Dockyard".

Albert lost that election by just thirty-five votes. Afterwards, at the Labour Party's headquarters in Batchelor Street, Mrs M. E. Stearne, who presided, spoke of the party's good results that day: W. Blackmore had won St Paul's ward, and Albert had put up "a magnificent fight". Albert spoke too. "I shall live to fight again," he said. "Let us try and organise a little better and we shall win next time. If I can get a band of workers like that which assisted Mr Blackmore I can beat Clr. Harris next year." Councillor Harris held Luton ward's other seat, which would fall due in 1934.

Labour put him up again the next year, in Christ Church ward, against Councillor Frances M. Freeman. On Thursday the first of November 1934 Albert lost that election by three hundred and twenty-seven votes.

At Broadmoor, in the asylum's infirmary, William Gouldstone died at five minutes past midnight on Saturday the nineteenth of January 1935. He was seventy-eight. On the Monday the coroner, Robert Sidney Payne, held an inquest at the asylum with a jury of eight. Kenneth Milne, an assistant medical officer, said on oath that William had been admitted on the fifteenth of October 1883 from Chelmsford Prison, that "he was a married man & a blacksmith by occupation", that he had been "in poor bodily health for the last six years", and that "during the last 3 months he gradually became weaker". Edward Priest, an attendant, said that he had been on duty in the infirmary when William died. The jury found that he had died of hypostatic pneumonia following myocardial degeneration, from natural causes. Milne had begun to write the name as "Goldstone", then struck it through and wrote "Gouldstone". The typed slip in the coroner's papers reads "Widower Blacksmith."

That Monday, the day of the inquest, the Evening Standard carried the news on page ten, under the heading "Death After 52 Years in Broadmoor": "William Goldstone, a Walthamstow blacksmith, who 52 years ago was found guilty of the murder of his five children and found to be insane, has died in Broadmoor Criminal Lunatic Asylum. He was 80 years of age, and had been described as an ideal patient. He worked in the various wards."

Brian died at St Bartholomew's Hospital, Rochester, on the fifteenth of May 1937, of tuberculous meningitis. He was four, and the family had moved by then to 33A Connaught Road. Clarie was with him when he died, and three days later she registered the death; the registrar entered the boy as "Son of Albert Goldstone Rigger H.M.D." In June, Vera, who was sixteen, passed the Civil Service examination for the clerical assistant grade, and was to be offered a post.

Albert was secretary of the Luton Working Men's Club, which had opened in 1931. At its first ladies' dinner, in Luton Parochial Hall on the twenty-sixth of January 1939, he told them that the club would have premises of its own within two years, and asked for more members, since there were only eighty. "In view of the unique position of the club we believe that membership should be far greater than it actually is," he said.

A month into the war, on the twenty-ninth of September 1939, the government compiled a national register, household by household, for identity cards and rationing. At 33A Connaught Road Albert was entered as a ship's rigger, born on "19 Jun 86". Clarie and three of the girls were with him. Lily was a civil servant and the younger Clarie a shop assistant in a grocer's; Doris was still at school. Vera was in Bath, a civil servant too, living at 3 Marlborough Street.

Vera was back in Chatham by the spring of 1942, a clerk, living at 35 Dagmar Road. On Saturday the twenty-eighth of March she married George William Ferguson, a twenty-one-year-old aircraft fitter from the same address, at Luton parish church, after banns. Against the bride's father the register has "Albert Goldstone", "Rigger", and Albert was one of the three witnesses who signed it. Vera and George's son, Michael George, was born at 42 Magpie Hall Road on the twenty-first of April 1943.

Albert and Clarie were both witnesses at Chatham in March 1945, when a coalman was charged with stealing coal from his employer and delivering it to the Working Men's Club. Albert, as the club's secretary, told the court that no coal had been ordered since the seventh of February and that the gates were kept locked; Clarie said that the bar had been locked and the club empty when she left at half past two. The Chatham Observer reported that the bench dismissed the case without calling on the defence.

Albert was still the club's secretary eight years later. At its annual dinner and dance, on a Wednesday night in January 1953, he spoke, by the Observer's account, "more to the younger men". "We now have a vast amount of ground at our disposal and I see no reason why we could not build our own dance hall and billiards hall," he said. "I shall not worry one iota so long as Luton Working Men's Club is going ahead." And he added: "We have got to pull together; only then will we be able to pay off the debt owing now, and then call the club our own."

Clarie died on the second of January 1958, aged sixty-eight. Five days later she was buried in Palmerston Road Cemetery, in the grave where Brian had been buried twenty years before.

Albert died at All Saints Hospital, Chatham, on the twenty-first of June 1962. On the twenty-sixth he was buried with them, the third burial in the grave he had bought. The cemetery's register entered him as "Retired", aged "76". On the headstone the three of them are Albert, Brian R. A. and Rennie, "wife & mother". It gives none of them a surname.
