Chapter Twenty-One

I SHALL LIVE TO FIGHT AGAIN

Three weeks after his discharge, on the seventeenth of April 1919, Albert was issued with the King's Certificate of Discharge. The last entry in the character column of his service sheet, beside his final weeks in the barracks, reads "V.G." For his war service he was awarded the 1914-15 Star, the British War Medal and the Victory Medal.

His third daughter, Vera Georgina, was born at 46 Edinburgh Road, Chatham, on the first of March 1921. Clarie registered her in April, and against the father's occupation the registrar wrote "Seaman Yard Craft H.M.D. Ex. R.N". The Yard Craft Service was the Admiralty's civilian service for the tugs, lighters, launches and harbour ferries working in and around the dockyards.

The census that year was to have been taken in April, but because of industrial unrest it was put back to Sunday the nineteenth of June, which was Albert's birthday. That night he signed the household's schedule. He gave his occupation as "Seaman Yard craft" and his employer as Chatham Dockyard. Clarie was "At Home" with the two younger girls, their second daughter, Clarie, who was two, and the baby; Lily, at seven, was at school. Clarie's sister, Alma Rose Setterington, a general servant, was living with them, and the six of them had six rooms. Ten years before, on the night the Dominion lay in Glengariff Harbour, Clarie had been alone at the same address. She had signed the schedule as "Rennie Goldstone", tailoress.

Lily sat the entrance examination for Chatham County School for Girls, and in the summer of 1924, aged ten, won one of the Medway Education Board's free places; the Chatham Observer printed her name in the list as "Lily A. R. Goldstone, Luton Council". The local authority maintained the school, but it charged fees, and with a free place the family paid none. Four years later Clarie gave birth to their fourth daughter, Doris May.

In July 1930 Albert came home from Bermuda. The Orbita, of the Pacific Steam Navigation Company, was on her way from Talcahuano, in Chile, to Plymouth, and he went aboard her at Bermuda and travelled second class. On the same page of the passenger list, also from Bermuda, were an actress, Florence Glossop-Harris, an actor-manager and an actor, a butler and a nurse. The list enters Albert as a rigger, of 46 Edinburgh Road, aged "44". The Orbita landed them at Plymouth on the fourteenth of July.

Clarie and Albert's last child was a boy, Brian Ronald Albert, born in 1932. In her turn Vera, who was eleven, sat the entrance examination for Chatham County School for Girls, and in June the Chatham Education Committee awarded her one of its scholarships there. The school had been extended the year before, nearly doubling in size.

By the autumn of 1933 Albert was standing for the council. The municipal elections were to be held on Wednesday the first of November, and at the end of September the Chatham Observer reported that the only Labour candidate yet chosen in Chatham was "Mr A. Goldstone, the propaganda secretary", who would oppose Councillor Mark Packer in Christ Church ward. A fortnight later Packer was Mayor-elect, and Albert was switched to Luton ward, against Councillor G. B. Silver. In the list of nominations his address was 46 Edinburgh Road and his work "ship rigger in H.M. Dockyard".

Albert lost that election by just thirty-five votes. Afterwards, at the Labour Party's headquarters in Batchelor Street, Mrs M. E. Stearne, who presided, spoke of the party's good results that day: W. Blackmore had won St Paul's ward, and Albert had put up "a magnificent fight". Albert spoke too. "I shall live to fight again," he said. "Let us try and organise a little better and we shall win next time. If I can get a band of workers like that which assisted Mr Blackmore I can beat Clr. Harris next year." Councillor Harris held Luton ward's other seat, which would fall due in 1934.

Labour put him up again the next year, in Christ Church ward, against Councillor Frances M. Freeman. On Thursday the first of November 1934 Albert lost that election by three hundred and twenty-seven votes.

Brian died at St Bartholomew's Hospital, Rochester, on the fifteenth of May 1937, of tuberculous meningitis. He was four. Clarie was with him when he died, and three days later she registered the death; the registrar entered the boy as "Son of Albert Goldstone Rigger H.M.D." He was buried on the twentieth, in Palmerston Road Cemetery. The grave was dug to ten feet.
