Chapter Twenty-One

I SHALL LIVE TO FIGHT AGAIN

Three weeks after his discharge, on the seventeenth of April 1919, Albert was issued with the King's Certificate of Discharge. The last entry in the character column of his service sheet, beside his final weeks in the barracks, reads "V.G." For his war service he was awarded the 1914-15 Star, the British War Medal and the Victory Medal.

His third daughter, Vera Georgina, was born at 46 Edinburgh Road, Chatham, on the first of March 1921. Clarie registered her in April, and against the father's occupation the registrar wrote "Seaman Yard Craft H.M.D. Ex. R.N". The Yard Craft Service was the Admiralty's civilian service for the tugs, lighters, launches and harbour ferries working in and around the dockyards.

The census that year was to have been taken in April, but because of industrial unrest it was put back to Sunday the nineteenth of June, which was Albert's birthday. That night he signed the household's schedule. He gave his occupation as "Seaman Yard craft" and his employer as Chatham Dockyard. Clarie was "At Home" with the two younger girls, their second daughter, Clarie, who was two, and the baby; Lily, at seven, was at school. Clarie's sister, Alma Rose Setterington, a general servant, was living with them, and the six of them had six rooms. Ten years before, on the night the Dominion lay in Glengariff Harbour, Clarie had been alone at the same address. She had signed the schedule as "Rennie Goldstone", tailoress.

Lily sat the entrance examination for Chatham County School for Girls, and in the summer of 1924, aged ten, won one of the Medway Education Board's free places; the Chatham Observer printed her name in the list as "Lily A. R. Goldstone, Luton Council". The local authority maintained the school, but it charged fees, and with a free place the family paid none. Four years later Clarie gave birth to their fourth daughter, Doris May.

In July 1930 Albert came home from Bermuda. The Orbita, of the Pacific Steam Navigation Company, was on her way from Talcahuano, in Chile, to Plymouth, and he went aboard her at Bermuda and travelled second class. On the same page of the passenger list, also from Bermuda, were an actress, Florence Glossop-Harris, an actor-manager and an actor, a butler and a nurse. The list enters Albert as a rigger, of 46 Edinburgh Road, aged "44". The Orbita landed them at Plymouth on the fourteenth of July. At the end of June the Royal Gazette and Colonist Daily, in Hamilton, had advertised the farewell performance of "Miss Florence Glossop-Harris and her English Repertory Company" at the Colonial Opera House, on Tuesday the first of July.

The senior girls of the school in Grove Road gave a nativity play, The Cradle King, in Christ Church, Luton, on Wednesday the sixteenth of December 1931. One of the shepherds was "I. Goldstone": the younger Clarie, who was thirteen. In the Observer she was always Irene.

Vera was at Luton Junior and Infants' Council School. Two days after the nativity play Laidman Browne, of the Rochester Repertory Players, came to the school as Father Christmas, with a colleague dressed as Mickey Mouse, and the Rector of Luton gave out the form prizes. In the Chatham Observer's list the first, for Class 1A, is "Vera Goldstone".

When Luton crowned its May Queen in the Parochial Hall on the twenty-sixth of May 1932, "Irene Goldstone (captain)" headed the list of the children taking part.

Clarie and Albert's last child was a boy, Brian Ronald Albert, born in 1932. In her turn Vera, who was eleven, sat the entrance examination for Chatham County School for Girls, and in June the Chatham Education Committee awarded her one of its scholarships there. The school had been extended the year before, nearly doubling in size.

Irene had the chief part in a musical play, The Fortune Ballot, given in the Parochial Hall that December, as "a servant girl who believed that she, like her mistress, had won first prize in a sweepstake." By the summer of 1933 she was Luton's May Queen herself, and at the Christ Church fete in the rectory meadow at the end of June she gave the Mayoress of Rochester a bouquet, and buttonholes to the Mayor and to Sir Park Goff, the Member of Parliament.

By the autumn of 1933 Albert was standing for the council. In September he and a committee had arranged the Chatham divisional Labour party's first dance of the season, at the Town Hall. The municipal elections were to be held on Wednesday the first of November, and at the end of September the Chatham Observer reported that the only Labour candidate yet chosen in Chatham was "Mr A. Goldstone, the propaganda secretary", who would oppose Councillor Mark Packer in Christ Church ward. A fortnight later Packer was Mayor-elect, and Albert was switched to Luton ward, against Councillor G. B. Silver. In the list of nominations his address was 46 Edinburgh Road and his work "ship rigger in H.M. Dockyard".

Albert lost that election by just thirty-five votes. Afterwards, at the Labour Party's headquarters in Batchelor Street, Mrs M. E. Stearne, who presided, spoke of the party's good results that day: W. Blackmore had won St Paul's ward, and Albert had put up "a magnificent fight". Albert spoke too. "I shall live to fight again," he said. "Let us try and organise a little better and we shall win next time. If I can get a band of workers like that which assisted Mr Blackmore I can beat Clr. Harris next year." Councillor Harris held Luton ward's other seat, which would fall due in 1934.

In December the divisional party held a Christmas bazaar and fair in Chatham Town Hall to pay off its debts. Albert sat on its executive and finance committees, and Clarie was one of the two women who ran the cocktail bar.

The Mayor of Chatham, Mark Packer, came to Christ Church Girls' School a week later to present a shield to Gundulph House, whose girls had collected more than five pounds in halfpennies and farthings that year for St Bartholomew's Hospital, Rochester. Irene, the house captain, welcomed the visitors.

At the end of January 1934 Albert was on the committee that gave a party in the Methodist Schoolroom, Luton, for more than two hundred children of the unemployed in Luton and Christ Church wards.

The County School for Girls performed Maeterlinck's The Blue Bird in July, and "V. Goldstone" was in the cast twice, among the Happinesses and among the Unborn Children.

Labour put Albert up again the next year, in Christ Church ward, against Councillor Frances M. Freeman. On Thursday the first of November 1934 Albert lost that election by three hundred and twenty-seven votes.

The Luton Working Men's Club gave its first party for its members' children on Saturday the twenty-second of February 1936. About forty came. After tea and games there was a concert, and Albert was one of those who performed in it; he was on the committee that arranged the party, and at the end he thanked the members for their help.

The Observer reviewed the Chatham County School girls' production of Lady Precious Stream in March 1936. It was played without scenery, and the paper called it "perhaps their best production". The highest praise went to Joan Kay in the title part; Sylvia Cox played the hero and Molly Purchase the Tiger General, and "Vera Goldstone acted with charm and conviction in the part of the Prime Minister's wife".

In August 1936, at the crowning of Chatham's Carnival Queen in the Town Hall, three hundred schoolchildren danced in a floral pageant. Among the dancers from Luton Council Girls' School was "Doris Goldstone". She was eight.

Brian died at St Bartholomew's Hospital, Rochester, on the fifteenth of May 1937, of tuberculous meningitis. He was four. Clarie was with him when he died, and three days later she registered the death; the registrar entered the boy as "Son of Albert Goldstone Rigger H.M.D." He was buried on the twentieth, in Palmerston Road Cemetery. The grave was dug to ten feet.
