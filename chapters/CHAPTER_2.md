# Chapter 2 — The Same Witness

By the time Elizabeth was born, in February 1855, her mother had already buried almost all of her first family. Jemima had married once before, borne eight children by that marriage, and buried five of them before her second wedding even took place. Elizabeth grew up inside a household built, in every sense, over an older one.

Jemima was baptised on 15 December 1811, at Hornchurch, on the London side of Essex, the daughter of Edward Suckling and his wife Sarah. Sometime in the following thirty years the family moved north and east, out of the Barking and Hornchurch flatlands and into the wool and straw country around Finchingfield, following whatever work a labourer could find.

Edward is recorded there across four censuses, in the same small household each time, his occupation sliding steadily downward: agricultural labourer in 1841, gardener by 1851, and by 1861 blind, out of work altogether, and a parish pauper. He and Sarah lived out that last stage of their lives at Malting Yard, on the eastern edge of Finchingfield, past the almshouses, past a smithy, past the Three Tuns. The malting yard took in barley, steeped it in a tank until it swelled, spread it across the floor to be turned by hand, day after day, then carried it up to the kiln to dry: an industrial address for a family with nothing industrial left to do.

On Christmas Day 1833, aged twenty-two, Jemima married John Ansell at St Mary Magdalene, Wethersfield. Her new husband was the son of a Wethersfield brewer, also called John, who would spend his last recorded years blind at his daughter-in-law's table, nearly three decades on and one more marriage of hers besides.

The younger John farmed the land his father had built a business around, and the couple began their marriage in his father's household at Dog Chase, a lane at the heart of the village that still survives today, under that same name. For a stretch of years around the turn of the century, mapmakers preferred the more prosaic Dog Lane, but the parish registers held to Dog Chase.

Jemima bore seven children between 1834 and 1843. By the 1841 census there were already six of them under one roof: John Nathan, seven; the twins Sarah and Eliza, both six, entered by hand in the Register of Baptisms under a shared birth date, 4 August 1835; Alfred, three; George, two; and Emma, eight months old. Two years later, in July 1843, Arthur was born, and baptised at the same church.

Then, across a little over three years, Jemima buried nearly all of it.

Sarah went first, in March 1844, aged eight. Seven weeks later Arthur followed her, not yet one. The following spring, John himself died, thirty-two years old. Between his children's burials, the year had produced only another grave. Four months after that, Eliza was buried, aged ten. The parish burial register lists her interment under her full name, Elizabeth. The next summer took Alfred, eight. Somewhere in amongst it all, an eighth child, a daughter named Edith, born after her father had already died, lived just long enough to be buried in February 1847, not yet two years old.

The register that records each burial doesn't say what took them. Scarlet fever, diphtheria, and typhus all moved through Essex villages in those years, any one of which could empty a house this thoroughly, but the entries themselves give no information beyond a name and a date. Six deaths in three years: a husband and five children, out of a household that had numbered eleven only a few years before.

Three children remained. By 1851 Jemima was still living on Dog Chase, by now a widow and straw plaiter, twisting those split lengths of wheat straw into the long plait that fed the hat trade centred on Luton and Dunstable, sold on by the yard at market. Still at home with her were John Nathan, sixteen and working now as an errand boy. George, eleven, and Emma, eight, were still at school.

On 16 June 1854, at the same church where she had buried five of her own children, Jemima was married again, to Thomas. The building itself had not changed: the restoration that would add a vestry and organ chamber to its south side, and refit much of the interior, was still nearly two decades away. Thomas Stock was himself a widower, though no record survives naming his first wife. The witnesses were Jemima's brother, Robert Suckling, and Frederick Smee, the parish clerk, a Wethersfield man born around 1790 who was still recorded in the post, aged seventy-one, in the 1861 census. His signature had already gone into the same register at her first wedding, twenty-one years before, and would go into it again for whoever stood at that altar next. The office didn't care whose life it was recording. It just kept recording.

The following year, Elizabeth was born.

She was not yet a year old when John Nathan, the eldest of her mother's Ansell children, died in July 1856, aged twenty-two. He was buried under the name Nathan alone, the John dropped from the register. Two years after that, George followed him, nineteen. Only Emma was left of the eight, living on in her mother and stepfather's household until her own death in November 1861, the first of her mother's losses that Elizabeth was old enough to understand.

By then, every child Jemima had borne before Elizabeth was dead. Elizabeth was the only survivor of nine births across her mother's two marriages.

A few miles off, in the blacksmith's forge at Great Sampford, a different family was already being enumerated in a ledger of its own.
