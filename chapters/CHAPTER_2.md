Chapter Two

THE SAME WITNESS

By the time Elizabeth was born, in February 1855, her mother had already buried almost all of her first family. Jemima had married once before, borne eight children by that marriage, and buried five of them before her second wedding even took place. Elizabeth grew up inside a household built, in every sense, over an older one.

Jemima was baptised on the fifteenth of December 1811, at Hornchurch, on the London side of Essex, the daughter of Edward Suckling and his wife Sarah. Sometime in the following thirty years the family moved north and east, out of the Barking and Hornchurch flatlands and into the wool and straw country around Finchingfield, following whatever work a labourer could find.

Edward is recorded there across four censuses, in the same small household each time, his occupation sliding steadily downward, from agricultural labourer to gardener, and then, by 1861, to nothing at all: blind, out of work, a parish pauper. He and Sarah lived out that last stage of their lives at Malting Yard, on the eastern edge of Finchingfield, past the almshouses, past a smithy, past the Three Tuns. The malting yard took in barley, steeped it in a tank until it swelled, spread it across the floor to be turned by hand, day after day, then carried it up to the kiln to dry.

On Christmas Day 1833, aged twenty-two, Jemima married John Ansell at St Mary Magdalene, Wethersfield, witnessed by the parish clerk, Frederick Smee, among others. Her new husband was the son of a Wethersfield brewer, also called John: the man who, nearly three decades on and one more marriage of hers besides, would sit blind at the Stocks' table.

The younger John farmed the land his father had built a business around, and the couple began their marriage in his father's household at Dog Chase, a lane at the heart of the village that still survives today, under that same name. At the top of the lane, where it met the High Street, stood the Dog Inn, kept through all of those years by William Shead. The building survives, a private house now, called Chase House.

Jemima bore seven children between 1834 and 1843. By the 1841 census there were already six of them under one roof: John Nathan, seven; the twins Sarah and Eliza, both six, entered side by side in the register of the Independent chapel, under a single shared date of birth, 4 August 1835; Alfred, three; George, two; and Emma, eight months old. Two years later, in July 1843, Arthur was born, and baptised at St Mary Magdalene, the church his parents had married in.

Then, across a little over three years, Jemima buried nearly all of it.

Sarah went first, in March 1844, aged eight. Eight weeks later Arthur followed her, not yet one year old. In April 1845, John himself died, thirty-two years old. Four months after that, Eliza was buried, aged ten. The parish burial register lists her interment under her full name, Elizabeth. The next summer took Alfred, eight. Somewhere in amongst it all, an eighth child, a daughter named Edith, born after her father had already died, lived just long enough to be buried in February 1847, not yet two years old.

The register that records each burial does not say what took them. Scarlet fever, diphtheria, and typhus all moved through Essex villages in those years, any one of which could empty a house this thoroughly, but the entries themselves give no information beyond a name and a date. Six deaths in three years: a husband and five children, out of a household that had numbered eleven only a few years before.

Three children remained. By 1851 Jemima was still living on Dog Chase, by now a widow and straw plaiter, twisting those split lengths of wheat straw into the long plait that fed the hat trade centred on Luton and Dunstable, sold on by the yard at market. Still at home with her were John Nathan, sixteen and working now as an errand boy. George, eleven, and Emma, eight, were still at school. Old John Ansell was in the house too, seventy-five.

On the sixteenth of June 1854, at the same church where she had buried five of her own children, Jemima was married again, after banns, by the curate, C. A. L'Oste. She was forty-two. Thomas Stock, an agricultural labourer, baptised at Finchingfield in 1802, was nine years older, and himself a widower, though no record survives naming his first wife. The witnesses were Jemima's brother, Robert Suckling, and Frederick Smee, the parish clerk, a Wethersfield man born around 1790 who was still recorded in the post, aged seventy-one, in the 1861 census. His signature had already gone into the same register at her first wedding, twenty-one years before, and would go into it again for whoever stood at that altar next.

On the eighteenth of February 1855, at forty-three, Jemima had a daughter. They named her Elizabeth. On the certificate her mother went down under all three of her names: Jemima Stock, late Ansell, formerly Suckling.

She was not yet a year old when John Nathan, the eldest of her mother's Ansell children, died in July 1856, aged twenty-two. He was buried under the name Nathan alone, the John dropped from the register. Two years after that, George followed him, nineteen. Only Emma was left of the eight, living on in her mother and stepfather's household until the November of 1861.

By then, every child Jemima had borne before Elizabeth was dead. Elizabeth was the only survivor of nine births across her mother's two marriages.

A few miles off, in the blacksmith's forge at Great Sampford, a different family was already being enumerated in a ledger of its own.
