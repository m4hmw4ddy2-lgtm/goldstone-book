Chapter Two

IN OCCUPATION

On the night of Sunday the seventh of April 1861, the census found them both. Elizabeth was six, and William was four.

At Wethersfield the enumerator entered the Stocks as one of four households under a single name, Brewery House. It was the name for the whole of Thomas Raven's brewery, which had been established in 1855, the year Elizabeth was born: a brewhouse, a malthouse, the brewer's house and, next door, the tap. Raven was entered as a farmer of a hundred acres, employing six men and three boys, and as a maltster and brewer. His two sisters kept his house, and his nephew, nineteen, was already a brewer. In the next household William Westwood, seventy-two, a master shoemaker, lived on a plot he owned. In the last, George Love, a journeyman brewer, lived with his widowed mother. At the Brewery Tap Edwin Martin sold the beer, and up in the village, at the top of Dog Chase, the Dog Inn was the Ravens' too. Nobody in the village had water in the house; it came from the springs and from the pump on the High Street, and it would until 1937.

Thomas Stock was down as an agricultural labourer, Jemima as his wife, and Elizabeth as a scholar. Emma, twenty, a straw plaiter, was entered as Thomas's "daughter-in-law", though she was Elizabeth's half-sister. John Ansell, eighty-five and blind, was his "father-in-law", though he shared no blood with Thomas Stock. The census had a word for what everyone in that house was to Thomas. It had none for what most of them were to each other.

In the village, two doors from the Red Lion, the same night found another girl of five entered as a scholar, Susan Newman, whose mother was a widow of twenty-three and in service.

Against John Ansell's name the enumerator also wrote "Pauper". The word meant that he lived on poor relief, paid for out of the parish rates. A few miles off, at Finchingfield, the same night found Jemima's parents, Edward and Sarah Suckling, seventy-six and seventy-one, at Malting Yard, on the edge of the village, with their son John, a farm labourer, his wife, Ann, a straw plaiter like Emma, and their baby. Against both the old people's names the enumerator there wrote the same word.

At Great Sampford the enumerator, John Burton, began his book with a grocer, William Philpott; a tailor; a young farm labourer and his family; and George Gray, a master carpenter employing one man, with his wife and seven sons. The fifth house was the smithy. There he wrote down Thomas Gouldstone, thirty-nine, blacksmith, employing three men; his wife, Emily, twenty-seven; and their children, Emily, nine, Robert, seven, William, four, and Thomas, two.

That summer the Stocks' home was put up for sale. Samuel Newman advertised in the Chelmsford Chronicle at the end of August that he would sell by auction, at the Dog Inn, Wethersfield, on Monday the twenty-third of September, a draper's and grocer's shop, a field, and a string of cottages across the parish, among them "A DOUBLE TENEMENT, near the 'Brewery,'" which Thomas Stock occupied with one other tenant.

Emma died at home on the twenty-seventh of October. Her certificate gave the cause as diseased lungs, of a month's standing. Mary Livermore, who was with her at the end, registered the death with her mark. Emma was buried at St Mary Magdalene on the second of November, aged twenty-one. She was the last of Jemima's first family.

John Ansell outlived her by less than three years. He died in 1864, aged eighty-eight, when Elizabeth was nine.

In the middle of the decade, at Great Sampford, the smithy ran into difficulties. In the same years Emily was ill in her mind, and tried at least once to take her own life. Thomas brought a woman into the house to be with her.

Joseph's wife, Ruth, died on the twenty-seventh of December 1865, at seventy-two. She had had heart disease for three years. Harriet Monk, who was with her, registered the death with her mark, and on the certificate Joseph was a journeyman blacksmith.

Cholera came to Great Sampford in November 1866, and the first to die was a stranger. On the Saturday afternoon, the third, a man named William Winterflood, seventy-three, came to the Bull Inn with a companion from Walworth and took a room. He was taken ill in the night and said nothing to anyone until he heard the landlady moving in the morning. A surgeon came from Thaxted. Winterflood died at about six that evening, and the surgeon certified Asiatic cholera.

That Monday a boy of ten, Henry Andrews, a farm labourer, worked alongside his father at Mr Portway's Houses, and stayed out in the street until ten that night, watching a travelling show. Cramps came on in the night. By eight on the Tuesday morning he was dead. The Chelmsford Chronicle said that the house where he lived and died had been reported for "very foul drainage", and its landlord summoned, about six weeks before. William was ten that autumn.

The Chronicle counted "about 18 cases and seven or eight deaths" by the twenty-third. A large fire was kept burning in the part of the village where the disease was worst; the dead were buried at once, and their clothing burned. The medical officer, Mr Fry, came to live in the village until it passed, and a Mrs Horners made beef-tea for the sick. The same day the Guardians of the Saffron Walden union thanked Fry for "arresting the progress of the cholera".

At the Rose and Crown Hotel in Saffron Walden, on the sixteenth of August 1867, the auctioneer William Rand sold thirty-three lots of property from parishes round about. Thomas occupied two of them. A garden called the Orchard, which he had with a W. Phillpott, went for fifty-eight pounds. Lot 26 was a "Freehold house and blacksmith's shop, &c., in occupation of T. Gouldstone". It went to a Mr G. Wright, for a hundred and two pounds.
