The truest thing anyone can say about Elizabeth Stock by the time William Gouldstone met her is that she had no parents to disappoint and none to please. Her mother and father had been dead five years. Every brother and sister she had ever had was dead before them. She had no near relatives at all, and there was no house in Wethersfield that was hers to go back to.

In the autumn of 1878 William left Great Sampford for London. He was twenty-two, not quite two years younger than Elizabeth, fair and stoutly built, and not tall. He went to the Falkirk Ironworks, at 67 Upper Thames Street, where the foreman of his department was William Graves.

Graves had known him since the day he was born. He was an Essex man himself, baptised at Radwinter, the next parish to Great Sampford, in January 1829, and he had been a whitesmith in London since he was a young man: a lodger in Tower Hamlets at twenty-two, a married man in Bethnal Green with five children at thirty-two. By 1871 he was in Hackney, at 4 Mare Street, married again. His second wife, Martha, kept a chandler's shop. Two of the boys listed with him in 1861, William and George, were not with him ten years later, and a new William, aged four, had the name.

That autumn the firm was in the City papers. On the thirtieth of October the City Press printed its fifth list of awards made to London exhibitors at the Paris Exhibition, and among them was the Falkirk Iron Company, 67 Upper Thames Street, with an honourable mention.

William had come to the works as a blacksmith, and there he was made a whitesmith. It was a different kind of work. A blacksmith forged at the fire and the anvil, and made heavy things, horseshoes and ploughshares and chains. A whitesmith took iron and steel already forged and finished it, filing and polishing it into lighter and finer things, locks and keys and tools.

Graves lived at 88 Frampton Park Road, in Hackney, and William lived with him. The house was full of Graves's children, and it was nothing like the solicitor's house in Albion Road. By the following March, Elizabeth Stock was living there too. Long afterwards she would say that they looked upon Graves "as one of our own family".

Their banns were read at St Luke's, Hackney, which meant that Elizabeth had been living in the parish for the weeks before, and on Saturday the fifteenth of March 1879 they were married there by John C. Harding. The register gave William's age as twenty-two and his occupation as iron worker, and Elizabeth's as twenty-four, which it was, with a dash where her occupation would have gone. Both gave the same address, 88 Frampton Park Road. Under the fathers' names went Thomas Goldstone, smith, and Thomas Stock, and beside Thomas Stock, where his trade should have been, "(dead)".

The name was written Goldstone throughout, and William signed it so. The witnesses were his parents. Emily signed her name, Emily Goldstone. Thomas made his mark. Elizabeth stood at the front of the church with her own side of it empty.

Twenty-four years before, Elizabeth's father had made his mark where he should have signed for her birth. Elizabeth signed her own name.

"We commenced housekeeping on 21s a week," Elizabeth wrote, "and latterly he had 25s."

They stayed on in Graves's house, in rooms of their own, for about three years, and all three of their boys were born there. Elizabeth had all the spending of the money. "Before we had the first child, and right on till the birth of the second, we regularly went to church."

The first child, Charles Thomas, was born in the first months of 1880. The second, Herbert Ernest, came at the end of the year. That Christmas Day, at Penge, Susan Newman married a man named Arthur John Davis, and by the spring they were living in Lambeth, at 362 Clayton Road. "The second child was weakly, and the first was still only a baby, so that we could not leave the house together." When the money was short, at a confinement or when one of them was ill, she did not tell William. The strain weighed on him all the same, she feared, though he never spoke of it, "except in the way of saying how sorry he was that my hands were so full, and that we could not go out together as we used to do. That was the only way in which he ever spoke of the burden of the family."

When the census came round on the third of April 1881, the enumerator found two households at 88 Frampton Park Road. The first was the Graveses: William Graves, fifty-two, white smith; Martha, forty; Charles, nineteen, a white smith like his father; William, thirteen, Mary Ann, ten, and Thomas, eight, at school; and Lewis, six months old. The second he wrote down as "William G. Stone", twenty-four, blacksmith, born at Sampford; Elizabeth, twenty-six, born at Wethersfield; Charles, one; Herbert, four months; and a boarder, Bennett, nineteen, William's younger brother, a blacksmith too. There were two babies in the house that spring, two months apart.

Frederick William, with his father's name, followed in the spring of 1882.

He was lively, she said, for all his absent ways, and kind with her and with the boys. He was handy when they were ill, and he helped her about the house. He had few acquaintances beyond his own family and the men he worked with, and he was nearly always at home when he was not at work, except on a Sunday morning, when he sometimes went for a walk alone. "But for hours together he would keep the children going with his concertina, which he was very fond of playing, while I was engaged in household duties. The children were very fond of him, and were never tired listening to the concertina. If he seemed worried, amusing the children—at which he was very good—or a remark from me would at once change his mood."

Elizabeth remembered one thing William said to her in those years, and said often. "He was painfully aware of how easy it was for him to get worried, and he has often said to me that it was a good job he had not much to think of, as if he had he believed it would turn his brain."
