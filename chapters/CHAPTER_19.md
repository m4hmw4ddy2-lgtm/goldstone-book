Chapter Nineteen

LATE GOLDSTONE FORMERLY STOCK

A girl was born in Beach Alley, Whitstable, on Monday the seventh of April 1890, and named Lilian Florence Isabel. Her birth was registered on the nineteenth of May.

The informant was the child's mother. The registry entry gives her as "E. Madams", "Mother", of Beach Alley, and the father as William Madams, general labourer. In the mother's column she is "Elizabeth Madams late Goldstone formerly Stock".

Three years after Lambeth, Elizabeth was on the Kent coast, using the surname of the child's father.

By the census, on the night of Sunday the fifth of April 1891, the household had moved to 41 Harbour Street, and lived in two rooms. William Madams and Elizabeth were living as husband and wife: the enumerator entered him as head, a general labourer, born in Whitstable, and her as his wife. Below them came "Albert Goldstone", three years old, born in Lambeth, and in the column for his relation to the head of the family, "Stepson". The baby was "Lillian Madams", eleven months.

Eliza Jane was born on the sixteenth of September, at Harbour Place.

The two girls were baptised on Saturday the seventeenth of October, one entry after the other: Lilian at eighteen months, Eliza Jane at a month old. The register gives the parents as "William & Elizabeth", the surname as "Madams", and the father's trade as "Coal Porter".

Twelve days later Elizabeth registered Eliza Jane's birth herself, as "E. Madams", "Mother", of Harbour Place. The father's column again names William Madams, and the mother's column holds the same three surnames she had given in May the year before.

In 1896 William Madams was summoned for not sending a child regularly to school. The Herne Bay Press of the second of May gave his case in two lines: he "was similarly summoned in respect to his son aged eight", and "The mother of the child pleaded poverty as the cause." The bench put the case off for a fortnight, on the same terms as the one before it, to give the boy time to attend better.

The School Board's warnings went on. In May 1898 it ordered a final warning to the boy's parents, and in June legal proceedings against them. In July 1899 a fine of five shillings was imposed in his case. In the reports of 1898 and 1899 he is "Albert Madams".

By March 1901 he was thirteen and at work. The Folkestone Chronicle's report of his case gives the story as the witnesses told it. Albert was a basket boy, helping to unload a cargo of coal from a vessel called the Christabel. On the twenty-eighth of March he took a pair of sea boots to John James Pout, a bootmaker in Harbour Street, and asked him to "put pieces on the bottom". Pout told the court that the boy had said "they were his own boots, they having been given to him", and that he had told the boy they were "more suitable for his father than for him". Two days later Albert came back "and said his father wanted 3s. for them", and Pout paid it. The boots belonged to Alfred Baker, an able seaman on the Christabel.

The census was taken the next night, Sunday the thirty-first. At Middle Wall the enumerator entered William Madams, harbour labourer, as head of the household, and Elizabeth as his wife. Albert was his "Son", fifteen, and a harbour labourer too; Lillian was ten and Eliza nine. They had fewer than five rooms.

On the Monday a constable arrested Albert, who told him the boots were at Mr Pout's.

The Canterbury Journal gave Elizabeth's part in that day. "Elizabeth Goldstone, of Middle Wall, was charged at the County Police Court on Tuesday with being drunk and disorderly at Whitstable on the previous day. She is the mother of the boy in custody charged with stealing a pair of sea boots, and when she heard that he had been locked up she went in a state of intoxication, to the police station and created a disturbance. She was requested to go away, which she refused to do, and as she was very drunk the police locked her up also." She was fined five shillings, with ten shillings and threepence costs.

At St Augustine's Petty Sessions on Saturday the sixth of April, "Albert Goldstone, aged 14" pleaded guilty. Mr Bray, the police court missionary, "offered to get the boy on a training ship", and the bench adjourned the case for a week to let him try.

On the thirteenth, the Herne Bay Press reported, the boy was "bound over to come up for judgment when called upon", the missionary "having got the boy on the training-ship Warspite".

On the fourth of September 1901 William Madams was admitted to the Kent County Lunatic Asylum at Chartham, near Canterbury.

Albert joined the Royal Navy on the eleventh of November, as Albert Goldstone, a boy second class on HMS Impregnable.

Madams died in the asylum on Saturday the twenty-third of November, aged forty-two. His death certificate gives the cause as "General Paralysis of the Insane about 3 months", certified by J. C. FitzGerald, M.D. The informant was the asylum's resident clerk and steward.

Elizabeth died at Beach Alley on Friday the twenty-ninth of June 1906. Her certificate calls her "Widow of William Madams" and gives the cause of death as "Syphilis 2 years".

General paralysis of the insane was syphilis in its last stage, reaching the brain: she and Madams had died of the same disease. In 1901 doctors were already arguing as much; it was proved in 1913.

The informant was her daughter Lilian, who was present at the death.
