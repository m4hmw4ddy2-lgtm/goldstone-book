# A Happy Man: independent audit

23 October 2026. Run from the full local clone, against primary sources only. Your notes, rulings and DO NOT USE list were not used as a standard, and `notes/` and `ChatGPT-archive/` were treated as claims to test. Nine reviewers worked in parallel; their detailed findings are in `A_Happy_Man_independent_appendices/` (files `ind_A` to `ind_H2`). This file is the synthesis. It supersedes the earlier audit where they differ.

## 1. How it was done

- **Scripted checks over the whole text.** 437 quoted strings of three words or more in the 22 chapter files were searched against every transcription, trial-document text and book extract. 415 appear word-for-word (punctuation and case ignored). The other 22 are short headings, ledger entries, or the cases listed in section 3. All 31 weekday-and-date pairs in the text are correct. A verbatim match shows the words exist somewhere in the repo, not that they are attributed to the right witness or paper; that was the reviewers' job.
- **Images.** Reviewers viewed the deposition scans (Ada, Emma Clarke, Cheeseman, Folkard, Wheatley, Gould, the accused's statement), all 11 Old Bailey Proceedings pages, Albert's service sheet, the Lambeth register, the Whitstable censuses, the Madams certificates, Elizabeth's 1906 certificate, the 1879 marriage, the 1871 and 1881 censuses, the Ansell and Stock certificates, the medal roll, and the Broadmoor papers.
- **One independent cold read.** A reader opened only the manuscript, as an agent's reader would.
- **My own spot-checks** of the findings I rely on most (marked ✓ below).

## 2. Verdict in short

The quotation work is unusually clean. Of 415 verbatim matches, the Old Bailey passages agree word for word with the printed Proceedings, and the trial and press chapters (11–16) have no high-severity factual errors. The weaknesses are of four kinds: (a) a handful of hard factual errors, almost all in Parts One and Five; (b) the scene in Chapter 6 and some other passages, which state inference as fact; (c) quotations that are right but blend or pick between witnesses without telling the reader; (d) structure and balance, where the book falls short of its own aim (section 6).

## 4. Stated as fact without a source, or more than the source says

**Chapter 6 (the drowning).** The order of the children, "held them under", the bodies lying together, "conscious", "swallowing", and "no longer than it took Ada to remake the bed" have no source. Ada's Old Bailey evidence says twenty minutes. No document gives an order or an eyewitness account. This is the book's central scene and the biggest exposure.

**Blended scenes (Ch 6–8).** The reviewers' witness-conflict table shows the evening is assembled from Ada's 9 August statement, her coroner deposition, her Old Bailey evidence, Emma, Cheeseman, and press-only lines. Examples:
- ✓ Door words: Ch 6 uses the coroner version, "I am not a wicked man. I am a happy man." Ada's earlier sworn statement has "I have killed the children. I am a happy man now." Ch 6 and Ch 13 also give two different versions of what Elizabeth said at the door ("Open the door" against "Come in; he is murdering my children"), without telling the reader that one witness gave different words on different occasions.
- Press-only dialogue ("Shall I leave the room?") is set as plain speech; the Telegraph and Times text is one reporter's.
- "You wished they were dead, and now they are" matches no source. The caution "What you say will be used in evidence" is direct speech in the chapter and indirect in every source.
- At trial Ada denied that William said anything about the other children; the chapter omits that.
- The babies are in the Hamiltons' front room with no one moving them; Elizabeth is variously in the back bedroom and "the front room".

**Other claims with no source in the repo.**
- Ch 1: Elizabeth's birth registration (the book's first scene, with the quotation "the mark of Thomas Stock, Father"). There is no image or transcription of it in the repo. The date comes from a tree index.
- Ch 3–4: Isabella Chilton "had been a governess", the Chiltons' move to Eltham House and their 1881 servants, William Graves's early life, Susan Newman's 1880 marriage and Clayton Road address, "nine people living in sheds", the plait-trade dates and Hertfordshire figures, Luton rateable value, the White's directory and tithe details, "She had gone into service".
- Ch 4: "no near relatives at all." Thomas Stock's 1827 marriage to Sarah Hockley is in the repo, so he may have had children; Chapters 1–2 themselves name an uncle and a cousin.
- Ch 10: gives Martha as Robert's widow flatly; the twins' coffin-plate names appear against Ch 7's unnamed twins.

- Ch 9, 10: "His first words were 'I am the resurrection…'" (the Guardian gives no wording); the grave "not filled in until three"; "crowds at all three stations" (two named); Emma Clarke's quoted inquest evidence is not in the Guardian or Dover Express (it is in the coroner deposition, image 0032, but the chapter does not say so).
- Ch 15: the Leeds Mercury paragraph's Criminal Appeal Bill dates; Savage's Old Bailey answer quoted without its preceding line, "I do not think it would have influenced his actions".

Ch 8 retains Wheatley "not showy"; the transcription header calls that reading wrong. "Ramble" in Ch 8 is "mumble" in the deposition. Honey's time is ten to six in his deposition, "ten o'clock" in the Guardian. The Cheeseman, Honey, Folkard and other transcriptions were checked against images only in part (the reviewers list the pages they did not open).
- ✓ Ch 5's "wished they were dead … only the twins" is in Ada's coroner deposition (image 0032, folio 31: "he remarked 'I have a very large family' and he wished they were dead. I thought then he referred only to the twins"), so it is sourced.
- Notes errors: the inquest note's "five weeks short of twenty-nine years" is about five months; the BMJ notes say headaches, the "lift" and "wished dead" are in no witness statement and they are; the dramatis note "beer retailer" against the back matter's "later publican".
- Both DNA CSVs hold data for chromosomes 1–9 only, and each headline total equals the sum of those nine. That is truncation, not a tool "undercount". They show nothing about Albert's father.
- The ChatGPT archive asserts an 1888 Madams marriage, Albert as a councillor, and 2 John Street as S. Frost's address. The chapters avoid all three. None is supported by a primary source in the repo.
- Merkulova (2022) has no Albert, Navy or ship content; one paragraph on the case (pp. 199–200). Albert's 1910 marriage certificate names his father "William Goldstone, deceased"; Chapter 21 does not use it.

## 6. Does it meet the stated aim?

"A nonfiction book examining the crime, the trial, and the wider social and legal context, including Victorian attitudes to mental health, criminal responsibility, and the often-overlooked experiences of women like Elizabeth."

- **The crime and the trial: yes.** Chapters 6–8 and 11–14 are the strongest writing. They are over-long on repetition: the same testimony appears five times across Chapters 6–13, and the cord, the hammer, Gould's findings and William's demeanour recur.
- **Criminal responsibility: told, not examined.** M'Naghten, Day's direction and the Savage correspondence are all there. The Trial of Lunatics Act 1883 is never named, "special verdict" is never explained, and nothing shows that the English test lasted another eighty years.
- **Victorian attitudes to mental health: through quotation only.** Chapter 15 is a digest of letters with no argument. Emily's illness, Ann Andrews's and the aunt's appear as trial evidence only. Broadmoor and William's 51 years get about 150 words.
- **Wider social context: patchy.** About 2,500 words of village history in Chapters 1–3 are mostly not tied to the people. The context a publisher would expect (criminal lunacy law, family killing, public hanging in 1883, the poor law and workhouse for Elizabeth) is absent.
- **Women like Elizabeth: weakest.** Elizabeth is 4% of the text (Part Four, 1,325 words of 33,002). She is present in Chapters 6–8 and 15, an object of other people's actions in Part Three, in registers only in Part Four, and absent from Part Five. Her death (29 June 1906) falls in the same week as the Jupiter manoeuvres in Chapter 19, and the book never connects them. The rest of the women (Jemima, Emily, Ann Andrews, Mabel's mother, Ada, Emma Clarke, Martha Graves, Susan Newman) are never threaded into a theme; the Mabel Frances parallel with Elizabeth's own birth, and Susan Newman's Lambeth address, are set up and not paid off.
- **Albert:** the Prologue frames the book as his, and he is a Navy file from Chapter 19. The questions the Prologue raises (Germany, the father, the 1921 census) are never answered on the page.

**Recommended structural changes, in order of effect:**
1. Cut the repetition in Chapters 9–13 to what is new at each hearing (about 3,000–4,000 words) and the village set pieces in Chapters 2–3 to what ties to the people (about 1,500).
2. Label or remove the unsourced reconstruction in Chapter 6, and give each blended scene a source line.
3. Add a medical-legal chapter from the papers already held (23 of about 30 BMJ opinion passages are unused, plus Savage's January 1884 JMS papers, Bucknill, Mercier, Tuke). That delivers the mental-health strand without implied authority. Name the 1883 Act.
4. Expand Part Four with sourced context on the Lambeth workhouse, unmarried mothers, and a convict's wife's legal position. Bring Broadmoor in. Both need new sources; none is in the repo.
5. Write Part Six as the narrator's story and as the place where Elizabeth's thread closes; bring the narrator back (the Prologue's "I" is absent for 20 chapters).
6. Add a note on names (Gouldstone, Goldstone, Goldston), a cast list (too many Williams, Thomases and Roberts), and source notes per chapter. The book has no apparatus.

## 7. Length and market

33,002 words (Prologue 318; One 6,043; Two 3,455; Three 17,302; Four 1,325; Five 4,559). Part Three is 52%. Trade narrative non-fiction normally runs longer: I have not confirmed a figure, and my recollection is 70,000 to 100,000 words, so this reads as short. With a narrator, a Part Six and the additions above it reaches about 55,000. In the editorial reader's view the book is a strong dossier with a strong opening and closing image, but not yet an argued book.
