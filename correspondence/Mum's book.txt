It was a snowy winter’s night…




I came into the world on 19th January 1947, during what would become known as the worst winter of the century. Snow blanketed Chatham for three bitter months. Britain was still catching its breath after the war, with rationing casting its shadow over daily life and families like mine making do with whatever we could get. I was part of the baby boom post-war generation and that fact has coloured the rest of my life.
My parents' love story was woven into the fabric of the war. They met in North Shields in the YMCA. Dad’s ship had moored on the Tyne for supplies. They married on June 10th, 1944 and had a two night honeymoon in the beautiful small Northumberland town of Rothbury. Four days later, D-Day sent thousands of young men across the Channel. Dad, a Petty Officer in the Royal Navy,  was posted to India. Mum stayed behind, finding her own way to serve by continuing her training as a nurse at Preston Grange Hospital in North Shields.
All Saints Hospital in Chatham, Kent, where I was born, was as familiar to my mother as her own home - she'd walked its corridors countless times as a staff nurse, making the daily trek across the Great Lines from Gillingham.
'Home' then was just a bedroom in my grandmother's house It was my father’s room at the top of the family home, which no-one else was allowed to use.  Housing shortages were dire in the post-war years, and like many families, my father’s childhood home was a crowded one, bustling with the sounds of his siblings. He had seven sisters and two brothers, some of whom also still lived at home, although all were in the Services, plus the lodger my grandmother had taken in after her husband had died during the war.
The snow was so thick that my mother and I had to stay in the hospital longer than expected, as travel was nearly impossible. The roads were impassable for a pram, as Gillingham is on the far side of the North Downs to Chatham and the only means of transport for them then was Shanks's pony (on foot)! I never did know who Shanks was, but it was a common expression at the time, so my parents walked from the hospital home to Gillingham pushing the pram. 
My father worked in the stores at Chatham dockyard.  At some time during my first year of life, a workmate (everyone who worked in the ‘yard’ was called a mate or matey) told my father that his mother was having to move from the Victorian terraced house she owned, and that the family had decided to let the house. Was he interested?  Of course, with there being a massive housing shortage at the time my father jumped at the chance, and hence we moved to 23 Herman Terrace in Chatham, the red-brick, Victorian-built, childhood home I clearly remember. 
The first glimpse of our new home revealed a house caught between past and present - the small front room was filled with the previous owner's furniture, like a museum of someone else's life. As a child I used to try to push the door open and peer at what was inside. Dad and Mum had their own space in what we could use: the dining room, kitchen, the three bedrooms upstairs, and an outdoor toilet. The hallway remains etched in my memory - the front doorway, which was reached by two steps up from the front pavement, had an arched fanlight window that cast dancing patterns on the floor below. The front door itself was a piece of Victorian craftsmanship: a lion-headed knocker that seemed to guard our home, the letterbox that brought news from the world beyond, and a substantial black doorknob worn smooth by decades of hands. The hallway had a picture rail and a dado rail, below which was dark brown Anaglypta wallpaper.  The wooden staircase between the front room and the dining room was very dark and steep, and had a carpet runner secured in place by brass stair rods. The ceiling seemed very high to me but I don’t suppose it was! Of course there was no central heating, all the rooms, including two of the bedrooms, had open fireplaces. We burned coal in the grate which was delivered to the dark, spider filled, cellar through a grated window from the pavement outside. The coalman, like the baker and milkman, still drove a horse and cart.




From the age of six months I went to a day nursery.  My mother’s role as a nurse, was considered a “reserved occupation” essential to post-war recovery efforts. I guess that was because a lot of the women who had worked so hard during the war were expected to give up work so that men, returning from war, would have jobs to go to.  As a consequence, my nursery was free to her. My mother was a staff nurse at All Saints Hospital, Chatham when the NHS began in July 1948.  My memories of nursery include a sand tray which I know I loved, scratchy army blankets on little day beds in a dark room where we were all put for a sleep after our midday dinner, and the smell of boiled cabbage. I remember complaining that I did not want to go to sleep, and being sternly told to lay down and go to sleep!  I think the best part of the day, which I clearly remember, was my mother collecting me at the end of the day and putting me on the little wicker seat on the back of her bicycle. And I still don’t like boiled cabbage!
Christmas 1950 brought my first glimpse of London. Just before my fourth birthday, our nursery arranged what felt like an expedition to the heart of our capital - a journey to see the famous Trafalgar Square Christmas tree. None of us children knew then that this towering Norwegian spruce represented something far more profound than seasonal cheer - it was Oslo's annual thank-you gift to Britain for sheltering their king during the dark days of war.. The first Trafalgar Square Christmas tree was a gift from Norway in 1947 and has been given annually every year since then. We were simply spellbound by its twinkling lights and imposing height, our small faces tilted up in wonder. This trip was an amazing treat and an experience I have never forgotten. We went on the train to Victoria from Chatham and then by tube to Trafalgar Square. The tree looked positively enormous to me, and was brightly lit. We were then taken to Lyons Corner House on the Square for tea. My memory is of a black and white tiled floor in this cavernous space and areas cordoned off by red rope in brass holders. I also remember waitresses in black and white uniforms with little white hats on their heads, but I have no idea what I ate or drank!
That following summer in 1951, I left nursery to stay at home with my mother who had left work as she was expecting a baby in the September.  It was a nice summer. On very special days my mother would walk with me to the corner of The Brook in Chatham to wait for my father riding his bicycle home from work.  All the dockyard mateys rode bicycles except for a very small minority who could afford cars! They all wore flat caps too. I was always very excited to see my Dad coming along the road.  He would stop beside us, put his cap on his crossbar and wheel me home with his cap for a cushion. I loved it!  My father often spent his evenings with a friend Mr Morris who was teaching him carpentry and wood turning. His wife (whom I always called Aunty Morris), was my godmother. One day Dad came home with a wonderful wooden rocking horse he had made. It had wool for its mane and a red leather strip of material for the saddle. I rode miles on the horse in our small back yard!
By the time my mother knew she was pregnant, they had purchased our home for the princely sum of £200.00. They even had a small mortgage from the Friendly 
Foresters Society my dad belonged to.  The previous owner’s belongings had been removed from the front room and builders were employed to convert our outside toilet into an internal bathroom. Up until that point we, along with most other families, kept a tin bath in the back yard which was brought in once a week for everyone to bathe in.  I do remember being bathed in front of the coal fire in the winter and being quite excited about it!  My mother would heat the water in our gas boiler, or dolly tub as they were called, and my father would empty buckets of hot water into the bath. As the baby, I always had to be washed first.  I can remember standing naked with my hair already tied up in rags to make ringlets, stamping my feet and refusing to get in the water because it was too hot.  I couldn’t make a fuss for long as my parents were too worried that the water would be too cold for them use after me.  Hot water it was! I loved the original Victorian fireplace which was set with sparkling dark green enamel tiles, the grate and hearth was black-leaded to keep it shiny.
We did not have washing machines then, all the washing was done, always on a Monday, in the dolly tub, water heated by the town gas, and dragged through the water by a dolly stick or scrubbed on the scrubbing board in the sink.  When the bathroom was built, the shape of the kitchen was also changed, a coke boiler was installed and a hot water tank was placed in a newly built airing cupboard.  The tank must also have been an immersion heater as we then had the luxury of hot water from a tap!  I had the small box room above the kitchen and my father had a wash basin installed in my bedroom.  Such luxury, although the only toilet was still downstairs so we kept our potties under the bed!
My sister was born in September 1951. My mother was to give birth in All Saints hospital, and when she went into labour I was taken to stay with Aunty Morris. I’m sure I had no idea why I was going to be ‘on holiday’ with Aunty Morris but I was quite happy to be there.  They lived in a small corner terrace house only quite a short distance from our home, although  I doubt I knew then exactly where it was. I do remember the rooms seemed very tiny to me though, even then. The little front room had two fireside chairs and a large glass cabinet. To me, the cabinet seemed full of ornamental Japanese dolls. I was mesmerised but, of course, not allowed to touch them as they were not toys. We spent the days in the dining room or in the garden. The dining room had a square table in the centre of the room with a red chenille cover. The garden was long and narrow with a large Anderson shelter in the middle.  Like at my grandmothers’ house, we were never allowed to enter it.  Uncle Morris had a very good vegetable patch and some of our vegetables came from his garden. I can still smell the lovely cottage pie Aunty Morris made.  She was also an excellent dressmaker and she let me play with all the off cuts of material. I have memories of Aunty Morris and my mother, whilst she was still waiting for her new baby to be born, sitting in that small dining room embroidering table and tray cloths cloths.
After my sister was born, I remember Mum and I sitting in the kitchen in our own home where it was warm, beside the coke stove with my new baby sister Patsy.  I was fascinated watching Mum feeding her and then washing her in a baby bath full of warm water.  With my doll Janet always by my side, I would carefully wash her in the bath after my sister had her turn. Sadly, some of her painted features began to wash off as did some of her ‘skin’, and I remember she developed a decidedly oatmeal look
I loved my small bedroom, tucked away at the top of the staircase, accessible only through my sister’s room. Patsy’s own room had an open fireplace and my father had made a wooden shutter fitting to cover the actual grate. This meant you could hear the wind  whistle down the chimney on a gusty day. Her old blackout blind had lots of pin holes in it through which the moon or streetlight would shine. Sometimes my young sister would call out to me that she was frightened by what she could hear or things she thought she could see through the blind.  I used to just call back that everything was OK and to go to sleep. Honestly though, she frightened me too and there was no way I was going to get out of bed and investigate!
My parents bought a small pine dressing table with a mirror and a small pine chest of drawers for my room which I still have in our spare bedroom. I loved the mirror which I could see in if I stood on my bed.  In the summer months, when it was too light to sleep I used to stand in the middle of the bed and drape myself in the old, gold, shiny bedspread and act to myself in the mirror!  There was also a Mountain Ash tree outside my window, behind the new bathroom extension. It had lovely red berries in the autumn and I loved sitting on the small chair looking out at the tree, the sky and clouds behind it. I remember, in the summer, sitting beside the open window when it was a lovely balmy evening and watching my mother in the garden talking over the back fence to the neighbours.  I also remember her looking up and catching my watching her and being told firmly to get back into bed! I loved sneaking in some extra reading in bed, staying up as long as I dared without turning the light on.
It was from then on, and I suppose it was the fact that they had become home owners instead of tenants, that my father took enormous pride in improving our home. The cellar became his workshop and I loved being there with him. I probably did not stop talking; wanting to know the names of his tools and what they did.  Dad showed me how to secure a piece of wood in the vice, how to use a hammer and nail, and a small fret saw.  I loved the smell of wood and coal and, providing he was in the cellar with me, I felt safe in the dark shadows cast by the single lightbulb.
Every summer our family went on a one week summer holiday.  We were very lucky as not many working people did then. When I was young, and before we owned a car, we would usually go to a boarding house in Westgate on Sea just outside of Margate, by steam train from Chatham station. Brown Windsor soup always reminds me of the boarding house.  If the weather was fine, and in my memory it usually was, we walked straight down to the sandy beach for the whole day, The ladies who ran their Boarding Houses would only let you to return for your evening meal, you could not stay in the house during the day. I loved the seaside and playing in the water, walking on my hands and pretending I could swim! We built lots of sandcastles with our buckets and spades and every afternoon we would go to the main Margate beach to watch the Punch and Judy show shouting out when we could see the crocodile was going to eat the sausages!
One magical evening I remember so well, after our dinner , probably with Windsor soup as the starter, my mother said she was going to take me out and my father was going to baby sit eighteen month old Patsy. We walked along the seafront together and I realise now my mother would have only been twenty seven years old. After her upbringing and the war years, this was magical for her too. I could not believe where we were when we entered Dreamland.  As we walked down the slope we entered a magic kingdom where the fake (he looked real to me!) Clown in the glass box laughed at us passing. Mum and I went on the wooden scenic railway and I squealed as it climbed the steep hill then roared down the descent. When we left Dreamland to walk back to the Boarding House I was totally amazed as it had started to get dark and all the fairy lights along the promenade had been switched on. I had never before seen anything so wondrous in my life. 




The railway line that bordered our street wasn’t just a noisy source of soot and steam; it was our window to adventure. Each passing train carried the promise of distant destinations and untold stories. The boys were the self-appointed chroniclers of every locomotive, their notebooks filled with carefully recorded engine numbers.
Life in those days was ruled by routine. Mondays were always washing days, and almost every home in Britain followed the same predictable weekly menu. Sunday brought the classic roast beef dinner, followed by cold beef on Monday, and minced beef on Tuesday. Midweek meals varied - Wednesday might be liver and onions (Aunty Bowsher had tripe, yuk!) and Thursday could bring fried kidneys and bacon. My favourite was Toad in the Hole where sausages baked in golden batter became a treat.
Friday was traditionally reserved for fish, though my parents often shopped together on Saturday afternoons at the fishmonger’s. They would bring home fresh herrings, cod roe, or kippers. With fresh crusty bread from the bakers, that made a delicious treat. Cod roe on toast was a particular favourite of mine.
Takeaways were rare and limited to fish and chips, which my parents seldom indulged in, preferring home cooked food. Anything from outside the traditional British diet was dismissed as “foreign muck” and my father would insist “You don’t want any of that!” His experiences in Calcutta, where his ship had been based, no doubt shaped his views. He told us many tales of the conditions there and recounted how the bread was full of bugs that had to be painstakingly picked out before eating. One of his favourite sayings was, “You eat a ton of dirt before you die” and woe betide anyone who didn’t clean their plate.
Our meals were simple but wholesome. We drank water with every meal - fizzy drinks were unheard of - and ate fresh seasonal produce, much of it grown in our own garden. My mother baked current buns for a treat once the rationing ended, alongside her delicious scones and Victoria sandwich cakes. With such a diet, it’s no wonder we grew up slim and fit. Obesity was virtually unheard of in those days.
Facing onto the main Great Southeastern Railways, London Victoria to Margate line it wasn’t just the setting for our home but the playground for countless adventures, from ball games to train spotting regardless of weather. All over Britain, Monday was known as wash day, and I certainly didn’t know anyone who owned a washing machine. They only started to enter people’s homes later in the 1950s and even then you had to have enough money to buy one. Once washed in the dolly tub fired underneath by gas jets, the washing was fed through a mangle to remove the soapy water.  It was then rinsed in cold water, with Robin Blue and Starch often added, then fed back through the mangle before being hung on a washing line in the garden to dry. If it was raining the wet clothes were draped on a clothes horse, often around the fire.
In my childhood, there were only four car owners in the road. We lived in the house near to where the last white car is parked at the top of the road in this photo. Joe’s sweet shop and newsagent was on the corner, and below the railway was the arch where a lot of courting happened, and where, many years later, I would wipe off my make-up before walking up the road home!
Opposite the row of terraced houses was a line of rusty, spiked railings, which followed the railway embankment. In summer the railings were covered in convolvulus and we would ‘pop’ the white flowers from their sepals saying, "Grandfather, grandfather pop out of bed" and laugh at how high we could make grandfather pop!  Fascinatingly, for a child, the railway then disappeared into a dark tunnel under the Great Lines making its way to Gillingham station. The terrace ran uphill and we were very near the top, so the embankment fell away at our end down to the track and also to the signal box.
The railway line itself was a source of constant fascination, as steam trains were still the kings of the tracks in the 1940s and 50s. We would watch the drivers and stokers build up a head of steam with a loud whoosh before the train pulled away as the signal turned to green. We often got a toot from them too!
The road, without traffic, was our playground. Halfway down was a gas lamp, which was as far as I was allowed to go to play when I was young. There was a bar near the top where the gas lighter used to place his ladder to climb up to light the gas mantle. It changed over to electricity in the early 1950s but the bar remained and was frequently used by the local boys who would throw a rope over, so we could use it as a swing! A lot of the local lads used to love to come to our road to train spot. The Golden Arrow and the Bluebell occasionally used that route to great excitement. Train spotters also wrote the numbers of all the trains they had seen in their notebooks and loved comparing notes with each other, competing to see who had spotted the greatest number or most interesting of trains!
We also played a lot of ball games and inevitably the balls sometimes sailed over the railings onto the embankment. They were then lost to us until the grass caught fire and the fire engine came. That was always a very exciting time; the embankment often caught fire in a dry summer because of the sparks from the train engine when it was being stoked. We would line up along the rusty, spiked railings waiting for the fire to be put out and our balls to be found and thrown back to us by the firemen.  “That’s my ball mister” was the excited cry!
Our street games followed the unwritten rules of childhood, passed down through generations. We'd perform handstands against the sun-warmed brick walls, our skirts sensibly tucked into our knickers. If you were determined enough you could then walk your legs down the wall until, with your face looking at the ground, you could walk on hands and feet like a crab across the pavement! The rhythmic thump of balls against walls accompanied our sing-song rhymes, each game a small piece of cultural heritage preserved in playground chants and children's laughter. 'Nebuchadnezzar, King of the Jews' echoed down the street, our voices keeping time with the bounce of rubber balls - until some exasperated neighbour would emerge to angrily to tell us to “Clear off!!”, sending us giggling to the next available wall. One of our favourite two-ball rhymes was


“Nebuchadnezzar, King of the Jews,
Bought his wife a pair of shoes.
When the shoes began to wear.
Nebuchadnezzar began to swear.
When the swearing began to stop,
Nebuchadnezzar bought a shop.
When the shop began to sell,
Nebuchadnezzar bought a bell.
When the bell began to ring,
Nebuchadnezzar began to sing……”

From this point we counted out whatever had been agreed, throwing one of the balls through your legs or over an arm chanting “One, two, three a’leary, my balls down the dairy”! I can’t remember the rest but I am fairly sure it was a counting game..
We also chalked out hopscotch, played marbles in the gutter, or lots of different skipping games. Of course many of us also played with our dollies, taking them for walks in their prams! As well as Janet, I also had a lovely bear, which I still have, made by my Dad’s brother, Uncle Ron. He was made out of grey army blankets and stuffed with straw. On sweltering summer days, the tar in the road melted into a sticky black pool, perfect for dipping sticks and drawing pictures. The tar clung stubbornly to our fingers, but woe betide you if it got on your clothes! I can still remember my hands being rubbed very hard with butter paper by my mother to remove it! I can’t remember anyone ever saying, “I’m bored”!




The Victorian streets of Chatham were a labyrinth of secrets, with narrow alleyways threading between rows of terraced houses. These passages were nature's own forgotten corridors - overgrown with stinging nettles, pungent with the unmistakable musk of unneutered cats, and lined with the remnants of wartime survival.  I loved walking the alleyways and looking in the gardens. Most of the families on our street had small vegetable plots in their back gardens, a holdover from wartime rationing when those "Dig for Victory" campaigns encouraged people to grow their own food, and some still kept rabbits for meat. I preferred admiring all the beautiful flowers, the colours and the smell. We used to collect rose petals and make our own perfume.
My father gave me a small plot of my own at the bottom of our narrow garden. He gave me a few of his seeds to grow things, like my own radishes. I also used to collect the seeds from his flowers and try to grow them in my plot. Dad's gift was more than just a small patch of earth at the bottom of our narrow garden.I loved organising how things should be placed in order, pulling the weeds, and tending to the seeds to watch them grow. During the spring and summer I would check every day, when I returned from school, to see if anything had changed, such an exciting time. About the age of eight I gathered his lupin seeds, sunflower seeds and antirrhinums. I put them into twists of paper and sat outside the back gate, trying to sell them to passers-by for one penny each. Sadly I don’t remember making much money!
Housewives were very proud of their homes, I was repeatedly told ‘cleanliness is next to godliness’. Our clothes were kept spotless even if they were well repaired and quite possibly hand me downs. A lot of soot accumulated on the window sills because we lived next to the steam railway line. At the dirty houses you could write with your finger in the soot on the window sills; that seldom happened in our home! My mother, along with almost all the other women in our street, once a week would wrap her hair in a cloth turban, put on her wrap over pinafore and scrub the door step which led straight on to the pavement. The front pavement was then swept and if it was icy in the winter, my father would sprinkle salt or hot ashes. That happened at most homes and meant everyone could walk safely on the pavement. The only time I heard insurance mentioned in our house was when the man from the Co-op came once a month, to collect my parents’ insurance money, which he entered in a little black accounting book.
The baker, the milkman, and the coalman all still drove carts drawn by horses. The horses that pulled the carts occasionally left droppings in the road. Whoever owned the house opposite would run out with a shovel to collect the manure for their garden.  I even remember disputes arising as to the ownership of the manure, it was so prized! The milkman left a pint of milk for us every day with two pints on Saturday and Sunday. In the early 1950s, fridges were a rarity, and we relied on a cold safe in the cellar to keep things fresh.  The cold safe was made from wood and the door was covered in a wire mesh to try to keep the contents cool and fresh. When the milk turned in the hot summer months my mother would make the most delicious scones with the sour milk.




Mrs. Bowsher - 'Aunty' to me, though not a blood relative - was a living bridge between Victorian England and the emerging post-war world. She was very proud that she had made her way up from being the youngest scullery maid to being a Ladies’ maid. Her life was a testament to the resilience of women who had survived two world wars: from her days 'in service' to her careful preservation of a world rapidly disappearing. She became more than a neighbour - she was a surrogate grandmother, a historian, and a window into a vanishing way of life. My mother remembered that she got to know Mrs Bowsher when the washing line broke and all the freshly washed towelling nappies landed in Mrs Bowsher’s garden.  Every clothes-wash day, all the clean clothes were hung from washing lines which ran the length of the gardens.  The line was attached to a rope which was pulled up a very tall pole and the washing snapped in the wind as it dried.  The washing was often commented upon, as everyone got to view everyone else’s belongings! Was someone ill?  Were there stains on nappies (a great sin!) and what about that underwear! Washing lines and weather forecasts were a regular topic of conversation, with neighbours often predicting rain or snow while rescuing each other’s laundry., “Oh look at the clouds. It’s going to rain” (or snow, or thunder…) Access was often through a neighbour’s back gate to quickly rescue their washing  if they were not home. You knew if they were or not by shouting “Cooee” through the letterbox, and that was often my job!
Aunty was born in the late nineteenth century and when she left school, like very many others of her generation, she was sent to work ‘in service’. She was very proud that she had worked her way up to become a lady’s maid.  She married before the First World War and her husband, George, had been a tailor. I suspect he had his own business because they had bought the house next to ours before he joined the forces. It would have only been about fifty years old then and must have been wonderful for them. Very sadly he was killed in action in the First World War and they did not have time to have any children. Aunty never remarried and the house was always kept immaculately clean, and remained exactly the same. The front room was called the parlour and I remember an aspidistra on a beautiful lace Victorian cloth on a plant stand in the window. The room was only used on Sunday afternoon. Sometimes I joined Aunty for afternoon tea served on the small gate-legged table by the fireplace and we listened to 78 rpm records which were made from shellac, rather than vinyl. They were played on a wind up gramophone and the stylus literally was a small steel needle, I still remember that I loved putting my fingers in the small needle holder as it felt soft and smooth. As for the 78s, I think I liked The Laughing Policeman best and Aunty liked In a Monastery Garden.  Occasionally, after our tea, I was allowed to go to St. Paul’s Church on the New Road with her, for evensong.
All her furniture was Victorian and kept in beautiful condition.  The fire grate was kept black leaded and the brass stair rods polished. Now I look back, almost all the appliances she owned were manual.  There was no electric vacuum cleaner, food mixer or washing machine.  Her cooker was an old gas stove, she had a scrubbed deal table in the kitchen and made the most delicious pastries and cakes.  The ingredients were kept in large stone storage jars in a cupboard in the dining room.  Every few months all her silver cutlery was laid out on a cloth on the dining table and I would help her polish each individual piece. She had beautiful table covers and napkins which were used at every meal, even though the vast majority of the time she ate alone. The napkins were always rolled inside a silver napkin ring.
Aunty must have been in her late sixties when I first knew her. She still worked early mornings, cleaning daily for a family in Rochester who owned a car sales business. It was called Trice’s and was beside Rochester Cathedral. Almost every Saturday afternoon I spent with Aunty whilst my parents and sister would go shopping. We searched the local papers to see if any events were happening nearby that we could either walk to, or go to on a bus. We went to Spring Fairs, Garden Fetes and Christmas Fairs! These outings were as much about the stories Aunty shared as the events we attended, weaving the past into the fabric of my childhood memories. We took the bus to Rochester and visited the Dickens Museum, or we walked around the keep at Rochester Castle passing first through The Vines where Aunty showed me Satis House which Dickens used in his novel Great Expectations. Sometimes we went to the Sun Pier by the river in Chatham. I loved the pier where you looked between the planks and saw the River Medway flowing fast underneath.  A steam pleasure boat called the Medway Queen offered trips to Borstal and as far as Southend from there. I didn’t know it at the time but the Medway Queen was also known as a “heroine of Dunkirk” and had received four awards for gallantry!
In the summer, when the weather was beautiful, we would take picnics to the Coney Banks which meant we walked past the cemetery where there were memorials to people Aunty knew. This actually meant nothing to me at the time, but I did like to visit to see all the well-kept graves and beautiful flowers. I must have thought it was a lovely park! It was certainly well kept by the park keeper with all the edges trimmed and big lead watering cans. I was instructed to walk carefully around the graves and not to step on them; neither was I allowed to pick the flowers! In 2020 the Coney Banks was designated Green Belt land meaning it could never be developed. We also had picnics on the Darland banks, but the trip I loved most was taking the bus, and the obligatory picnic, to the River Medway at Borstal. There I could tuck my skirt in my knickers and paddle in the river,  I thought the little grass and pebble embankment was a lovely beach. I lived in a beautiful area on the North Downs and although Chatham was a very big naval town, we were surrounded by beautiful countryside.
Aunty had a sister, Lena, who lived in Maidstone with her husband Jack.  Of course I called them Aunty and Uncle too. I loved the occasional visits we made to Maidstone, the county town of Kent. Aunty Bowsher and I would walk to Military Road in Chatham where all the buses stood.  From there we would catch the bus to Maidstone which was about 10 miles away. On the way, we travelled over the North Downs and down the very steeple Blue Bell Hill. We eventually stopped outside  Maidstone Prison, a very large, stone built building. I don’t think I really knew what a prison was then and was very intrigued. From there we often walked through the town centre down to the river Medway and sometimes caught another bus. Finally, we had to catch a brown and cream trolley bus to the area where Aunty Lena and Uncle Jack lived. Uncle Jack had been wounded in the Somme in the first world war and lost one of his legs and part of the other.  He was always in his wheel chair.  I can only imagine now what the first world war must have been like for the two sisters.  One sister lost her young husband and the other had a very badly injured husband return home. Lena and Jack were lovely, cheerful people. Another chenille table cloth covered the dining table and Uncle Jack sat in his chair smoking a pipe.  I had never seen a pipe being smoked before or seen the tobacco that I watched him fill his pipe with. Aunty Lena gave me a lovely bluebird toffee tin in patterned gold metal for me to keep all my treasures in.  I still have it today in my sewing box!  Like most people then, they still had an outside toilet but theirs was a real Victorian original one with a long, well scrubbed and smooth, fixed seat the length of the small room over the toilet basin. I was too small to be able to scramble up on top of the seat!  No wonder I still remember it clearly!




My mother had returned to nursing when my sister was about three years old, working two nights a week at All Saints Hospital. Nursing was more than a profession for my mother - it was a calling. My sister started school in September and, possibly because she had never really been separated from her mother, found it very difficult to adjust. I was given the responsibility of taking her to school each day and for quite a few weeks she cried all the way to school every morning. I was almost ten years old and found it difficult to persuade her that everything was fine. My  mother then saw an opportunity to expand her healing reach beyond hospital walls. The District Nurse role was a position that not only meant she could use all her caring and nursing skills, it also gave her more flexibility in her working hours with a young family. When she first started she worked every other weekend, with a day off in the week. Initially she started out on a bicycle in her uniform, and with her nurse’s bag on the back of the bike. Excitingly she also learned to drive!
I was with Aunty Bowsher one summer day having our usual Saturday treat, when Mum and Dad returned home from their Saturday shop. They called out to us over the back fence that they had some exciting news. I can still remember my mother’s face totally wreathed in smiles and her eyes shining. “We've bought a car!' The words burst from her like a celebration, her entire being electric with joy. The car, a silver-grey Austin 8, I still remember the registration DKU 892, was delivered to our front door by the garage. L-plates were donned and my mother commenced her driving lessons. (Have you got the photo of Nanny sitting in the old car with L plates on to put here?  If not, I have one in the book I made her.)Originally she was taken out by Mr Goldstone, who was a neighbour in the street behind us and was an ambulance driver, but then Mum enrolled with the British School of Motoring.  My father decided driving was not for him and so, forever, my mother drove. Once she had passed her test, her life as a District Nurse became much easier and the mileage rate she received, in addition to her salary, made life very different. Mum’s money was always saved in the bank and only my father’s wages were used for us to live on.  My father always said if you wanted something, you must save for it, which they obviously did very successfully as they quickly bought our first fridge in 1956, and a Servis, agitator washing machine. My sister and I, however, were never given pocket money freely—we had to earn it by doing chores.. Although, at that age I was really happy and had everything I wanted. 
When my mother became a district nurse, she also needed a telephone. The telephone arrived - a sleek black bakelite marvel from Kent County Council, perched beside our second hand radiogram in the dining room. Every personal call though was a careful accounting, each conversation weighed against the precious pennies it would cost. We were learning the cost of connection in an era when communication was still a carefully measured luxury and we had to record and pay for any private calls we made.  This was also very exciting as we no longer had to walk to the public telephone box at the Luton Arches to call Aunty Florrie, Dad’s sister, to ask if we could visit them where they lived in West Malling. To use the public telephone, in the big red cast-iron box, you had to put two large old pennies in the slot which took the money. You then dialled the number of the person you wanted to call (West Malling 2258!) and when they answered you very quickly pressed button A. You heard your money drop further into the machine, and you were connected.  If there was no answer you would press button B and your coins were returned. If you didn’t have any money you could dial 0 for the operator and ask to make a reverse-charge call. They would then dial the number you gave them, from their switchboard and you could hear the phone being answered and the operator asking if the recipient would accept a reverse-charge call from you.  If the answer was ‘yes’ the operator said ‘you are now connected’ and you could start your conversation. I actually don’t remember ever ringing anyone else from the public call box, I don’t think we knew anyone else that had a phone!
The phone was installed so that mum could receive her daily patient list from the doctor’s surgery and also report back any messages about the patients condition. The phone line was a shared party line which meant, when you first picked up the receiver, you could hear the other user’s conversation.  If it happened to you, you could always tell when the line was picked up.  You either just said ‘line busy’ and they would put their receiver down or you might just say, if it was you trying to make the call, ‘sorry’ and put it down. A few years later we received our own line, Medway 41980!




I remember I was travelling by train when I was quite young and talking to another family about where they lived.  They told me they lived in London and I said that it must be wonderful to live in London and how I wished that I lived somewhere famous. Little did I realise that my hometown’s history was just as remarkable, filled with its own stories of courage and ingenuity. The father asked me where I lived, and I replied “Chatham”. He then told me that Chatham was very famous and that Nelson’s ship the Victory had been built in Chatham dockyard. I was quite thrilled by that, and also that the Royal Marine Barracks were there and the Royal Engineers had a famous barracks in Brompton, near where the main gate to the dockyard used to be. At carnival time the band of the Royal Marines used to march through the town, and we could tell when ships were docked in from far-flung parts of the world by the different uniforms and hats the sailors wore as they walked around the town.  The Medway Towns were more than a place to live - they were a microcosm of post-war Britain, a landscape of the river and the North Downs. Where history breathed through every pavement and every modest terraced house. Things were starting to change.Educating Eileen, the fifties




January 1952 marked my grand entrance into the world of education. At five years old, I could barely contain my excitement that first morning as my mother wheeled my baby sister in her pram toward All Saints’ Primary.  Before leaving, I had said goodbye to Janet and tucked her up in the little blue dolly’s cot in my bedroom - a small ritual that helped bridge the familiar world of home with the unknown adventure ahead.
I entered the school through the door into the infants’ section of All Saints Primary and Infant school in Magpie Hall Road, Chatham, and was handed over to my first teacher, Miss Sharpe. The classroom teemed with children - a sea of faces that reflected the post-war baby boom. We were everywhere, forty or more of us squeezed into each classroom, the practical result of what would later be called the baby-boom generation. The sheer number of us shaped everything about our education, from the way teachers managed their classes to how resources were shared.
The classroom was fairly large, with high windows down one side, and partitioned from the neighbouring one by a folding wooden door. Under the windows were cupboards and in one corner stood a beloved sand tray. Pictures of the alphabet adorned the walls, and a large stove with a fire guard stood to keep us warm in winter. The teacher’s desk was tucked into a corner  right by the stove and in another corner the blackboard rested on an easel. We all sat at little wooden, oblong tables and everyone had their own slate, a piece of chalk and a slate rubber. The entrance hall housed a large cloakroom, where we each had a peg to hang our coats. I didn’t like that cloakroom; it was dark and crowded there seemed to be too many coats, and too many people, a little overwhelming. 
The toilets were out in the schoolyard, with no hand basins, but having to raise a hand and ask to be ‘excused’ caused no problems. Most of us were well used to finding our own way to toilets outside our homes anyway. I remember puddles on the floor where a hand had not been raised in time, and little ones crying at being left by their mothers. Luckily, I was used to school settings, having attended nursery beforehand.
My second month at school coincided with a moment of profound national importance. On February 6th 1952, the death of King George VI transformed our usually bustling classroom into a scene of solemn ceremony. The announcement came amid thunderous gun salutes that echoed across Chatham from both naval and army barracks - my first experience of how national ev The sounds of those salutes, so close to our military town, seemed to underscore the gravity of the moment. We all had to stand up and repeat ‘The King is Dead. Long live the Queen’. At the age of five, we hadn’t a clue what we were saying or why, but it left an indelible mark on my memory. 
Easter brought my first real school crisis. What should have been a simple craft project - making baskets from coloured card - became a moment of pure childhood panic. In my eagerness, I’d rushed ahead, folding and cutting without proper care, only to watch in horror as my basket refused to take shape. The realisation that I’d ruined my project felt catastrophic in the way that only a five-year-old can experience disaster. Distraught, I raised my hand and asked to be ‘excused’, collected my coat, and ran all the way home, alone! My mother, washing blankets by hand in the kitchen sink, was shocked to see me tumbling through the door, tears streaming. I was very upset and told her what had happened.  She really was brilliant.  She dried her hands, and my tears, and said we would need to go back to the school and that she would take me, along with my baby sister in her pram. The teacher was lovely and gave me fresh materials to try again. I have never forgotten that lesson, and maybe that has helped me to help other little ones in my turn. It must have been very difficult for the teacher to help everyone with such a large class of over forty children, and we all did everything at the same time in those days.
In September 1952 most of our class all moved up to make way for the new children starting school. Everyone started school at the beginning of the. term after their 5th birthday.  I was very excited to receive my first ‘Janet and John’ book, marking the start of learning to read. Friday afternoons became special, as we could choose puzzles or games from the toy cupboard; I loved jigsaw puzzles. We were also allowed to have the games out for wet playtimes, when we were not allowed to go into the playground.  We were given pencils for writing.
Every day we all received a third of a pint bottle of milk. In summer, the birds pecked the bottle tops to get to the cream; in winter, the milk froze, and the crate had to be thawed by the stove, often resulting in sour-tasting first gulps.
Childhood health was a priority in those post-war years: before the age of five, we received free orange juice with no added sugar, a bottle of cod liver oil and a large jar of black malt both to be taken by the teaspoon, and there were drinking fountains in our playground which we all loved to drink from. As there was still some post war rationing then, it did help to keep all the children healthy and slim. The nit nurse came every term, and we had to queue up outside the first-aid room to have our hair checked for head lice. Our ears were also examined for wax and, if they weren’t clean enough, the nurse would often quip, “Goodness, I could grow potatoes in there!”  It was both mortifying and amusing to hear. Additionally, our teeth were inspected, and if further attention was needed, you were given a letter for your parents to make an appointment with the school dentist. I dreaded the dentist - his surgery was large and intimidating, painted in a stark green with sinks under the windows. The chair with its attached looming equipment seemed designed to instil fear. Fortunately, I never required gas, but the tales from classmates who did were enough to terrify anyone. Those poor children often came back pale and were promptly sick!
The dental surgery had no time for crying or distressed children. In hindsight, it seems likely that, in our area, a lot of the dentists were ex-services and would brook no problems from children!  The dentists were all paid by the NHS for the number of treatments carried out. I have a suspicion that the teeth of many children suffered from dentists who saw them as pound signs rather than patients!
From an early age, we were also taught the importance of saving money, though I didn’t fully understand it at the time.  The school issued us small National Savings books as a part of a government initiative, a reflection of the country’s efforts to recover from the enormous debt incurred during WWII.  Each Monday morning we were asked if we wanted to buy a National Savings stamp to stick in our books.  When Prince Charles was born commemorative stamps bearing his photo were issued, priced at two shillings and sixpence (half a crown) - a significant amount then - so very few children bought them, as their parents couldn’t afford to give that much money.  Later, stamps marking Princess Anne’s birth were issued at a more modest sixpence, which  I was occasionally given money to buy.
In 1955, at the age of eight, my parents took me to open my first bank account at their bank, the Trustee Savings Bank in Chatham High St. Depositing the princely sum of twelve shillings I felt incredibly proud - especially as many adults still didn’t have bank accounts, with most wages distributed in cash envelopes during the weekly wages round where they worked.
As I advanced to the next class, taught by Miss Webb, I found myself in a spacious room with plenty of windows that let in a wonderful light.  This class introduced us to music lessons, a joy that left a lasting impression on me. We listened to ‘Music for Schools’ on the radio, including Prokofiev’s Peter and the Wolf, where each character was represented by a different instrument. I was fascinated by the story telling through music! Once a week we brought out musical instruments from the cupboard, though we weren’t allowed to choose which ones we played. I remember the triangle being the least popular, while the cymbals - with their dramatic crashing sound - were the most coveted. So it was probably just as well we could not choose. Music and dancing became a passion for me, and I began dreaming of becoming a ballerina. 
After school we sometimes played in my friend Rona’s garden on Sturla Road.  I loved taking dressing-up clothes from our airing cupboard at home out to play; including my mother’s wedding dress! We would sing popular songs of the time, with Around the World I Searched for You a particular favourite.
In my next class with Mr Cornwall, I asked if I could put on a show for the class with my friends. The room was quite small for such a large class, so space for pretend ballerinas was limited. Nevertheless, we took it very seriously and must have looked ridiculous, but Mr Cornwall was the perfect teacher and audience. He graciously thanked for our effort, though I can only imagine the stories he had to share about it at home!




On the night of January 31, 1953 a powerful storm struck the East Coast continuing into the next day. The North Sea flooded, breaching coastal defences and the sea wall in 1,200 places. Over 300 lives were lost on land in Britain, and the devastation caused widespread property damage which must have been particularly distressing for a country still recovering from war. I remember the storm so clearly.  Our home wasn’t spared -  damaged by slates flying off the roofs, and water pouring through the ceiling of my parents’ bedroom. I was only six years old, and the howling wind rushing down the chimneys and into our bedrooms terrified me. The pouring rain and the noise of slates sliding down the roof and smashing onto the pavement below felt like an invasion. When I peered into the loft cupboard in my parents’ bedroom I was horrified to see nothing but open sky.
Fortunately our roof was repaired fairly quickly although I imagine my parents must have had to sleep downstairs until the work was completed. I still remember my father redecorating their bedroom once everything was fixed.  Two days after the storm,  he went with his friend Don Maylam to see the damage along the Kent coast. Don had a motorbike with a sidecar, and I remember being fascinated by it.
The second of June 1953, was Coronation Day for Queen Elizabeth II, and it brought a different kind of excitement. That morning we visited Don Maylam again, this time at his home in Gillingham, because they had a television. Since we didn’t have a car, Don came to pick up my mother and me in his motorbike’s sidecar.  I was thrilled to ride in it - it felt like a grand adventure. Very few people owned televisions then, and most tried to find someone who had one to watch the grand event.  The pictures were, of course, in black and white and the screen was tiny, surrounded by a large walnut cabinet. It was all so thrilling!
We arrived home late after lunch and I had a mad dash to put on my pretty blue dress with puffed sleeves and pictures of the coronation carriage and soldiers around the bottom hem. Unfortunately, it rained during the street party, but I still loved wearing that dress. I often wonder what happened to it. At school, we were each given a coronation beaker, which I used for years as my toothbrush mug.
My father was staunchly anti-monarchy and chose not to watch the coronation, but he had a deep love for new electrical inventions.  He would send for crystal radio sets  advertised in the national newspapers, spreading all the parts out on the dining table to assemble them.  I was utterly fascinated, watching as he pieced together the components with meticulous care. 
While the coronation stirred national enthusiasm for television sets, it also piqued my father's interest. In 1954, just a year later, we got our first television. The wooden frame was a dark polished brown and the screen was set in a cream-coloured surround. The set was installed in our dining room, the hub of our daily lives. On Sunday afternoons or special occasions, we would use the front room, or parlour instead. Ever resourceful, my father drilled a hole in the wall between the two rooms, so that the television could be moved into the front room and the aerial cable fed through.
We had a large H-shaped aerial installed on the roof, securely attached to the chimney breast. When ITV began broadcasting on the 22nd September 1955, it was initially only available to viewers in London. However, Chatham might have been close enough to pick up the signal, as at some point some homes began sporting the X aerial which meant they could access ITV as well. Despite the excitement of this new channel, my father steadfastly refused to have ITV in our home until many years later, sticking exclusively to the BBC. I’ve always wondered why - perhaps it was a touch of snobbery, though I have no idea why that would be!
When I was small, one of my favourite spots was the shelf under the bookcases my father had built in the alcove next to the fireplace. I would squeeze myself into the gap beside the radio, hugging it for warmth as the glowing tubes radiated heat. The back of the radio fascinated me - the light from the tubes sparkled like so many tiny stars!  It was a Kolster Brandes radio encased in dark wood, with a satisfyingly tactile knob on the front that I would happily twiddle to pick up different stations. Many of the broadcasts were in languages I didn’t understand accompanied by a great deal of of what I was told was static noise. Still I was enthralled.
I especially loved Children’s Hour on the radio and Uncle Mac on Saturday mornings. The music delighted me too, and whenever I visited Aunty Bowsher we would listen together to Workers’ Playtime.
The bookcase housed my father’s collection of books, which ranged from physics and engineering texts, to more eclectic choices. The ones I was most interested in were the “Teach Yourself” books on French and Esperanto. When I asked my father about the French book, he told me that, before the war, he had visited France with friends and had witnessed the blackshirts march into Paris. My father was deeply left-wing, which explained the Esperanto book. He believed the world would be much more peaceful if everyone shared a common language.
Sadly neither of my parents realised how much I would have benefited from learning the French my father knew then.  Hindsight, as they say, is a wonderful thing!




As a toddler, I was taken to Sunday school at the Ebenezer Congregational church in Chatham by two young sisters who lived at the bottom of our road. Sunday school took place in the afternoon, after our traditional roast dinner and listening to Two Way Family Favourites and The Billy Cotton Band Show on the radio.  I think my parents were pleased to have a little bit of time to themselves! The sisters led me to the baby class, where I was enrolled on to the Cradle Roll. 
Over the years, the church’s social life became an important part of my childhood. I enthusiastically joined in the various children’s activities, which brought a sense of belonging and excitement.  The first major event I can recall was the annual spring concert put on by the church. The main hall transformed into a proper theatre, with decking for the seats and a fully-equipped stage with curtains and lighting.  It was magical and I loved every moment of it!
One year, nine of us girls aged five to seven, dressed in our best frocks with ribbons tied in big bows in our hair, were part of the opening act. We sang a song after the curtain rose, and I vividly remember in the moments before stepping onto the stage. Behind the curtain there was a buzz of whispering, shushing, and peeping through gaps to spot familiar faces in the audience. The thrill was palpable! 
The second act that evening was particularly memorable. An older boy and girl performed the humorous song There’s a Hole in my Bucket. We all got to join in on the final line, affirming that, indeed, there still was a hole in the bucket. The audience dissolved into laughter and the joy of that moment stayed with me for years to come.

Every summer, the Sunday school held a sports day on Chatham Football Ground, a much-anticipated event that brought together teams from all the local churches to compete for a cup. It was a day of fun and laughter, and naturally, I went along with the group from the Ebenezer Congregational Church.
The adult members provided wonderful refreshments for everyone and we eagerly tucked into paste sandwiches, egg sandwiches, homemade cakes, and orange juice.  The day was packed with traditional races like the egg and spoon, sack, and three-legged races, along with high jump, and triple jump competitions. Back then, the triple jump was simply called the hop, skip and jump! Thinking back, the sun always seems to be shine in my memories, though I’m sure it didn’t. But the kindness of the adults and the cheerful atmosphere ensured that we children were always happy.
The church also had a Girl Guide company and, when I was seven and a half, I joined the Brownies which met very early on Friday evenings. We gathered around a papier-mâché toadstool to sing songs, and the group was divided into four Brownie Patrols.  I was in the Little People patrol. Our leaders, Brown Owl and Tawny Owl guided us through games and activities, and we worked hard to earn badges that we proudly displayed on our sleeves.
One year I went on a Brownie Pack Holiday at a boys boarding school in North Kent, possibly near the River Darenth. My memories of the trip are dominated by two things: the horrible smell of the boys’ toilets and the lack of hot water! On the brighter side, the creamy fresh milk delivered daily from a local farm was a revelation.  I had never tasted such delicious milk on my cornflakes, and haven’t since!
Every week we paid 2d in subs for Brownies which my mother gave me.  But there was one small problem: on my way home,  I passed a fish and chip shop that sold bags of batter scratchings for 2d! The temptation was too much to resist. Occasionally, I would give in, claiming I had “forgotten” my subs and promising to pay double the following week. Raising an extra 2d during the week was tricky,  so I had to dip into my threepenny pocket money to make up the difference.  That left me with just 1d to spend at Joe’s sweet shop. Looking back, I think it was these weekly visits to our corner shop that planted the seed for the idea of my own children having their “sweets for the week” all those years later!




When I was eight, I moved into the primary section of the school. Gone were the toys we used to play with - this was a new, more grown-up world! My first teacher was Miss Pitt, a very strict but effective guide though this next stage of education. In her class we learned joined-up writing and how to use an ink pen. The pens themselves were quite simple – a wooden shaft with a nib at the end, ready to be dipped into ink.
The transition to ink felt like a rite of passage. Pencils were a thing of the past, and  now we had to master these temperamental tools that seemed ready to blob, splatter, or smear at the slightest provocation. Each desk had an inkwell, carefully filled before class by the ink monitor. Thank goodness I was never called upon for this messy duty! Each morning, the ink monitor would make their rounds, carefully filling our desks’ inkwells with what often felt like liquid mischief. Along with our pens we were also given sheets of lined paper and blotting paper, which saw plenty of use as we navigated the tricky art of handwriting. Learning joined-up writing wasn’t just about forming letters; it was an exercise in patience and coordination, and often a lesson in accepting that perfection rarely survived contact with an ink nib.
Fortunately, we did not have to use ink every day. For my tenth birthday I was lucky enough to receive my very own fountain pen. Even then, fountain pens didn’t have the convenience of ink cartridges - they still had to be manually filled from the inkwell, a delicate task that felt both grown-up and perilous.
Outside the classroom, the playground was alive with skipping games, races, and rounds of jacks played with small stones and a ball. Lunchtimes were doubly special for those of us who lived nearby. Like many of my classmates, I was close enough to be able to go home for dinner. My mother would be there with my sister while my father cycled home from the dockyard every day for his dinner too. Since he only had an hour, everything had to be timed to perfection. The dockyard’s  hooter, that could be heard all over the town, marked the clocking on and off times. At school that same midday hooter signalled the end of class, prompting a quick dash home for a hot meal.  The walk back at the end of the day was always much more leisurely, and a time to chat with friends and wend our way through the different alleyways home.
Another significant milestone when I moved up to primary school was learning to swim. This meant embarking on weekly Friday afternoon pilgrimages to Rochester, where the “baths” - as everyone called them then - sat in the shadow of the ancient castle beside the Medway. The setting was as imposing as our instructor, Mr. Barker, who carried himself with a distinct military bearing, a remnant of his service days.In his navy blazer adorned with a mysterious emblem and that ever-present red lanyard with its whistle, he was a commanding figure. The whistle wasn’t just for show; it maintained strict order among the ranks of shivering children braving the challenges of the frigid open-air pool.
The water was bone-chilling, especially when we began our lessons at Easter! Despite this, I adored being in the water and was desperate to learn to swim. Mr. Barker seemed to recognise the ones who really tried and offered plenty of encouragement. His approval spurred me on, and eventually I became skilled enough to represent All Saints School in the summer schools swimming gala. 
The gala was an exhilarating experience. I competed in a length crawl and had to dive in to start. I fought madly and to my amazement, I won! I doubt there was much elegance or style to my performance, but that didn’t matter in the moment. While it was an inter-school competition, meaning there were no individual medals or trophies to take home, the sheer joy and pride of that victory stayed with me. It was a day I’ll never forget.




I had three particular girlfriends at school who made those years especially memorable. One was Jean, who lived in Henry Street on the other side of Magpie Hall Road. Many families in her neighbourhood still kept rabbits in hutches in their garden, fattening them up for rabbit pie. To me their homes always seemed very cosy, though a few years later, after we’d all moved on from that school, the houses were all demolished and replace with new flats.
Another close friend was Jaqueline. Every Saturday morning the two of us would take the bus together to Rochester Casino for roller skating. I adored skating even though I never did get the lovely white boots I always dreamed of. Instead I made do with the skates you clipped on to an old pair of sandals! My lovely godmother, Aunty Morris, was a wonderful needlewoman and she made me a black satin circular skating skirt. I loved it so much that  I’d twirl on my skates just to show off, knickers flashing, feeling like the bee’s knees! I could skate fast backwards, and even balance on one foot with the other leg up like a ballet dancer.  Goodness, I must have looked like quite the show-off, but all I only remember is how happy I felt and how much I loved those moments.
Jacqueline and I made that journey to the skating rink almost every Saturday, on our own even though we were only nine and ten years old. At Jaqueline’s tenth birthday party I met her cousin Colin during a game of Postman’s Knock. Colin turned out to be my very first boyfriend but I’m sure we had no idea about such things, and it couldn’t have been more innocent.  Colin even came to call for me one Saturday afternoon, asking my mother if I could go to the afternoon pictures with him. He brought along a small box of Milk Tray as a treat.  I have no memory of the film we saw, though I suspect it was one of those Saturday cowboy flicks. I don’t remember seeing Colin again after that, though I know that he passed the 11+ and went on to Sir Joseph Williamson’s Mathematical School in Rochester. Many years later, I met up with him again, but that’s a story for another chapter.
My other good friend was Sonia, who lived near the butcher’s shop. I think she might have been an only child and she had the most amazing russet-coloured hair in beautiful ringlets, always tied up with ribbon. Perhaps I was a little star-struck!  Sonia also had the most wonderful dolls, with real hair you could brush. Together, we’d push them up and down her garden path in her doll’s pram, complete with frilled satin pillows and covers. It all felt so glamorous to me at the time.




On the corner of Herman Terrace stood Joe’s newsagent, a treasure trove for sweet-toothed children since sugar rationing only stopped on 4th February 1953. Sweets were very special and some cost less than a penny. With a farthing I could get four blackjacks or fruit chews and sometimes Joe would even weigh out a penny’s worth of loose sweets or sherbet for you. My father also made sure I had a magazine every week so I felt especially lucky. I started off with Sunny Stories which I absolutely loved. I have only found out in very recent years, Sunny Stories was written by Enid Blyton and that was my father’s choice for me!
My father took the News Chronicle every day, and I believe that once a week it featured a column by Big Chief I-Spy. I was captivated by that column and begged for every I -Spy book I could get my hands on. Completing them became a mission.  Once a book was filled and signed off by an adult, you could send it to the newspaper and receive a certificate and a coloured feather in return.  The idea was to collect enough feathers to create a full headdress, though I don’t think I ever managed to collect nearly enough.
One particular memory stands out: I invited Big Chief I-Spy to my tenth birthday party. To my amazement, he sent me a nice reply saying he hoped to come. I spent most of my party waiting eagerly for his arrival but, of course, he didn’t appear. I was heartbroken and learned a big life-lesson about managing my expectations that day. I think my enthusiasm for the I-Spy books waned not long after that, though I still look back on that time fondly.
As a child, one of my regular tasks was to do the shopping for my mother. She would hand me money and send me to to John’s the butcher to buy a rib of beef, which cost about 7/6d (about 38p in today’s money). John’s shop was fascinating to me - every cut of meat proudly displayed and, on the back counter, he made his own sausages using a sausage machine that seemed magical.  At the end of each day, the shop floor was meticulously swept and scrubbed, with fresh sawdust sprinkled down for the next morning. Woe betide me if I did not bring the correct change home with my neatly wrapped parcel of meat!
For vegetables, I would visit Mr Mead’s the greengrocer, which operated from the front room of the house he shared with his mother. The room was modest but practical, with boxes of produce stacked on the floor of the front room. Mr. Mead would weigh out the potatoes and greens, then tip them into the bag I brought from home.
For general groceries we went to the Co-operative store next to my school.  This shop was an institution of its own, with barrels of dried peas, tea, and sugar. Sugar remained rationed until 1953, and meat rationing ended the following year, in 1954. Butter and cheese were cut to order, weighed, and carefully wrapped, as was the bacon. The white wrapping paper they used was simple but elegant, and I would carefully unfold it at home to use as drawing paper - a small but cherished repurposing. The payment process at the Co-op was a source of endless fascination. All transactions were conducted in cash, and customers had to quote their dividend number, known as the “divi”, which was recorded. The note and the money were placed in a small wooden pot, then screwed closed and sent up in a chute that whisked it away to an upstairs office at the back of the shop.  Moments later,  the change would clatter back down the chute in the same pot. For a child, it felt like magic!




The only time I can recall being ill as a child was just before my seventh birthday in 1954. Apart from a smallpox inoculation, there were no vaccinations for children back then, so when I caught whooping cough, it was a real ordeal. I vividly remember my mother making kaolin poultices which she heated under the grill before placing them, still very hot,  on my chest.  I hated those poultices and made a terrible fuss every time. Once I began to recover, my mother made me stand on the cold back doorstep telling me to take in deep breaths of fresh air.  Looking back now, I realise there was probably a lot of air pollution around at the time, thanks to  all the coal fires and steam trains. Though we wouldn’t have even known the term air pollution back then.
No sooner had I returned to school then I caught chickenpox.  Compared to the misery of whooping cough, that didn’t seem too bad, but it did mean another period of illness and recovery.
Polio was the next big, disabling disease to sweep the country. The first major epidemic in the UK had occurred in 1947 and returned each summer until the early 1960s, affecting thousands of children each year. We were told not to play in our local River Medway because polio was said to be carried in the water. The worst summer in the UK was 1956, a year after the first polio vaccine was discovered in the US, by Jonas Salk. In the summer of 1957 my mother took my sister and I to the children’s clinic, the same one that housed the dreaded dentist.  My sister, only five years old at the time, screamed and fought so hard that she had to be physically restrained.  I found the whole experience very upsetting, but it was deemed necessary, and we got through it.  My mother tried to make the day special by packing a picnic and, on the way home, we stopped at the the Coney Banks.  The long grass was seeding, and the sun bright and strong - a lovely day in theory, but not for me. I began sneezing uncontrollably, my eyes stung and watered, and I couldn’t stop rubbing them. This was my first experience of hay fever.
My mother, unsure what was happening, took me to the doctor the following day. I was diagnosed with hay fever, and the doctor suggested there might be a link to the vaccination,  though it was more likely that  the allergy had been dormant in me all along. I had to visit the optician, who prescribed National Health sunglasses.  I hated their pink plastic frames and only wore them when the situation was dire.




I feel very fortunate to have grown up as a child in the 1950s. It was a time of optimism, and unparalleled freedom for children, offering a sense of independence that became rare in the years that followed. After starting school, it only took one day for me to earn the trust to go on my own, twice a day. I could roam freely in my small world, which expanded far beyond the lamppost half way down our street.
From our back gate, which opened onto Cromwell Terrace, I would turn right into Hartington Street. There, the remnants of two bombed-out houses from the Second World War had become a makeshift, and rather hazardous, playground.  Over the preceding five or six years the locals had been using the crumbling gap as a convenient point to leave their discarded rubbish, broken prams and old furniture. Behind it rose the hillside, known as Chalk Pit, a natural extension of our adventurous playground. For thrills we’d drag pieces of discarded furniture to the top and use them to slide recklessly down the chalk face into the bombed-out gap between the houses!  All our homes were built on the chalk of the North Downs, and beyond them, wild land stretched out, dotted with hawthorn - the beloved May-tree. We would pick the beautiful white blossom and pretend to be brides or fairies, weaving the magic of childhood into every game.
I fully believed in fairies and the power of wishes.  I read lots of fairy stories and I was certain, if you wished hard enough your dreams would come true. Beneath the hollyhocks in the garden, I created a little fairy nest, waiting for my dreams to take flight; chief among them to have a bicycle of my own. 
One evening in the spring of 1957, my father returned home from work telling me he had a surprise for me. He led me to the garage they had had built at the end of the garden, and there it was -  a beautiful black ladies’ Raleigh bicycle, with Sturmey-Archer gears!  I have no idea where it came from, but my father lovingly dismantled and rebuilt it to fit me perfectly. Learning to ride became a shared adventure between my father and me. We would walk up the hills to the Great Lines, where he’d push and carry the bike all the way. Time and again he held on to the saddle as I tried to pedal on the grass banks, offering encouragement and steadying me until I found my balance. We must have repeated that journey at least three times until he felt confident enough to let me ride on the road. I loved it! The roads I the 1950s were wonderfully quiet. There were only three cars on our street: Mr. Allan’s taxi, Mr. London’s car (he worked a white-collar job at the dockyard), and my mum the district nurse. The only other vehicle was a van owned by Mr. Rate, the handyman. With my new bicycle, the world suddenly seemed to open up, and I loved the newfound sense freedom to explore.
That summer my schoolfriend Myrna Laws and I made the most of our bicycles. After tea, we would rush out to explore the countryside in the Luton area of Chatham. At the time, the only new buildings were the prefabs constructed as emergency housing after the war. Beyond them lay open countryside and endless hop fields. In late summer, families from East London would arrive to pick hops from the vines adding a lively bustle to the serene landscape. I loved the feeling of riding through the open fields, surrounded by the sights and smells of  the countryside, knowing it was just a short ride from home.




At the age of ten, in my last year at primary school, the frigid days of open air swimming in Rochester were finally behind me.  The Medway District Scouts had had an indoor pool built at Buckmore Park in Chatham. The water was blissfully warm, making our Friday afternoon swimming lessons something to look forward to, rather than endure. Of course there was a catch, on Friday mornings we had our weekly spelling test, and we were told that unless we scored a perfect ten, we wouldn’t be allowed to swim. I’m not sure how much truth there was to that threat, but I wasn’t willing to take any chances - I always made sure I was ready to dive into that pool!
Buckmore Park wasn’t just a place to practice swimming; it was where I learned to dive, starting cautiously with the low board before ambitiously making my way up to the top.  Tales of disastrous belly flops - where you could supposedly split - circulated among us, ensuring I took great care to perfect my form. With my arms outstretched and hands breaking the water first, I managed a reasonably decent dive.It may not have been polished enough to earn me a spot representing the school, but the sheer sense of achievement was enough to make me proud. 
Reflecting on those days, I feel deeply grateful for the solid foundation of education I received at All Saints School. Despite the lingering effects of the war, which meant that not all of our teachers were formally qualified, they were deeply committed to our learning. We were all encouraged, supported, and, most importantly, loved.
When I was eleven and in Class One, we had the privilege of being taught by the best teacher of them all, Mr Clarke. A former RAF pilot who had been captured as a prisoner of war, he carried a quiet authority and a deep commitment to his students. It was under his guidance that our entire class of 50 children sat the 11+ exam. On that day, the rest of the school had been sent home, leaving us alone with the task that seemed shrouded in mystery and importance.
What we didn't realise was that the weekly spelling and arithmetic tests we had been diligently completing, and the termly reading and comprehension exercises taken individually at Mr. Clarke’s desk, had all been preparing us for this moment Despite the buildup, as children, our understanding of the exam’s true significance was limited.Most of us didn’t grasp what passing or failing would mean for our futures, though there was no shortage of chatter about it among us. For some, the stakes felt higher, as parents dangled the promise of rewards for success—bicycles, watches, and other coveted treasures. I remember broaching the subject with my father one afternoon over dinner, and asking if I might get something if I passed. He  paused, looked directly at me with his piercing blue eyes and replied, “Lets wait and see first shall we?”.  His response left me rather glum. I couldn’t go back to school after my dinner and  join in with my classmates’ gloating over potential prizes, but, of course, he was quite right. That moment has stayed with me, a small but meaningful lesson in earning recognition for its own sake.
The 11+ exam loomed over us in early spring, casting its shadow across our final year at primary school. Having just turned eleven that January, I approached the test with a blend of innocence and curiosity. In hindsight, that mindset probably served me better than the anxious preparation that consumed some of my classmates. While many saw the exam as a dreaded hurdle, I found myself oddly at ease with the challenge. The questions seemed like familiar friends, not unlike the puzzles I'd always enjoyed. It is strange to reflect on how that single morning quietly drew a sharp line through our collective futures, separating us in ways we couldn’t yet comprehend. At the time, none of us truly grasped the weight of its significance. My love of reading undoubtedly played a role in how I approached the exam. I spent countless hours in the children’s department of our local library, immersed in the worlds of books. The librarian had become so accustomed to seeing me and my class friend Richard that she occasionally let us help with tasks like sorting returned books, and even stamping some of the outgoing ones.  Those small responsibilities made me feel wonderfully grown up. That library, built by George E Bond was more than a place to borrow books; it was a cornerstone of my childhood. Its eventual demolition by Medway council in the late 1980s was nothing short of tragic - a loss not just of bricks and mortar but of memories and opportunities for future generations.
A couple of months after the 11+, just before the Easter holidays, Mr. Clarke took the class register and then told us that he had an important announcement, and that we all should listen very carefully.  His tone commanded attention, and we sat as quiet as mice at our desks, our curiosity mounting. The moment we had been anticipating - perhaps dreading - had arrived. He had the results of the exam. Reading the surnames in alphabetical order, he began to announce who had passed, and who had not made it.  With a surname beginning with E, I didn’t have to wait too long. The possible results were pass, take a second test, and fail. Only two of us had passed outright: Myrna Laws and I. To be honest, I can’t remember who else did what on that day but I know that, a week or so later, five or six children took a resit. If you passed first time, you went to a Grammar School.  The ones who scored best from the second group were offered an interview at the Grammar School to see if they were considered the ‘right fit’ (how terrifying that must have been!) And the remainder were directed to either the boys or girls Technical School. The rest went to the Secondary Modern School.
When we returned after the Easter break, the summer term was uneasy for Myrna and I.  Name-calling and teasing became a part of our daily lives. Today it would probably be called bullying, but we coped as best we could. My saving grace was the swimming lessons - proving I could swim faster and dive from higher boards  than all of those who sought to bully me brought some small solace.  And then there was the watch my father bought me as a reward, a treasured gift I was thrilled with.
Amidst the challenges, there were moments of joy, like the Christmas Fair at the Ebenezer Church. Each year, it brimmed with homemade items for sale and competitions to enter. One year, I won the Name the Doll competition, proudly choosing the name Naomi - a name I knew from my scripture lessons and liked very much. By then it was almost my thirteenth birthday and I considered myself too old for dolls. It was 1959. Still, I kept Naomi in her box, a relic of my childhood,  until my granddaughters came along in the next century. All four of them played with her and , in 2020, my youngest granddaughter, Matilda, took Naomi to her new home in Warsaw. 




The rhythm of our family life was marked by annual pilgrimages to the North-East, where my mother's roots remained firmly planted in the strong soil of Northern England. These journeys – whether by train or by coach – were more than mere visits; they were expeditions that bridged the geographical divide between Kent and Newcastle, and between our families.
My father suffered from travel sickness, so we travelled mainly by train in the early years. The journey itself was an adventure: leaving the familiar setting of Chatham station, navigating the sprawling complexity of London's Victoria, then boarding the northbound train from Kings Cross. Each mile brought us closer to a world that felt both familiar and foreign. My grandmother and family lived in East Howdon on the north bank of the river Tyne. Uncle Billy would drive to Newcastle Station to meet us.
In 1956, everything changed when mother passed her driving test. From then on, she drove us north, with steady determination. The journey began by driving north from Chatham along the A2, crossing the river Thames - sometimes via the Tilbury ferry and sometimes through the Blackwall tunnel - before joining the famous Great North Road.
What an exciting route it was! The road cut straight through towns and cities;  Peterborough, Stamford, Grantham and then further north through Doncaster and Darlington until, at last, we arrived in Newcastle upon Tyne. The only stops we made were for roadside picnics on a long and extraordinary trek, one that filled us with both anticipation and a sense of achievement when we finally reached our destination.
My mother had four brothers and two sisters, we always stayed with her eldest brother, Uncle Billy, and his wife, Aunty Lilly, along with their children - Judith, who was nine months older than me, and  Robert, eight months younger than my sister. They lived in a ground-floor flat in East Howden, while my granny lived just a street away in a top-floor flat.
The houses were all back to back with a wide concrete alley running between them where washing lines criss-crossed on wash days, creating a fluttering maze of sheets and clothing.  Each house had its toilet in the back yard, which must have been particularly difficult for Granny with her flat on the top floor. To reach it, she had to navigate an external flight of concrete steps that led down to the yard - no small challenge for an older woman.
The houses sat on land high above the River Tyne, separated from the water by a swathe of wasteland. It was a magical spot for us children: wild and rugged,  scattered with boulders, pretty weeds, and grasses.  We often went there for picnics with the other children, carrying bread and jam sandwiches, and our precious “bottle of pop”. In reality, it was just an old pop bottle we had filled with water, but we took turns sucking the stopper, convinced it still held the faint flavour of whatever was in it before! It didn’t matter, we loved every moment of it.
To me, Granny commanded a presence that could fill a room – every bit the matriarch. Her hair was always carefully contained in a hairnet, her glasses perched authoritatively on her nose, and she wore the ever-present wrap-over apron that seemed to be the uniform of her generation. Years later, I would think of Ena Sharples from Coronation Street, when I pictured her – strong, steadfast, and hewn from the same northern bedrock, shaped by hard times and determined resilience.
My mother’s younger sister lived in the front room of Granny’s flat, and she was engaged to be married. Judith and I were over the moon to be bridesmaids, brimming with excitement as Aunty Lilly, stitched our dresses. They were beautiful creations: pink satin with frilled bodices and delicate little pink satin caps for our heads.
When the big day arrived, a biting North-Easterly wind swept through the streets. We stood shivering but resolute for the photographs, holding our smiles and our white posies of flowers as our dresses fluttered in the breeze. After the photos, we headed to the Co-op Hall in Wallsend for a wonderful party that made all our efforts feel worthwhile. There were lots of sandwiches to eat, a beautiful cake, and a small local band playing popular music to dance to. I was seven years old and thought it was magical!
Judith and I decided to take my younger sister Patsy and Judith’s brother Robert to the indoor Wallsend swimming baths. We caught the bus from Howden, feeling very grown up and responsible for our charges. I’m sure we were probably very bossy - neither Patsy nor Robert could swim, and the only swimming aid available in those days was a rubber ring.  To add to the indignity, we all had to wear swimming caps, which we grumbled about but dutifully donned. I don’t think we stayed for long, between managing the non-swimmers and trying to enjoy ourselves, it must have been a short-lived adventure. Still, it felt like a triumph at the time, just for the fun of it.
Uncle Billy had served in the Merchant Navy during the war, stationed on the  perilous Atlantic run, tasked with bringing vital food supplies from America to Britain. It was an incredibly dangerous journey as German U-boats relentlessly trailed their convoys. Uncle Billy would tell us harrowing stories of being torpedoed on three separate occasions. Each time, they had managed to escape into life boats, enduring hours of freezing temperatures as they waited to be rescued. Those stories told in his humorous yet vivid way, painted an unforgettable picture of courage and survival. 
After the war, Uncle Billy became a director of a welding company on the River Tyne called Anglo-Swedish, located on the docks in Newcastle. He was rightly proud of his success and, especially, of his large company car - a Ford Humber - which he drove with great pride. Every year, Uncle Billy and Auntie Lilly would drive the family to visit us in Kent. When they stayed, our little house became a hive of activity. Aunty Lilly and Uncle Billy would sleep on the bed-settee in our dining room, while baby Robert shared a bedroom with my sister. Judith and I would top-and-tail in my single bed, giggling well into the night. One particularly memorable year, they even brought my Granny with them. That time, Judith and I were sent to sleep in Aunty Bowsher’s house, which felt like an adventure of its own, so Granny could sleep in my bed. 
Those summer holidays were always so exciting. We went out in the car to beautiful parks; Dunorlan Park in Tunbridge Wells, Moat Park in Maidstone, and Racecourse Park in Tonbridge. Racecourse Park was particularly special because it had an open air swimming pool and a miniature railway, set near the impressive Tonbridge Castle.  Our family picnics were wonderful. My father would bring his trusty kettle and boil water, over a methylated spirit stove, making tea in a proper teapot, which was then lovingly covered with a tea cosy! Such happy days - simple pleasures that I remember so fondly.
At the end of their holiday with us, I would often return with Uncle Billy, Aunty Lilly, Judith, and Robert to their home in the North-East. Unlike the journeys with my parents, Uncle Billy’s trips always included a stop at a restaurant or hotel for a midday meal. One such stop I remember vividly was the George Hotel in Stamford. Much later in life, I discovered that my dear friend Penny’s brother had once been the manager there. In a lovely twist of fate, I also later had a trainee positioned at The George and ate there myself with friends. Life has a funny way of weaving connections you’d never expect!
By the late 1950’s Uncle Billy and Aunty Lilly had moved to a newly built house in Tynemouth, just a short walk from the North Sea. I always took my roller skates with me, and Judith and I had glorious times skating on the newly built, smooth pavements. The beach was another source of joy. We spent many happy days on the sandy shore, where I would run into the sea to show off my newly acquired swimming skills. I would then dash back up the beach shivering and shaking, triumphantly declaring that the water was “boiling”!  This became a a running joke in the family, and to this day, my cousins still ask me if the water is “boiling” whenever the topic of the beach comes up!
At the end of summer, my parents would drive up to Tynemouth to visit for a few days and take me home again. Those long exciting summers, filled with laughter, family, and simple adventures remain some of my happiest childhood memories.




My father had an even larger family than my mother - seven sisters and two brothers who made it through infancy, along with three brothers who tragically did not survive. This sprawling network of siblings painted a vivid portrait of resilience and family bonds, shaped by the hardships of the era and the enduring strength of those who carried on. His youngest brother, Frederick, was a Leading Telegraphist on submarines. During the Second World War, Fred served on HMS Parthian, which was tasked with vital supply operations to Malta, carrying aviation fuel and ammunition. Tragically, in 1943, the submarine struck a naval mine and was lost with all hands. Fred was posthumously mentioned in dispatches for his bravery, as he stayed at his post to radio the Mayday signal. His name is proudly inscribed on the War Memorial on the Great Lines in Gillingham. I still vividly remember my father taking me to see it - his pride in his younger brother evident as we stood before the memorial.
We often used to visit Granny Ethell. When I was very little she lived in a three-storey house in Marlborough Street in Gillingham, opposite the Great Lines. The kitchen, its I remember it, was a sort of lean-to on the back of the house, with a glass roof which really impressed me! In the garden stood an Anderson air raid shelter, which I was desperate to explore, but was never allowed to.
Granny had a sweet tooth, and she liked condensed milk in her tea. I loved it too and treasured the chance to have a spoonful of that incredibly sweet milk as a treat, especially since sugar was still rationed in those days. 
Eventually, Granny’s house was demolished and she was rehoused in a new, first-storey flat on Twydall Green. The flat had a modern indoor bathroom, which must have felt like a real luxury. Her bedroom was just a curtained-off section of the living room, but she made it feel like home. I loved the little brass bells on the mantlepiece, the enormous radio in its beautiful case, and the oval-framed portraits of some of her children, hanging on the walls.
On Sundays, we often fetched Granny to join us for dinner. Sometimes, we would take her to West Malling to see her daughter, my Aunty Florrie. I have such fond memories of Granny always wearing a beautiful, soft fur coat. I used to love snuggling into that luxurious coat when she sat in the back of the car with Patsy and me. Granny also had a wonderful voice and would often sing wartime and variety hall songs. I particularly loved, In Your Easter Bonnet, Why Am I always the Bridesmaid, and It’s a Long Way to Tipperary, among others. Granny had been born in London and family history has it that she sang on the Music Hall stage. I don’t know if there is any truth in that, but I like to believe it.  Music certainly runs in our family. My dad could play music by ear - he often spoke of having a ukulele before I was born, and I remember him playing the mouth organ beautifully.
West Malling represented a different world entirely – a place where history and childhood adventure collided in the most magnificent ways. At its heart stood the Queen Anne house where Aunty Florrie and Uncle Ted lived with their children: Julie (formally Julia, though she'd always be Julie to me), my partner in countless adventures and older by six months, and Peter, who paired naturally with my sister in the way that cousins of similar ages often do.
The house itself was a treasure trove of history, having sheltered generations of Uncle Ted's family. His blacksmith father had once worked there, and his legacy still echoed in the building's very name: The Forge. But The Forge wasn't just a house – it was an entire world contained within its grounds, full of hidden mysteries and endless possibilities for two young explorers.
Every outbuilding seemed to tell its own stories. There was the silent forge, still dreaming of its fire-bright past; Uncle Ted's carpentry workshop, dominated by the  two-storey band saw that pierced the ceiling like some medieval torture device; and the chicken coop where Julie and I created our precarious den high in the rafters, accessible only by rope. (How horrified our mothers would have been had they known).But most intriguing of all were the dilapidated old stables, which led to a chalk cave – a wartime shelter carved deep into the earth. It was a magical place, with its rock-hewn seats and candlelit alcoves whispering silent stories of air raids and those who had once huddled there in the dark. What an amazing playground it was, a child’s paradise where imagination reigned supreme and every corner held the promise of adventure.
There was also an unassuming small garden shed near the house where Julie and I planned all our secret escapades! Inspired by Robin Hood on children’s TV, we would turn the shed into our own Sherwood Forest, lost in imaginary adventures. I loved the garden too, as there was real swing to play on and apple trees to climb. 
Not far from the house, further along St Leonards Street, was a lake and a park. On summer days, we would picnic there and build camps from the cut grass. That idyllic fun came to a rapid end for me at ten years old when, after having my polio injection, I developed really bad hay fever. Endless sneezing and streaming eyes meant no more grassy forts or carefree play in the fields. Before we had a telephone, I remember pleading with my mother to let me go to the phone box, clutching my precious two 1d coins  just so I could ring Aunty Florrie and ask if I could visit. During the school holidays, I often travelled there on the bus alone, proudly independent and well under the age of eleven! Aunty Florrie would meet me at the town centre, and we would walk back down the hill, past the lake and the Tower to The Forge.
Their house had a beautiful piano and Julie, who took music lessons, would play Autumn Leaves. I loved listening to her, completely mesmerised. On family visits, we would rope Patsy and Peter into putting on plays for our parents. An old screen in the corner of the room became our makeshift curtain, and we would disappear behind it, stiffling giggles, before dramatically revealing our performance. I like to think all that practice served Julie well as, much later in life, she became an opera singer in Paris, producing her own successful performances and teaching others.
Opposite The Forge stood. St. Leonard's Tower - a Norman keep, whose ancient  stones were silent witnesses to centuries of history. But to us, it was our tower. Aunty Florrie had custody of its key, and that little magical object granted Julie and me exclusive access. We climbed its ancient walls with the fearless enthusiasm of childhood, never imagining that decades later, the key itself would become part of local legend after it went missing in 1973. Decades later, it would make headlines when it reappeared after 47 years. Returned anonymously by post to English Heritage, the key was accompanied by a note apologising for the delay! The timing was almost poetic, as it coincided with my cousins' sale of The Forge, as though time itself was quietly closing the chapter on our childhood.
As the 1950s drew to a close, those early years – marked by secret adventures, family traditions, and idyllic freedoms – had shaped me in ways I would only fully understand later. From classrooms to church halls, from family visits to solo journeys, the foundations had been laid for the person I was becoming. The confidence I gained from those years would become invaluable as I stepped into the next chapter of my life, ready to face its challenges with the resilience and curiosity nurtured in that golden era.


Moving on, the 1960s




Just before the Easter holiday in 1958, my parents received a letter from the Kent Education Committee in Maidstone confirming that I had been offered a place at grammar school. The letter included information about the grammar schools in our catchment area, and we were required to make a choice. I’m not sure how much interest my parents took in the process, as there was no suggestion to contact any of the schools for further details.  Despite living in Chatham, I asked if I could attend Rochester Girls’ Grammar School and that was the school my parents selected on the form. 
Shortly afterwards, we received a letter from the school confirming my acceptance, along with detailed instructions about the school’s expectations and the required uniform, which was to be ordered and fitted by Leonard’s of Rochester, the supplier for all the schools in that area.  It also included information about PE and games kits, as well as the need for both indoor and outdoor shoes.  I’m not entirely sure why I was accepted, but comments made by my mother many years later, lead me to suspect it was tied to what we would now call ‘social inclusion’. In hindsight, I realise that my class consisted mainly of girls whose parents worked in factories or the dockyard and lived outside the town boundary. Meanwhile, the other two classes in our year group, were largely made up of of girls who’s parents were professionals.
That summer I was duly fitted out with the required uniform. As first-year students, we had to endure the indignity of wearing unflattering brown knee-length socks, a grey skirt with braces, and seasonal hats: a velour hat for winter and a Panama hat for summer. Our blouses had blue and white stripes, and we wore navy blue jumpers. For outdoor wear, we had a navy blue showerproof coat with a fitted hood and a navy blazer with the school badge stitched onto the breast pocket.  A scarf in the school colours was also available, but my parents deemed it unnecessary.
Upon arrival at school, we had to change into indoor shoes, while plimsolls were mandatory for PE and games. For gymnasium activities, we were all barefoot, and hockey required rubber-studded boots.  I was equipped with a new, brown leather satchel, which I worked extremely hard to give a lived-in appearance, and a sports bag.
In September 1958, I began attending Rochester Girls’ Grammar School. Back then, parents did not take their children to school so, at the age of eleven, I was sent off alone to catch the bus from the Luton Arches in Chatham. The busses went either to Borstal or Cookham Wood - destinations I later discovered carried quite notorious reputations!  My grammar school uniform quickly became a target for ridicule  from the local children, who jeered and called me names. To avoid further trouble, I soon learned to whip my hat off the moment I stepped off the bus, stuffing it into my bag before anyone could snatch it off and throw it away. I learned to stand my ground too, and before long, I was giving as good as I got, language wise! It was time to move on with my life!
When I started my new school I was put into class 1H. Our form tutor was Miss Harmer, the domestic science teacher, and a very kind lady. There were three classes in our year group which must have totalled around 100 girls altogether.  Coming from a mixed primary school, it was all completely new to me.  I couldn’t run home at lunch time anymore, I’d never worn a uniform before and, like every child starting at a new senior school, everything seemed huge and a bit overwhelming. One of the biggest changes was having to move to different classrooms for different subjects. Thankfully, we were assigned our own desks in the form room, each with a with a lift-up lid, where we could keep our books and belongings. It saved us from having to carry everything around the school all the time. We loved personalising our desks - mine had pictures of film stars and singers inside the lid.  Warren Beatty held pride of place for a while, until he was replaced by Billy Fury and Adam Faith!  Even though the form rooms were shared by different classes, none of us ever worried that our things would be taken, damaged, or defaced by anyone - and they never were.  
In domestic science lessons we learned how to cook basic meals, cakes, and scones. To be honest, most of us had already already knew the basics from helping at home, but I thoroughly enjoyed working in the bright, modern kitchen.  We also had sewing lessons, where our first task was to make identical blue gingham aprons for our cookery lessons. Each apron had to be neatly hemmed and finished with embroidery along the bottom.  In adult life, these skills proved invaluable.
The entire year group studied French and General Science, with the sciences splitting into specialised subjects in the third year. At the end of every term, we sat tests in each subject, and the results appeared on our end-of-term reports. Back then, there were no Government tests like today - just the GCE O-Levels we sat at sixteen. The national school leaving age was fifteen but, for grammar school pupils, leaving early was considered a waste of a hard-earned place and very frowned upon.
At the end of the first year, the end-of-term tests determined our placement in different ability groups for mathematics and marked the end of our focus on arithmetic. I was placed in the top group based on my results, but I’m sorry to say I did not last long there. Algebra and logarithms quickly left me bewildered and I found it incredibly hard  to see any logic in them.
In the winter of 1960, during my second year, I came down with a terrible case of influenza and missed three weeks school. This caused me endless problems as I struggled to catch up on all the new material I’d missed. It felt like I was constantly playing catch-up, particularly in the subjects that had introduced entirely new concepts while I was away.
Also in the second year, Domestic Science classes were replaced with the requirement to choose a second language: either German or Latin. My father advised me to choose German calling it a "living language”. Unfortunately, I wasn’t much good at either. I often confused the two languages, and found myself completely uninspired by both classes. The school decided  I would study Biology, instead of all three science subjects, which were reserved of the most academically gifted students.
On the brighter side, my favourite lessons were English, art, history, and biology. I think it was in English that I grew the most. The classes were split into two subjects, English Language and English Literature. I had always enjoyed reading and I liked the logic of clause analysis. The English literature classes introduced me to Chaucer and Shakespeare. We also studied poetry and originally my favourite was Tennyson’s ‘The Lady of Shalott’.  We had all been given rough books to keep all our class notes in, when we first started at the school, we then had to write up our class notes as homework. I wrote out the poem for my homework and drew a picture of the lady floating down the river. We were given a reading list too, which I don’t recall, but the book I really remember studying for my ‘O’ level was George Eliot’s ‘Silas Marner’, although I’m not really sure I appreciated all the issues it raised at the time.
Every year we had an inter-class drama competition which played out on the school stage. I loved the opportunity to be involved in both acting and the production side. I can remember us performing the Pied Piper of Hamlin and Macbeth. I enjoyed my Art classes too especially when our Art teacher introduced us to her recent purchase of a pottery kiln! We had classes in still life and compositions.
In 1962 our ancient (well we were sure she was!) Headmistress, Miss Butterfield, retired. She had made all the daily announcements from the stage at our assembly and called us all her ‘gals’. She also wore large pink bloomers down to her knees which we could all see when we had to sit in front of her on the floor, much to our amusement! As a leaving present the art department was giving her a calendar with all the pictures made by students.  I was thrilled to have my picture for the month of April chosen. I had drawn and painted a typical spring scene of the countryside together with daffodils and rabbits. One of my paintings of the view from my home of the railway line and the roofscape, which I had originally sketched. and then transferred to a painting, was also chosen for the Rochester Schools’ Exhibition at the Rochester Guildhall. I was delighted to be presented with a book prize.  I chose ‘Twentieth Century Poets’ which we were studying for ‘O’ levels and I loved it.


I also really enjoyed all the physical education and sport. I played netball for the school house team and was chosen for the school first eleven hockey team. In the summer term I enjoyed athletics, especially the high jump, doing a western roll, and playing tennis. I also played for the house rounders team.
Our school, now sadly demolished, was a wonderful red brick Victorian building on the Maidstone Road in Rochester.  Every Founders’ Day, which I believe was in May, the whole school had to walk in procession to Rochester Cathedral for the Founders’ Day service. We also had to sing the school song. There are, I believe, three verses but it started “Down in Kent o Down in Kent…” I won’t bore you with the rest but it goes on to mention hop fields and Bishop Gundulf (who incidentally was probably responsible for the building of that tower in West Malling!) . We also had a wonderful Christmas service in the Cathedral and when I was in the fifth year, I sang there in the school choir.
The sixth form were housed in a separate detached house on the opposite side of the road, called Ravenscroft. There were also beautiful lawned tennis courts. The sixth-formers had a different uniform and appeared as a rare, distinguished breed to us young girls. In the daily morning assembly they sat above us all on the first floor gallery.  The sixth form prefects also joined us for midday school dinners sitting at the head of the table in a chair as the rest of us squashed onto benches on each side. They served us the main course and we passed around the containers of vegetables. Everyone had plastic beakers and a jug of water was on each table, along with a cruet set and occasionally, if they thought they could get away with it, some idiot would pour salt into the water jug when no-one was looking.
Our favourite desert, a local dish which originated on the Isle of Sheppey in Kent, was Gypsy Tart and the worst one which we all disliked was stewed figs!  If boiled beef and cabbage or stewed figs were on the menu we would try very hard to be excused from dinner!
Further up the Maidstone Road a new boys grammar school had been built to replace the old one in the middle of Rochester High Street. Apparently part of the original city wall ran inside of the building.  It can actually still be seen today in the High Street car park. The boys school was called Sir Joseph Williamson’s Mathematical School, locally known as Rochester Math.  The boys at this school were of great interest to almost all the girls especially us silly young ones! The buses home were full of Math boys and when the buses stopped outside of our school, we would check the windows to see who was on it before deciding to board! Every year, our first eleven hockey team would play the boys school and it was a totally uneven hoot.  Since I was in the team, I enjoyed taking part.  I seem to remember a lot of the match consisted of trying to trip the boys up and cheating.




Of course, at that time in my life, a lot more was happening than simply going to school every day. By the age of thirteen I was becoming increasing interested in what I was wearing and the way I looked. For a class of pubescent girls, getting changed together for PE lessons and running naked through the showers was actually quite traumatic for most of us. It was certainly not the way any of us had been brought up, I hadn’t even seen my father in his pyjamas! However, there certainly was a plus side, none of us were going to grow up as prudish as our parents! The sixties were a time of enormous change, which had started with the Teddy Boys and girls in the late fifties, I’m sure as a reaction to all the uniforms and difficult times of being born or being children during the war. Of course, the music was changing a lot too and younger people seemed to have ownership of that. In 1960 Elvis Presley had a huge hit for the second time in three years with “It’s Now or Never (O Sole Mio)” and Cliff Richard and the Shadows had a hit with “I Love You”.  The hottest discussion of the day was who you liked the best.
On a Saturday afternoons I went to the Gaumont Picture House in Rochester with my friend Joy, from school.  Joy lived in Rochester, she was shorter than me with lovely curly auburn hair and freckles. There was beautiful ballroom on the first floor above the theatre and on a Saturday afternoon it became the Victor Sylvester School of Dancing. At last, I was at a dancing school! We learned to waltz and to quickstep and a Cuban dance which started in the fifties called the Cha-cha-cha, we felt that was very modern. I had some little cuban heeled, sling back shoes which were the first heeled shoes I had ever had.  After our class I often went home to tea with Joy. Her mother made us tea in the kitchen because her father was in the front room watching the football results and could not be disturbed by chattering little girls! It was at Joy’s house I watched wrestling on the TV for the first time, and her dad was happy to have us watch that with him! I really liked that as my father never watched sport although he did like playing tennis.
I had already ‘flown up’, as it was called, from the Brownies to the Guides a couple of years before, when I was eleven, and by 1960 I had become a Patrol Leader for my Robin Patrol.  The Girl Guides was held in the same church hall but on a Monday evening. I enjoyed the guides, we did lots of very interesting things like camping and hiking and our patrol time was often spent learning new skills like tying the right knots, learning about guiding in the rest of the world, basic first aid and how we could help other people.
We also worked for our badges and when we were ready, we had to make arrangements to go to the tester’s house with our work and answer questions. I loved being able to add badges to my uniform sleeve. This was the 50 year Jubilee of the Girl Guides which had been founded in 1910. The Ebenezer Guides went to a national Jubilee Camp at Knole Park, Sevenoaks in Kent. It was beautiful parkland and there were wild deer which I had never seen before. Six of us slept in a Bell tent and my father had bought me an army surplus kit bag and sleeping bag.  The sleeping bag was dark green, waterproof canvas material which you had to open and add your own blankets and an inner sheet. It was nothing at all like the lovely warm ones you have today!
We also had to use the latrines, or lats as we called them, which were dugout trenches surrounded by hessian screens. Soft earth was piled up behind the trenches and there were shovels for you to cover your own toilet with earth! I guess we all had to fetch our water too but I can’t remember that.  I did love cooking over the camp fire which we learned to lay and light and I loved it when we sat around the camp fire in the evening singing our camp fire songs. I liked singing the rounds too as I thought they were great fun.  I especially liked to sing “ Kookaburra sits on the old gum tree” and of course ‘London’s Burning”. We all had to go out wood collecting in the day to make sure there was enough to keep the fire going and we made handy things like a place to store our bedding during the day so it wasn’t left on the ground and somewhere to keep our cooking utensils. They were made with sticks of wood and string.
Being a Girl Guide taught me lots of life skills, how to be self-reliant and how to read a map, a skill which has remained useful all my life. Now I do not have to rely on the Sat Nav to find my way and maps also show you where all the local places of interest are and where you are in relation to where you want to go.  I still love them!




That same year, 1960, my father wanted to take us to France, so we drove to Folkestone to catch the ferry to Boulogne for a day trip as foot passengers. We parked the car and excitedly boarded the boat. With hindsight I think my father was hoping to relive his own younger years, but with a wife and two children in tow it didn’t quite work out as he expected! Firstly it was a really hot day, too hot really, and a long walk from the ferry port to Boulogne town. My sister was only eight and problems soon arose when we girls wanted to go to the toilet but were told to keep on walking.  Eventually my father found toilets in a park for us to use, of course they were standing footmark ones which my sister and I and my mother had never seen before. They were very smelly too, but you can do anything when you are desperate enough! My father wanted me to use the French I had just started learning at school but my mind had gone blank.  I was marched into the boulangerie and had to request bread rolls. I guess I somehow made myself understood but then I refused to go into the charcuterie for meat.  My father bought the meat and we sat on the grass while dad used his penknife to open the rolls and fill with meat. My sister and I complained of being thirsty and as I said, it was a seriously hot day. We eventually found a shop selling orange juice and walked back to the port. So my first trip ‘abroad’ was not the most successful! Needless to say, we were never taken again. Around that time ,my parents started to go on holiday on their own in the summer holidays when my sister and I could stay happily in the North-East with our cousins, which we loved.
My very best friend at school was called Linda Carr, we remained good friends all her life. Linda, when she had first started at school with me, lived in a council house in Gillingham. Her father was a lovely man who worked in the offices in the dockyard and, at that time, her mother was at home looking after Linda’s younger sister Judy.  Linda’s father had been captured as a prisoner of war by the Japanese and had been kept in a dreadful underground prison.  He was one of many who had been very lucky to survive. His name was George and he organised monthly Friday dances at the junior school Linda and her sister went to in Featherby Road. There was even a proper dance band playing!  Quite a number of the old swing dance bands were still performing then.  Linda and I occasionally did a little waltz around the room which I had taught her after my lessons with Joy but a lot of our time was also spent preening in the cloakroom!  The hall had chairs all around the edge of the room where we sat and gossiped hoping to be asked to dance by one of the boys. I liked a boy also called George and tried smiling, making eyes at him and it worked, he asked me to dance! One Friday night he even asked if he could walk me home, great excitement, but I was always going home with Linda to stay overnight at her house so that didn’t happen!  Anyway, I had already asked my mother how old I had to be to have a boyfriend, and she had said fourteen.  I still had to wait! To this day I still have no idea why she said fourteen although it could have been because, in her day, that was the school leaving age and at fourteen she had been sent to London on a coach to work in service as a maid.
The following year when Linda’s sister Judy had also moved on to Grammar school, their parents bought a brand new house which had been built in the Wigmore area of Gillingham; her mother was very pleased! The dances at Featherby Road were over, but after they had moved to their new home, Linda found a new club for us to go to. The 61 Club had opened on a Saturday night in St. Matthew’s Church Hall in Wigmore, you were supposed to be fifteen to get in but we thought, at fourteen and a half, we would make it and felt very grown up with our dark eye liner and back combed hair! This was also the time when we starched our home made net petticoats in sugar and wore shirt waister dresses with full skirts. The club sold Hubbly Bubbly bottles of fizzy pop in the interval, but there was a pub called The Spyglass and Kettle further along the road which, as we got older, we would sneak off to with some of the older boys. I remember always looking for a seat behind the door just in case someone came in who knew my parents! I drank Babycham in a pretty little glass with a Bambi on.  I liked Bambis!
There were live groups playing every weekend at the club. Our favourites were called the Kingfishers, and Bern Elliott and the Fenmen a beat group that went on to have a record contract and a Top 20 hit, with their version of “Money”, in 1964. Linda and I continued to go to the 61 Club for a couple of years and had a variety of boyfriends that we had met there. When the Twist became popular Linda and I would show off on the dance floor hoping for company! It was packed most of the time and if you needed to look in the mirror in the Ladies, you had to fight your way through the haze of hair lacquer!  I often had to get the last bus home, back to Chatham, but sometimes I could stay the night with Linda. The last bus home was quite good fun depending on who else was making the journey. Buses then still had bus conductors who came around the bus to collect your fare and they always kept an eye on their customers so it all seemed really safe. I would then have to walk a few streets home from the bus stop but I can’t remember ever feeling unsafe. I would hastily stop at the bottom of my road to rub off some of my make up before my dad saw me when I got home!
On Saturday 30th December that year I had stayed the night with Linda; when we woke the next morning, it had been snowing very hard and we all went out to play snowballs with the neighbours.  The main roads had been cleared, so the buses were still running, but I had to leave before lunchtime to make sure I could get home. My friend from school, Sandra Pilkington, came to tea that day and stayed with us as she could not get home to Rochester. On New Year’s Day my sister Patsy, Sandra and I went out the front and cleared the snow off the pavements for a number of our neighbours then made a huge snowman in the road. The next day dad put new brass runners on our sledge and we took it up to Chalk Pit Hill.




On my 15th birthday in January 1962 I received a new handbag, a Buddy Holly LP, a bracelet and 18/6d (worth the equivalent of £20 in 2021); I was so pleased to be fifteen! The next day was a Saturday and Linda and I were very excited to visit the latest new offering in our area, a Top Rank ten-pin bowling alley, we loved it and we went on to spend many happy hours either playing ten-pin or just meeting up with friends in the coffee bar. It was at the top of Chatham Hill which was a very steep walk but I often walked there all the same. Chatham Hill was also on the main A2 road from London to the Kent coast before the M2 was built. On a warm summer evening the road was one long traffic jam back to London and a number of us would walk home from the bowling alley talking to all the people in their stationary cars.
1962 was also the year I started smoking, not a lot but I did think it was a very grown-up thing to do, and suited my image with my friends. You could buy a packet of five cigarettes back then and there was a lovely old fashioned tobacconist near my bus stop.  It seemed to be something all the young people did, like a rite of passage. I only smoked when I was with my friends at the weekend. I was still going to Art College classes on a Saturday morning and I was still using my bicycle to visit friends all over the Medway Towns. I had to be very careful with my money as I must have been given pocket money then and I also used quite a number of buses although they were not very expensive and I could still get half fare as a child’s fare was called.  There were a lot of different clubs to go to and I visited several with different friends. St. Augustine’s Church at the top of Rock Avenue in Gillingham often had live groups playing in the church hall and I went to the Mountbatten Club in Chatham on a Friday night with my school friend Pauline Willert. Pauline lived in Gillingham and we would often meet at the Luton Arches and cycle to school together in the summer term. For fun, coming home, we would sometimes dare each other to cycle twice around the Star Hill roundabout which was actually very busy - what idiots we were - fortunately we escaped unscathed.  I also continued going to the Ebenezer but now I occasionally went to the Friday night youth club and Sunday morning church if I woke up in time! I was no longer a girl guide. 
That summer I had a boyfriend called Roy Morton. He had red hair and went to Gillingham Boys’ Grammar School and was doing his A-Levels. He had a part time job at Fyffes Banana factory and brought gifts of bananas. My parents liked him and called him Banana Roy! I’m afraid that didn’t last because, in the summer he got sunburned and the skin peeled off his nose. I was such a shallow person I told him I would not go out with him any more! I know he did well with his exams and was accepted on an engineering degree course at Imperial College London.
The summer of 1962 was lovely and hot. At the beginning of August Aunty Lilly and Uncle Billy and family came to stay before they left for their own holiday, leaving Judith and Robert to stay with us. We had a lovely time together. Judith throughly enjoyed joining in with all the activities. We played table tennis at the youth club, went ten-pin bowling and visited the local coffee bars which had just become very popular with their shiny coffee machines and jukeboxes.  We also had our first ever Wimpy Bar in Chatham High Street, where you watched them grilling the burger buns, beef burgers and onions. The only sauce you added was on your own table in a plastic, tomato shaped, squeezy tube. The burgers were delicious and you collected them from the counter to take to your own table when your order was called out. They were so much nicer than today’s McDonald’s! We took Patsy and Rob swimming at the Strand in Gillingham and in the Maidstone pool too. Judith and I went dancing on Saturday night at the 61 Club. Life was so busy. We went to Folkestone with my parents and to the cinema to see Elvis Presley in Love me Tender.  My father had also bought a large, reel-to-reel tape recorder. We had a great time trying to record our favourite music from the radio.  There were also a lot of background noises recorded such as “shush, we’re recording” and “ don’t shut that door’’! Whoops! BANG! Then of course there was a lot of what would probably today be called Karaoke!




After another two weeks had passed, Judith and I took Patsy and Robert to Newcastle on the overnight train from London Kings Cross station.  Patsy was eight years old and Robert seven.  Patsy had a pet white mouse which she took with her in a toffee tin which had holes punched in the lid. On the journey she would lift the lid and peer in, we were terrified the mouse would escape and I don’t think any of us got much sleep!  It was just an ordinary carriage with eight seats and a door to the corridor. The ticket collector came round and asked to see our tickets, he asked what was in the tin and we told him ”toffees”. No way were we going to admit there was a mouse on board! Uncle Billy met us at Newcastle Central Station and off we went to spend a holiday with them whilst our parents went to have their own child-free holiday! 
Aunty Lilly and Uncle Billy had, by then, moved to their new home in Tynemouth where there were sand dunes leading down to a beautiful sandy beach called Tynemouth Long Sands. The Tynemouth Plaza was above the beach and Judith and I went to a couple of evening dances there. Aunty Lilly was a very good dress maker and she made me a twenties style dress in bright yellow cotton. I bought myself some black stockings to go with it and, with a chain of long beads around my neck,  I felt I must look like the artist I hoped to be!   Opposite the Plaza, on the other side of the road, was a park which also had a small boating lake.  We had a lot of fun on the boating lake. Judith and I took Robert and Patsy to the beach on nice days. The entrance to the River Tyne was marked each side of the river by lighthouses at the end of long piers. North Shields on the north shore and South Shields on the south.  On the north side, where we were, were the historical ruins of Tynemouth Castle and Priory.  It is now owned by English Heritage, as is the West Malling Tower. It has a history of over two thousand years and was once one of the most fortified areas of England. Beneath the priory is a small sandy bay called The Haven where the sea scouts had their meeting hall.  Robert always loved the sea and his first boat, at the age of nine, was a canvas blue and white canoe. He would take his boat out on his own and paddle across the mouth of the River Tyne to South Shields.  The Tyne was still a busy river then and Swan Hunter was still building boats in their North Shields shipyard.  The fishing boats delivered to the North Shields fish quay and large boats delivered their goods to Newcastle. It was quite a dangerous journey for Rob to make.  In later life he went on to own his own fishing trawler for a number of years.
One evening that summer Judith had arranged with her boyfriend Gordon, and her friend Ann with her boyfriend Barry, to take me to the cinema on a blind date with their friend Pete Wilkinson.  We went to the cinema in North Shields to see The Comancheros, a John Wayne film. I thought Pete was great fun and I spent the remainder of the holiday with new friends. We went shopping in Whitley Bay and I bought Once Upon a Dream by Billy Fury. Pete and I went off to the cinema on our own; we went to see Old Yeller which made me cry, an old Sinatra film called the Man with the Golden Arm and the original film of The Phantom of the Opera. It was in black and white and quite scary. All of us spent hours on the beach, it must have been a good summer. I was seriously impressed when Pete brought his Japanese Transistor 8 radio, with attached aerial, to the beach for us to listen to the Sunday afternoon Pick Of The Pops! After two weeks of great fun my parents arrived for two days after their holiday, then we packed up and returned to our home in Chatham where a new school term was about to begin.




I was pleased to meet up with my school-friends again. Larry, whose real name was Lorraine Hughes, lived on Chatham Hill and we often went ten pin bowling together, of course Linda and I had a lot of catching up and planning to do and other friends too.  Sandra Pilkington who suddenly looked very grown up as she often now wore her longer hair in a bun on top of her head, was great friends with Judith Hennin, Angela Sutton, Catherine Shirley and Kathy Weir too. We formed quite a social group together. We were in form 5E and Miss Eagle was our form tutor.  We were reminded it was our exam year and we all needed to work very hard. Apart from us all nodding in agreement I’m not really sure we took the advice very seriously! Our social life seemed to go on apace.
I continued to go to Rochester Art college for Saturday morning classes except when I was asked to play Hockey for the school first eleven team. I wrote and received letters from Pete Wilkinson in Tynemouth but that certainly didn’t stop me from having other boyfriends that I met at the Saturday night dances, the youth clubs or bowling alley! In December 1962 when I was with Sandra at the bowling alley, we were chatted up by two boys who has seen us there the previous week. The one that liked me was called Phil and he had blonde hair and drove a white Ford Consul.  I have to say I was impressed! He took me home and made a date with me for the following Friday back at the bowling alley. Sandra and the other guy, Graham came too. Phil’s name was Philip Barling and he worked on the family sheep farm at Upchurch on the marshes of the River Medway. They farmed Romney sheep. On Boxing Day that year it started snowing and it became known as the winter of the big freeze when there was not a frost free night between December 22nd and 5th March 1963. Phil put snow chains on his tyres and came to visit me from the farm.  He also brought two pheasants he had shot as a gift for my parents so there was another boyfriend they liked!  Well, that didn’t last for long, by mid January it was all over.  I had obviously said no too often! I was so heartbroken I bought Benda Lee’s record “Losing You’ which had just been released and played it over and over again. That heartbreak didn’t last long!
1963 was a very exciting time to be sixteen years old. The Beatles had released their first record ‘Love me Do’ the previous October and The Rolling Stones had their debut at the Marquee Club in London the previous July. We no longer talked about Elvis Presley and Tommy Steel it was always “who do you like best, the Beatles or the Stones?” Then of course there were the Mods with their fur trimmed anoraks and Vespa and Lambretta scooters covered in chrome, mirrors and fur tails and the Rockers with their leather jackets and motorbikes. There was far too much going on to worry about exams!  I still enjoyed sport and bought a new Slazenger tennis racket.  Sandra and I played a lot of tennis sneaking off to the sixth form lawn tennis courts, across the road at Ravensworth, especially when we were supposed to be at music lessons; that particular teacher didn’t seem to know who was supposed to be in her class and it was not an exam subject for us.  I also had a new boyfriend that summer who I really liked.  His name was John Baker and his father managed The Queens Head in Wigmore on the other end of the road where the 61 Club was, although I seldom went to the club anymore. He was a mod and had a lot of good friends.  We had a lovely few summer months riding on scooters and going to The Strand open air swimming pool.  His father’s pub also had a nice garden and we all used to meet there occasionally. His father held proper wrestling matches in a ring in the lawned gardens at the weekends.
In the spring of 1963 my parents once again returned from their Saturday afternoon shopping with some exciting news, they had bought a 22’ six-berth caravan from a dealer and it was to be placed on a static holiday site. The following weekend Mum and Dad went to visit the site on the Hythe to Dymchurch Road. The owners of the site had a small general store and there were two toilet/shower blocks. It was on the opposite side of the road to the  beach which had a Martello tower and steps down to a sandy beach. The Military Canal ran beside it. It was only an hours’ drive from the Medway Towns and promised an exciting future!
In the very hot summer of 1963 I sat my GCE O-levels.  In fact it was so hot that the flat tarmac roof of the gymnasium where we took our exams had to be constantly hosed with water by the school caretaker.  I also still suffered very badly with hay fever and the only prescription available then was Piriton which had the side effect of making you very sleepy.  There were no allowances in those days for any reasons you may have had for being disadvantaged. The subjects I took were English Language, English Literature, History, Geography, Art, Biology and Scripture. 
The end of term that year had a slightly odd feeling. We were all excited to be at the end of schooling for some of us, in fact two of our class members had not even completed a fifth year having left school the age of 15 which you could still do then. Only a small handful had decided to stay on in the sixth form including me. Everyone else had decided to leave and most were going into administration work. My friend Linda was going to work at Barclays Bank and another friend of mine, Jean Beard couldn’t wait to leave, find work and marry the bus conductor she had been meeting! That marriage remained a long and happy one.  Jean worked for a travel agency.  We all met in the playground to have a fish and chip lunch from the fish shop, instead of the regulation school dinner.  We wrote messages to each other in our summer regulation Panama hats and took photographs.  At the end of the day we all went our separate ways.



She’s leaving school




The summer holidays were spent once again in Tynemouth and again the sun seemed to shine all the time, although I’m sure it didn’t. Judith and I spent most of the time meeting up with all the friends of the previous summer.  A new coffee bar had opened in Tynemouth in a cellar called ‘The Cave’ and I think we all imagined we were going to meet up with the Beatles at some point in the Cavern Club! Pete became my summer boyfriend again, he had passed his driving test and was able to borrow his older brother’s Cortina. We visited lots of different places along the coast and especially liked seven mile long Druridge Bay, with sand dunes and a long sandy beach which was also used for racing motorbikes. We had picnics and visited different country pubs. One night he drove us to Newcastle to the Mayfair, a huge ballroom with a first floor balcony with bars all around three sides of the dance floor. I had never been anywhere like it before. There were five different groups playing one of whom was the Alan Price Combo who were later to become the Animals. What an exciting night that was!  Eventually it was time for us all to return home to Chatham and await the results of my exams.
I passed in three subjects and returned to school the following year still in the fifth form to retake the ones I had not passed. You needed to pass four subjects to enter the sixth form. I was the only one of my class to decide to take resits. I enjoyed the new class I joined and made some very good friends, some of whom I am still in touch with, in the spring term they even voted me as their form prefect. During the year I decided that, whatever happened, I did not want to stay on in the sixth form but I really had made up my mind that I would like to become a teacher.  I researched Teacher Training Colleges and discovered that providing I had at least one A-level and four GCE O-levels, which had to include English Language and Maths or a science subject, I could apply. I also discovered that if I went to Art College for a Degree I could then go on to do a years Teacher Training course. I was good at art and had been attending Saturday morning art classes at Rochester Art College, which had been offered free to me as the school had recommended me. I went to the office at the Art College and asked if I would be able to take Art A-level at evening classes as I had already achieved my O-level.  They said that I could, but that I would need to get my headteacher’s permission.  Back at school I asked to see the Head and told her what I would like to do.  She later told me she would not give me permission to go to evening classes.  Instead, she gave me permission to take my A-level in school while continuing with my other classes. I was able to complete and be entered for my Art A-level within one year. Another big lesson learned; look for all the information you can find and be brave enough to ask for what you would like. 
In November 1963 I realised that I needed to find a Saturday job so that I could buy the fashionable clothes and records that the shops seemed full of. Britain was becoming the place to be, with the birth of the Liverpool sound, plus the Rolling Stones and many other popular artists it was an exciting time. With Twiggy, Biba et al, it was a fashion centre too. I walked into Woolworths in Chatham High Street and asked if they had any vacancies for Saturday Girls, as we were known. My details were taken and I was told I had to come and sit an arithmetic test which I did.  I was sat alone in a room upstairs and given a test paper obviously all about adding up in pounds, shillings and pence. Phew! I passed and started my first Saturday on 23rd November 1963, earning the vast sum of 15/9d (about £14 in today’s money)!  Ironically the following week my school friend Angela, who had actually passed enough subjects to be in the sixth form, failed her test at Woolworths.  I did feel a little vindicated!  Quite a number of school-friends became Woolies Saturday Girls and although we did work very hard we also had a lot of fun and still had enough energy to have a Saturday night out! In fact there were lots of parties happening that winter and we used to find out where and just turn up, gatecrashing! None of us had any transport but if they were any distance away we used to thumb lifts.  I’m fairly sure our parents didn’t know and we didn’t even give our safety a second thought.  Winter moved into the Spring term of 1964 and I successfully applied for a place at Maidstone College of Art to take an Art Foundation year. I was thrilled.




At the end of April my parents told us they had decided to move home and they took Patsy and I to see a bungalow they had found in Walderslade.  It was very nice, detached with a very large garden and nothing at all like the home I had grown up in, in Herman Terrace. I also had another boyfriend at that time called Nick Storey and he had a motorbike.  His parents were artists and lived in an intriguing home in Bredhurst. Nick and I just had a lot of fun, we went to visit my parents when they went to stay in their caravan, went to lots of parties and on one memorable occasion we rode on the M2 which had only just been opened and would not be completed until 1965. There were no speed limits then and we managed to do the proverbial ‘ton’ before stopping at the service station which excitingly opened very late, for a cup of coffee. Actually, writing about all these exploits I am very glad I am still here to tell the tale and I’m afraid I have no idea what happened to Nick!
In the summer Mum and Dad let me have a week at the caravan with two of my friends from school. Angela and Catherine were taken by Catherine’s mother and my Mum took cousin Judith, who had been staying with us, and me. After tea, the mothers left and we four had a glorious week in the sun. We, once again, thumbed lifts into Folkestone and sat on the beach or wandered around the lanes always ending up in the coffee shop on the most ‘in’ lane of them all, wearing our hippy, arty  clothes! We were all quite broke at the time so a cup of frothy coffee lasted forever while we sat and listened to Marianne Faithful on the jukebox.  The cheapest meal we could get was egg and chips, so I think that was probably our staple diet that week! At the end of the week the mothers reappeared with transport and my mother brought my A-level result.  I had passed my A-level art and was very excited to know I was soon off to art college. I also achieved the three more O-levels that I really should have achieved the previous year.  I was very pleased to know my school days were over.
The following week I returned to Newcastle by train with Judith for yet another few summer weeks with Pete and all the friends there. Pete was now working in a travel agency and had bought his own car, a maroon Ford Capri. With school behind us and Judith at Teacher training college we all felt very grown up. Pete was working in Newcastle city centre and I went up to meet him on the Metro. I was very excited to be taken out for lunch to my first ever Chinese restaurant. Pete took a week’s holiday and we went out somewhere different every day in his car. We took a picnic which we ate in the sand dunes at Druridge Bay and found a lovely spot by the river in Corbridge for a picnic on another day. At that point I think we both believed we had a future ahead of us and he gave me a little heart shaped signet ring with both our initials engraved on it. I also applied for my first ever driving licence at North Shields Police Station who printed and gave me a small yellow, folded card containing my licence. The Police were the issuing authority then. Pete put L plates on his car and I practiced driving along the beach road and out to some of the villages.  I have no idea if I was insured or indeed if anyone else was insured!  Sadly, my father would not let my mother continue to give me lessons because he said I would wear the gears out! So that had to wait for another ten years.




August became September and I once again returned to Chatham. Whilst I had been away Mum, Dad and Patsy had moved to our new home in Dargets Road, Walderslade, Chatham.  Patsy had to get the bus from there every day to the Girls Technical School in Chatham, my father bought a moped to get to his final years at Chatham dockyard and I caught the bus into Maidstone to start Art College.  Before the term started I, most importantly, had to look like an art student and been trying desperately for the last year to grow my hair.  I would only wear plain dark colours, preferably black or grey but brown if I was desperate, and I purchased the requisite wooden drawing board, easel, portfolio and paint brushes.  All the paper, paints and drawing materials were bought directly from the college when we arrived. I found an old, battered basket to put my things in and the easel remained at the college with my name on. It was important to wear lots of dark eyeliner and have very pale lips. I had a full, fashionable fringe and my shoulder length hair had flick ups. My college fees were paid by the government, as were everyone’s then, and I did not get a cost of living grant as I lived with my parents.  I did keep my Woolworths Saturday job to make sure I had enough money for my social life and a few Cadets cigarettes which were the smallest and cheapest you could buy!  I loved Maidstone Art College which was still in the middle of the town next door to the museum. It did remind me of my school as it was Early Victorian, built of red brick with beautiful wooden floors and a sweeping staircase.  It also had a cellar basement which was used by the photography department. We often went to the museum to draw still life compositions.  I liked the smell of oil paints, seeing all the different students in different departments and meeting the new students in my group.  As it was the Foundation Year, we covered lots of different topics. The subject I liked least was History of Art, although there were some very interesting aspects too.  Maybe it was the teacher? We talked about art in theatre and we chose a Shakespearean play to design a set for, I chose Twelfth Night.
Once a week we had to walk up from the college to the workshop, which was in a street behind Maidstone Prison. I had never been near a prison before and wondered what lurked behind the old stone wall which surrounded the prison. The workshop contained the pottery section with several potter’s wheels, a wide selection of tools and colour glazes and the firing kiln.  I really enjoyed the pottery classes. We started off making slab pots and I made a base for a lamp, we then moved on to coil pots and thumb pots before learning to throw and centre the clay on the potters wheel. We also attended the woodwork classes there.  We all had to make a large wooden box with a lid and were taught how to use the basic tools and how to cut and glue dovetail joints.  It took me back to working with my father in his cellar. I still have my box, I have most of my pottery too. In the main art college we had lessons on drawing and painting using different media and subjects. I was rather taken aback by drawing nude models although the men wore G-strings or posing pouches as they were called.  It was fine once I got used to it and I was fascinated by recreating what I could see and learning the techniques to use. One of our models was Quentin Crisp and all I can really recall is the fact he dyed his hair, I think it was purple at that time!  
I quickly made a special friend, Penny Gough, and we have remained close friends ever since, we were seventeen years old. She has been by my side through most of the important times in my life, good and bad. We had only been at college for three weeks and I invited Penny to come and stay at my home for the weekend. Penny was living in digs in Maidstone as her family lived in Norfolk. As I was still working on Saturdays, I met Penny from the bus stop after work and together we caught the bus home to Walderslade.




I’m not sure when but just after starting college I had met Colin from my childhood again, maybe at one of those gatecrashing parties! Anyway, I knew on the grapevine there was to be another party that Saturday night in the Communist Bookshop on the Luton Road in Chatham. It was Dave Bradbury’s twenty-first birthday and his parents were both very active local communists. His mother smoked a white clay pipe!  Of course that was the time of the CND and left-wing politics so it was very hip for students to be involved. Colin and his friend Miffy (I have no idea why he was called Miffy) came to call for Penny and I on their scooters and we went off to the party.  We had been there a while when Colin and Miffy went on to somewhere else leaving us there until they got back.  I was sitting on my own drinking gin when a tall guy came up to talk to me. He asked me how I came to be there and what my name was when I suddenly felt very ill.  He took me through to the kitchen and asked someone called Pauline to look after me.  I remember she took me to the outside toilet and I was promptly sick! After a drink of water and no more gin, I was fine and the same guy came and talked to me.  He told me his name was Mick and when I told him I was at art college in Maidstone, he told me he also worked in Maidstone for the Kent Education Department as a clerk.  We arranged to meet during his lunch break on the Monday.  Soon after that Colin and Miffy reappeared and Miffy said we could stay at his house on the Maidstone Road for the night as they didn’t want to have to drive back out to Walderslade.  I went to the call box and rang my parents to say Penny and I were staying with friends for the night and would return the next day.  Off we went to Miffy’s parent’s very nice house but when we got there he was frightened his parents would know and told us we could sleep on camp beds in the garage! I was so tired as I had worked in Woolworths all day, I was willing to sleep anywhere! It was all a bit ridiculous but that’s what we did and we all crept out early the next morning and somehow got back to Walderslade.  We were actually quite near the bus stop so we may have just got a bus. We slept for most of the day on the Sunday and on Monday morning Penny and I took the bus to Maidstone.
That Monday morning as Penny and I were catching up with our friends on the weekend gossip, I told them about Mick and that I was meeting him that day, I was quite excited. Lunchtime came, I left the college at the planned time and walked up the road towards Maidstone High Street.  I had only gone a short way when I met Mick walking towards me. We decided to go to a pub called The London Tavern in the High Street for lunch. Once we got talking we both admitted we had no idea if the other would show up!  Mick must have been quite keen as it was a good few minutes walk from the County Council offices he worked in. Mick took my telephone number and promised to ring me. He did and invited to me to his family home the following Sunday afternoon. I took the bus from the top of Dargets Road and it stopped near Mick’s home in Castle Rd. I walked to his house in Dagnell Road and was invited into the bay windowed front room. The only thing that seemed rather odd to me was that there was also a motor bike standing on newspaper with some of it’s parts missing!  Mick explained to me that it was his brother’s bike and being repaired! Mick asked me if I liked music and produced his Dansette record player together with his LP sized box of 45 records which remained with him all his life. He had a lot of rock n roll records, Elvis Presley, Chuck Berry, Little Richard and many more.  He also liked country music and particularly liked Johnny Cash. Bob Dylan had also just become famous and Mick owned the first LP Bob Dylan had produced in 1962. During the afternoon Mick’s mother brought us tea and lovely, home made, warm, bread pudding. Apparently she then joined her sister, who I had not met at that time, in the back room and commented she had not met a girl Mick had brought home before that talked as much! They apparently approved!
My  life really changed at that point. We saw each other very regularly and I met up with and became part of his social group, some of whom were already in steady relationships. Of course we found out all about each other’s families.  We also discovered that it was very surprising we had never met before.  When I was little and lived in Herman Terrace, Mick had been one of the group of boys who used to come and train-spot.  Also Colin, that first boyfriend who I went with to the 21st birthday party, lived literally in the next road to Mick, in fact their gardens backed on to each other! They had also both been to Rochester Math, although Colin was a year younger in Mick’s brothers class.  Mick’s best friend who also went to Rochester Math School was called Geoff Garvey. I had known Geoff for about a year by then, and also been to Geoff’s twenty-first birthday party, in a flat he was renting in Chatham.  I know I had not been able to stay late at his party as I left to catch the last bus home to Walderslade.  Mick had turned up later that night too, but it was probably just as I was leaving. I also discovered that Pauline, who Mick asked to help me on the night we first met, was one of his ex-girlfriends!  I had always thought Chatham was quite a big town but obviously it was smaller than I realised!




Mick’s father had died quite suddenly of viral pneumonia just two years before, in February 1962, and his mother’s sister, Aunty Lil, was a single lady who had moved in to live with them.  Mick’s brother Steve was only eighteen months younger than him and was working at the Trustee Savings Bank in Gillingham.  Steve was doing banking night classes at Evening College, he went on to become the youngest ever bank manager for the TSB.  Mick’s mother was in charge of all the admin and accounts for a local builder in Luton. I don’t think Vera actually ever recovered from becoming a widow so tragically quickly, and so young. I don’t think I understood that then, hindsight is a wonderful thing. I do know that the man who owned the business she worked for was a very nice guy, I can’t remember his name now but he had an elderly, infirm mother he lived with and my mother knew them through her job as a district nurse. He was obviously desperately fond of Vera and took her away to the Swiss Alps on holiday.  She brought back a Swiss Smoking Man ornament which I know my sons will remember.
Every Thursday night Mick went to the pub with his mates for a boys night out and played Sunday morning football in the Medway league for Chatham Standards where his friend Geoff Garvey was a journalist. Nearly every Saturday night we all met up together. We quite often met at the Bull Hotel in Rochester High Street. I loved all the company, most of the guys were dockyard trained apprentices. Ron was an engineer, Clive was a shipwright, but there were also other people who had completed their training in other engineering companies such as Wingets and Blaw-Knox  that supplied components for the dockyard. We all went to dances at Wingets in the staff canteen where there was always a live band, and also some very good dances at Chatham Town Hall.
On our own, Mick and I would sometimes go to the cinema and sit in the one and nines (1/9d), which were the back rows, where all the courting couples sat!  Sometimes we went to one of the local pubs in Luton including the Elephant and Castle on the corner of Luton Road and Castle Road where Mick’s mother often worked as a barmaid or further along the Luton Road to a small pub called the Richard Cobden. On Sunday evenings we occasionally went to the Royal Navy Association club on the Luton Road,  I believe Mick’s mother went almost every week.  There was always some live music and lots of dancing.  I liked to do the March of the Mods and the Veleta, a sequence waltz which we did in lines with our arms around each other’s waists. We also did the Gay Gordons and a Barn Dance, it was great fun. In the summer we would often go the Gillingham Strand to play tennis.
Obviously I continued to go to art college and Mick and I would sometimes go to visit Penny in her digs. She had so little money to live on, I’ll always remember one evening when it was very cold, trying to keep the coal fire going and Penny decided to burn her old shoes! The smell was awful and I wouldn’t recommend it!  As the autumn of 1964 became winter, instead of our weekly outdoor sketching sessions we sometimes took our boards to one of the coffee bars, where we would sit at the back with a permanent coffee getting cold, the Kinks  and the Animals playing on the jukebox and sketch the people sitting at the tables.  I remember women in their headscarves or Rainmates (plastic concertina-folded head coverings), old men with their newspapers and large mugs of steaming tea chatting happily and appearing oblivious to Penny, Susan and I scribbling away!  We had a very interesting new teacher for painting.  We used hard board for painting on as that was much cheaper than canvas and we treated the boards first, covering them with white emulsion paint.  We would paint a whole side with the brush in vertical strokes only, then when the paint was dry, turn the board 90* and again paint with the brush vertically.  That gave the board a canvas texture. The new teacher set up a display of chairs with coloured bottles, we had to sketch these onto our boards then paint the spaces in between rather than the items themselves.  I found the idea fascinating and obviously it made a big impression on me as I have never forgotten it.
One evening when Mick and I were sitting in the Cobden as the aforementioned pub was called locally, I jokingly replied to a comment he had made, that I might be already be married.  His reply was “Oh that’s a shame, I might want to marry you myself” and that was it, my proposal! That was at the beginning of 1965 and just before my eighteenth birthday.  For my birthday Mick gave me a lovely gold locket.




Just before Easter the foundation year students were all asked to complete their application forms for the colleges they wanted to study at for their art degree later that year in September.  Obviously I wanted to stay fairly near home and Mick, so I put Maidstone as my first choice and, with no advice, stupidly put St. Martins in London as my second choice and Hornsea Art College as my third. My second and third choice, unbeknown to me were always filled by first choice students because they were so famous.  When we returned to college for the summer term we had to prepare our portfolios with our work for our interviews.  We selected our choice of our work and had to mount it for presentation. We did not receive any advice or help with our selection which, again with hindsight, was probably not a good thing.  We had no experience or idea what was going to be looked for at the interview. Students that came from artistic families obviously had quite an advantage.  I remember feeling a bit like a fish out of water at the interview in front of the college principal and two other male interviewers.
A couple of weeks later we received letters telling us whether we had been successful or not, and that our forms would be passed on to our second choices. Obviously I was not successful and there was no opportunity of a second choice. Clearing systems were not in existence then.  I decided I would look for a job instead.  I went to the careers office in Chatham to ask if they had any suitable vacancies and I was offered a three week temporary job as a telephonist at the Tax Office.  Why on earth they thought I could operate the large, plug in switchboard I have absolutely no idea!  I did though, have to ask the college for a reference.  I returned to the Art college and asked if I could speak to the principal and I told him what I had done and asked if it was possible for them to give me a reference.  He then told me that, at interview, we had all been graded and I had been graded B+.  Everyone who had achieved an A was awarded a place and he told me that if I reapplied it was very likely that I would be accepted.  I went home and gave this a great deal of thought.  One thing I felt, after the time I had spent at college, was that you either were an artist or you weren’t.  I truthfully felt I wasn’t and that I had a different future ahead of me.  Looking back, I really do believe it is much better to leave home to go away to college and live in a different environment and that is what I would advise anyone although I honestly have no regrets at all for the decisions I made.
So, off I went to three weeks work at the local tax office and, somehow, I did manage that switchboard. I also continued working Saturdays in Woolworths while I looked for a permanent job.  I had been working for some time on the tea bar in Woolworths.  It literally was a bar at the back of the shop.  The customers came and queued for sandwiches, cakes and drinks which they ate and drank standing at the bar using china cups and plates, no takeaways then! There was a huge urn of hot water and heated cabinets with sausage rolls and pasties. There was also a dumb waiter that went up to the kitchen above, where I often worked during the morning slicing fresh ham and corned beef on the food slicer and cutting up the fresh bread to make the sandwiches.  These were usually made to order so there was no waste. I learned how to make bread pudding with any left over bread; I thought then, and still do, that it was lovely! We also did all the washing up by hand and enjoyed being able to play the transistor radio .  One of our favourite songs that summer was ‘Concrete and Clay’ by Unit 4+2.

“You to me
Are sweet as roses in the morning
And you to me
Are soft as summer rain at dawn, in love we share
That something rare”

I liked working downstairs on the counter too, although we didn’t sell hot chocolate, we did have a huge trade box of Horlicks under the counter and also made cups of hot, strong tea and coffee using the hot water from the urn.  I did not receive any health and safety training and I’m fairly certain there wasn’t any.  We did have to wear clean catering whites and cover our hair.  One of the things I didn’t really like was that, on Saturdays, after spending their lunchtime in the pub, the Eagle Tavern in Military Road, Mick sometimes came to find me with his friends.  I was very embarrassed and often tried to hide behind the tea urn!  If I was working upstairs, Vera who was the tea bar supervisor, used to shout up the dumb waiter “Eileen, come down here, there is someone asking for you!”  She knew I would be mortified!
Work was very easy to find in the second part of the 1960s. The local newspaper was full of vacancies each week, obviously none of us owned a PC in those days so letters of application had to be written, neatly, by hand. Interestingly I had never been told about CV’s so whether one should have been sent or not, I have no idea. The first job I applied for was as a clerk to the Home Care Organiser for Gillingham Borough Council. I carefully wrote my application letter giving details of my education and work experience in Woolworths and my temporary three weeks at the tax office. A week later, I received a reply asking me to come for an interview. Dr. Helen Mair was the head of the health department at the council offices and also of the children’s clinic in the town. She had a male deputy whose name I cannot remember, but he had a lovely face and was a very kindly gentleman.  I was interviewed by the deputy and also the Home Care Organiser.  After my interview was successful, I gave in my notice at Woolies, leaving with fantastic memories of a very happy time as a Saturday girl and part time member of staff. 
My job at Gillingham Borough Council was to sit in the small office dedicated to Home Care, take messages and record them in the message book. I had to organise any typing that needed to be done and take it through to Kay in the main office who would type it. I also got everything filed and in order. One event I do remember clearly was the day the Home Care Organiser returned at lunch time with two fresh peaches, one for each of us.  I had never had a fresh peach before and it sounds ridiculous now, but I had to ask how you ate it.  She said “go to the kitchen and fetch two plates and a sharp knife” and off I went to the staff kitchen returning with the plates and knife.  She then showed me how to peel and slice it into segments. She put a spoonful of sugar on the side of the plate and dipped each segment into the sugar before eating it and declaring it was the best tasting fruit ever! I can remember being very excited and was pleased at the end of the day to go home and tell my mother. 
 To be honest, I did find the job very boring after while, and, as a very talkative 18 year old, I was quite lonely working in a small office on my own most of the time. I had only been there a few months when a vacancy arose in the large main office looking after the children’s health care records. I think six people worked together and my desk was opposite a girl called Avis Negas; we were the same age and became good friends.  On Thursdays Avis and I took it in turns to walk down to the children’s clinic. We helped to provide the new mothers with their free orange juice and cod liver oil and often weighed the babies too, recording their weight in their little record books. Watching ‘Call the Midwife’ has brought back lots of memories.  I loved working with all the midwives there.
I’m not sure really how much work we actually got done but there were always huge piles of school health records waiting to be filed. The filing was kept in large four drawer cabinets. Under each school name, all the pupils were filed in alphabetical order and I was taught how to file the different ‘Macs’ and the correct way to file double barrelled surnames.
In 1965 Avis and I both got engaged at about the same time, Avis to her fiancé Jack and me to Mick. Mick and I spent most of the summer weekends playing tennis together at the Strand and meeting up with our friends.  The boys all went off one Saturday on their Jolly Boys outing to Margate.  One lovely summer evening we met up with friends, who had their own transport, to spend an evening in the Pub Garden in Yalding on the River Medway. Mick introduced me to his friend Pete Last who had gone to Chatham Boys’ Technical School. He worked for Bowaters in the new production centre in Rainham, Kent on the main A2. Pete was a successful salesman and owned a new Mini Cooper, really impressive! Pete introduced his girlfriend Sue, who I was amazed to find I knew! Sue had been to the same school as me and had been in the same year group, although not the same class.




In September 1965. Mick and I went on holiday to a holiday camp on the Isle of Wight with Mick’s old school friend Geoff Garvey and his girlfriend June.  I had to ask my parents’ permission, and assured them that June and I would be sharing a chalet.  We all went on the train from Paddington station to Portsmouth where we caught the ferry to the Isle of Wight.  When we arrived we then caught a little steam train on the island to take us to the camp, Gurnard Pines, which was near to Cowes. We really did have a lovely week.  It was the first time I had ever stayed at a holiday camp and I thought it great fun. I even sang with the pianist in the bar one night!
Even though we did not have a car, we enjoyed catching a bus to Shanklin Old Village and walking down the Chine. We went on a coach trip to Blackgang Chine and I bought a little lighthouse of coloured sand to take home, which was something all the tourists did. 




When we got home, we started planning our engagement party, but before that we had to be able to afford to buy the ring! Mick found a new job, looking in the Evening Standard that was full of job vacancies in London at that time, and he successfully applied to Unilever Industrial Soaps which had a sales office in Wapping. He became a sales order clerk and commuted from Chatham by train every day. His salary improved and he was able to claim his superannuation payments back from Kent County Council giving him enough money to buy the all important ring! One Saturday afternoon we went to a small, family jewellers in Military Road in Chatham, E M Vincent. It was so romantic and exciting! I chose a small diamond solitaire ring and it was given to Mick to keep in a white, small, heart shaped box to keep until THE DAY.  I bought him a plain, gold signet ring.
We had arranged to get engaged on the 5th November 1965 and to hold a party in the back room of a pub in which we often all met, on Delce Road in Rochester called the Rising Sun. It wasn’t a very big room and we took along Mick’s Dansette record player and his box of records which also contained my much smaller collection.  Our friends all brought records to play too.  My cousin Julie came from West Malling, and obviously Mick’s brother Steve, with his new girlfriend Joy but they were the only family members there. We had lots of friends and Pete Last came with his new girlfriend Pat. I really can’t imagine what sort of event she thought he had brought her to but it was a very happy evening.
The food consisted of lots of crisps and the guys all bought loads of beer over the bar and brought it into the back room.  They also bought all the drinks for the girls, that seemed to be the way it was then, and I don’t think it took too many Babychams, Cherry Bs, or lager and limes to get the girls very merry! I do remember there seemed to be quite a lot of spilt beer on the floor, and nearly everyone smoked so it was very smoky too! The evening ended when last orders were called at 11pm, but the evenings also started much earlier then too. It was a very happy night.  I had bought a special ‘little black dress’ for the occasion. It was black lace over black satin and ever so  grown up, as the little black dress had only just become de rigeur. I wore it with my new black high heels and seamed nylon stockings.  Tights had yet to be invented but followed on quite soon as it was not easy to wear the latest short skirts with stockings.
After we got engaged, I started a collection for my ‘bottom drawer’  something that we were all encouraged by our mothers to do in those days. The phrase started in the late 1800s when the bottom drawer of a chest was used by a betrothed lady to collect and store items for her marriage. I put away any presents we were given on our engagement but also small things I had collected like an embroidered tray cloth and when Mick went on his Jolly Boys outing to Margate, he won a bingo prize in Dreamland and chose a set of baking tins to proudly bring back for me!  This was obviously true love!
In the spring of 1966 Pete Last came to talk to Mick and I one evening, at Mick’s mother’s house.  As well as being a salesman for Bowaters, Pete had also become a part time salesman for Provident Life. Pete came to show us a product that could guarantee that we would be able to have a mortgage to buy our own first home. If we were regular savers for a year Provident Life (I believe they were called Friends Provident then) would guarantee us a 75% mortgage up to a certain amount.  If we saved with them for two years we could have had a 90% mortgage.  Saving up was quite difficult when we didn’t earn a great deal of money but we found a way to do it and took out a policy.  We already knew we were not planning to wait another two years but if we bought a house which was less than £3,000 we would be able to save £750. With very careful planning, £40 per month (plus a little bit extra) over eighteen months would just do it!




July 1966 was the well-remembered Football World Cup final with England as defending world champions. As it was on a Saturday, I remember watching it on my own, as Mick was at the pub with all his mates.  There seemed to be an unspoken rule then that girls were not allowed! I think the guys had their special occasions where no girlfriends or wives were allowed!  We didn’t question it, that was just the way things were.
Also in July 1966 the twenty-first British Grand Prix was held in Kent at Brands Hatch.  Avis’s mother was a waitress working for an outside catering firm who were providing the buffets and silver service waitressing in the marquees. Whilst at work one day, Avis asked me if I would like to work that Saturday with her and her mother at the Grand Prix!  How exciting, of course I said yes but that I didn’t really think Woolies tea bar had given me the experience for waitressing! I was told that didn’t matter, as I could help with the buffet.  We had to wear black dresses with white pinafores, and these were provided.  We met in Gillingham High Street and were all taken to Brands Hatch in coaches.  It was a very warm day and hard work, but I loved being part of such a big occasion and learned quite a lot about buffet catering which I had never seen before.  It was all of a very high standard and I suspect was put on for people with quite a lot of money or for important clients. The food looked and smelled fabulous. Champagne was also kept on ice, the first time I had seen real champagne!
Another really exciting day that summer was when Avis and I were asked by our department head if we would be attendants to that year’s “Miss Gillingham” on the Margate carnival float.  Of course we were both delighted to be asked and said yes! I bought myself a plain, cotton, pale blue dress for the occasion in a shift style which was the height of fashion then. I wore it for several years as I loved it so much.  The three of us were chauffeur driven in the Mayor’s liveried car to Margate quite early on the Saturday morning and we were all given beautiful bouquets of flowers to hold.  The Gillingham float was a small, well-decorated, lorry with three ‘thrones’ placed on it.  We joined in the carnival procession which was huge with different marching bands from the various services, various clubs which had put in entries and the many Kent towns who had sent their Queens and escorts. We processed along the full length of Margate seafront, starting from the car park by the railway station through to the Lido in Cliftonville.  Lots of people lined the route and it was all incredibly exciting.  After the parade we were served tea in the Lido and the Queens were whisked away to eat and get changed ready for the evening Miss Kent beauty contest, in the Lido that evening. Avis and I obviously had some free time but were very much looking forward to the evening as we had been told there was a carnival dance we would be going to and there would be one of those buffets!  Eventually, it was all over, Miss Gillingham hadn’t become Miss Kent but we were all certain she had been the best, and we were returned home by the Mayors chauffeur. What a day!




Our friends Clive and Jo got married in the spring of that year. He had trained as a shipwright in the dockyard and had a well paid job. They were able to afford a very nice new home in Hempstead. It was almost £4,000.00! In August Mick and I went to the Torbay Chalet Hotel holiday park with Clive and Jo. Of course I had my engagement ring then but I have to admit to going to Woolworths to buy a cheap wedding ring for our holiday, it turned my finger green! The weather was beautiful and we really had a very nice time in Devon making friends with a young family when we sat at long tables in the main dining room for meals. Lots of people enjoyed going to holiday camps for their holidays, with all the entertainment they wanted provided for all the family.
After we returned home I decided to look for another job.  Mick and I were saving for the deposit for our new home and every month he gave his pay cheque to his Aunty Lil to pay into the Bradford and Bingley Building Society.  Aunty Lil was a Senior Executive Officer in the civil service in Holborn, London and had lived with Mick’s family since her sister, Mick’s mother, Vera, had been widowed. The building society was close to her office and she had seen they were offering good rates of interest. She was also very keen on our savings plan and I suspect she was making sure we really were saving (though that didn’t occur to me at the time)! I shared my monthly pay with Mick after we had both given our mothers £5 for our monthly keep. The system seemed to work well for us.  We both had bus season tickets and had enough for everything else. There was no such thing as a mobile phone and we didn’t have a car. Mick’s family didn’t have a home phone and we only had one I think because of my mother’s work.  It had been another lovely summer and Mick and I, together with his brother Steve, spent a couple of weekends painting the front of his mother’s home. We had the transistor radio out the front of the house with us probably playing the music far too loud!
Again, looking in the paper for work was quite easy and I selected an advertisement for a clerical officer with the Ministry of Pensions and National Insurance.  I believe that is now the Department of Work and Pensions. It was at Crown House in The Brook, Chatham so was also much easier for me to get to as it meant I did not have to change buses.  Also I had continued to visit my beloved Aunty Bowsher and it meant I could now walk to her house once a week for tea.  Later in the evening I would then walk to Mick’s house before catching the last bus home.
On 21st October 1966 I caught the bus from work to Castle Road and walked to Mick’s home to have tea with them. As I walked into the dining room which was really the only room they used, kept warm with a lovely coal fire, everyone was staring at the television.  I remember standing in the doorway realising something was very wrong.  “What’s happened?” I asked also looking at the black and white television screen. (There were no colour TVs then) and Vera and Aunty Lil looked at me quite shocked.  “Don’t you know?” they replied but I had been at work all day and was not aware of the dreadful disaster that had occurred in Aberfan in Wales. There had been a catastrophic collapse of the colliery tip which had slid down the mountain onto the Pantglas village junior school. 116 children and 28 adults had been killed. It was a huge shock to all the nations of the UK but obviously the coal mining area of Wales were totally devastated. The coal tips, like the mines, were owned by the National Coal Board the remaining coal tips were eventually removed after a lengthy fight between the Aberfan residents and the Coal Board.
At the MPNI I worked on the decisions to pay short term benefits; they were sickness benefit, maternity benefit and Industrial injury benefit. When I first started I had to go on a four week course in Reading and stay in lodgings Monday to Friday which were organised for me by the Civil Service. The four weeks were split into two fortnights and we spent the two weeks between the course back at work putting into action what we had learned. My lodgings were in Tilehurst a suburb of Reading and a girl from another office was lodged with me. We stayed in a couple’s home whose children had flown the nest. They were nice people and tried hard to make us comfortable. Apart from camping with the Brownies and Guides it was the first time I had lived with strangers away from home. Mick would come to Chatham Station on a Sunday night to wave me off although on the first weekend I left he came to London with me and on the tube to Paddington station where I took the train to Reading.  I remember always knowing we were approaching Reading station by the big Huntley and Palmers biscuit factory which in 2006 was re-established in Sudbury, Suffolk.  For me, whenever I think of Reading, I remember that biscuit factory and the sweet smell as you arrived at the station! The songs I remember from that time were The New Vaudeville Band singing Winchester Cathedral, Wild Thing by The Troggs and Paperback Writer, The Beatles.
I enjoyed working at Crown House. We worked in small groups of four, with our four desks together beside a big picture window.  Crown House was fairly newly built and very modern. Of course, everything was done by hand and we had to compute the benefit due in our heads! There were systems for everything and the units, as each claimant’s file was called, were handed over on completion to be filed by the Clerical Assistants.  We all smoked at our desks, how awful that must have been! There was also a public office at the front of our building and we took turns to man the desk. There were always two of us on duty and claimants took seats in rows facing us until they were called.  There were no barriers between us and the public.  Sometimes we could deal with the query but sometimes we had to invite someone into a private interview room. Again, no barriers, no panic alarms, were people more kindly back then? The most difficult cases tended to be the Industrial Injury ones, which sometimes had to go before a board and the details of the interview had to be careful written down to be presented. I remember one of our regulars was a very difficult gentleman who walked with a stick and would walk straight up to the desk and bang it on the counter demanding to be served.  We could hear it in the back office and always one of the guys would go out to help whoever the poor soul was on the counter.
That year Mick also once again changed his job.  Unilever decided to take Industrial Soaps from Wapping to their offices Port Sunlight and, at that stage in our lives, we decided we did not want to live there.  I don’t even think Mick was offered a job transfer or, if he was, he didn’t tell me!  He decided to try to find work locally and succeeded in getting a clerical job at Medway Buildings in Rochester. He was very happy working with a team of people there.




1967 was a very exciting year. The first few months were spent house hunting, to see what we could buy with our savings and mortgage of £3,000.00. We eventually decided we would probably buy in the poets area of Gillingham.  These were nice, terraced, 1930s, bay windowed houses.  The ceilings were reasonably high and they appeared to be well built.  The kitchens and bathrooms were quite small but they all had quite reasonable back gardens, often narrow but quite long. We didn’t own a car so it didn’t worry us that there was no garage. There were also lots of new Wimpey Homes being built in the Medway towns and the Greater London Council (GLC) was giving mortgages to Londoners who wanted to move out of London and commute to work.  However, we did not want a GLC mortgage as we were already promised one with Provident Life. That spring we had a lovely surprise; my neighbour from when I lived in Herman Terrace, Mrs French, told Aunty Bowsher she may have a house we would be interested in. Her son had married a few years before and his wife was looking to sell her old family home.  Her widowed father was having to move into nursing care and the house was being sold. They would accept £3250.00 for it. It was a lovely 1950s three-bedroomed semi-detached house in Longhill Avenue, Chatham, with views over the whole of Chatham down to the river and part of Rochester. It had a double bay window at the front and at the back and faced on to the Great Lines with a footpath I could walk every day to my job at Crown House.  The  only downside was that it was built into the side of the North Downs so there was a very steep flight of concrete steps down to the garden from the kitchen, which, at the time, did not worry us at all. There was also a cellar, which was accessed from the garden where there was a door in the wall under the dining room bay window.  The deal was made and they extremely kindly gave us the keys even before the mortgage was finalised. I was over the moon!
Wedding plans went ahead. My parents were paying for our reception which was to be a late lunch at the Prince of Wales Hotel in Chatham.  We received the menus and chose a three course meal.  I believe it was a prawn cocktail (newly fashionable in the 1960s!), roast pork, and trifle for dessert. One of my mother’s patient’s daughters made the bridal bouquet, posies for the two bridesmaids, corsages for both mothers and carnation buttonholes for the groom, best man and guests. I loved the old-fashioned, trailing bouquets and mine included golden yellow roses to match the colour of the bridesmaid’s dresses. The bridesmaids had small white posies. My mother also organised for a lovely wedding cake made by another of her patients. One of the men I worked with in our group of four, Harry Tyrell, arranged for our wedding invitations to be printed and also his son, an amateur photographer, took the wedding photos. Mick arranged for our wedding cars which were from a firm of mini cabs as they were cheaper than taxis. I was thrilled, as my very close friend Pat Last was a very good dressmaker, as well as a secretary, and she offered to make my wedding dress. I chose a simple pattern with a fashionable Kaftan shape but with a high waist and a lovely long train from under the waist band. I borrowed a head dress from my friend Linda at work. My mother made a bridesmaids dress for my sister after Pat had cut the pattern material and tacked it together and my young cousin, Julie Brown from North Shields was my other bridesmaid.  Her Mother, my Aunty Lilly Brown, made her dress. I wanted the wedding outfits to be white and gold so the bridesmaid’s dresses were a lovely gold satin and my white satin wedding dress had gold braided trim. Their dresses were the same pattern as my wedding dress but without the train. 
We seem to have been very busy all that summer. Mick and I decorated the very basic, tiny kitchen in Longhill Avenue. We chose yellow paint for the walls, and the ceiling, which was in quite poor condition, we covered in stick on white ceiling tiles.  These were newly fashionable at the time but subsequently proved a huge fire risk. I loved the summer afternoons we spent there.  There was a beautiful laburnum tree in the garden that was so far below the kitchen window, I remember looking out of the kitchen window directly on to the beautiful yellow blossoms. The kitchen had a very useful, large, under stairs larder cupboard, an old gas stove and on the wall beside the sink,  a gas Ariston multi water heater which also heated the hot water for the bathroom above. The sink and draining board was under the kitchen window with a cupboard underneath. There was also a long shelf on the wall above the stove where I kept all my pots and pans. We were very lucky as some of the house furniture was being left and we were very grateful to have something to start us off, as every penny we had saved was going towards purchasing our home, helping to pay for the wedding and also our honeymoon. 
The honeymoon cost us the grand sum of £22.00 each, all of one month of Mick’s salary.  We booked through Headway Travel in Chatham to go to Jersey for a week.  People had only recently started to go on an airplane for their holidays and there was only one London Airport, now known as London Heathrow. The plane we flew in was a prop plane. Mick’s mother thought we were mad spending our money on a honeymoon like that but we wanted something we would always remember and I have never had any regrets. We stayed in a very nice guest house in St Helier, Clarendon Court. We had our own wash basin in the bedroom but had to walk along the corridor to the toilet and bathroom.  Generally the only showers anyone had then were rubber hoses pushed on to bath taps and you still probably only had a bath once a week washing your own ‘personal bits’ with a flannel daily! Two other honeymoon couples were staying there too and we all made friends going out on coach trips for entertainment on several evenings together. Twice we went on an evening coach trip to The Tartan Bar in Corbière. It was very well known for its tartan decor, live bands and sometimes even well known artists. I won the ‘Miss Lovely Legs’ competition and once, even sang with the band!

She’s leaving home




We were married on 30th September 1967 at St Paul’s Church on the New Road in Chatham. My father had to give me permission to be married as I was under 21 years old! The church was the one I had attended as a little girl, when I was allowed to, with Aunty Bowsher.  It is now sadly demolished along with very many of the other buildings of my childhood including the wonderful library on the New Road, designed by George E. Bond. I remember that BBC Radio One also started on the same day we got married. Tony Blackburn (who had previously worked for pirate radio on Radio Caroline in the North Sea) launched the station with the very popular “Flowers in the Rain’ by The Move. That song always reminds me of our wedding day!
Our wedding day was very special.  Aunty Lily brought my cousin Julie to our home to get dressed as a bridesmaid with my sister that morning. Aunty Lily was so proud of the lovely dress she had made but that I had not been able to see until they arrived, because they lived on Tyneside. Julie was only about ten and was quite shy, my sister was just sixteen and looked after her. Mick’s friend Clive who had a car and had offered to collect and deliver the wedding flowers, arrived at the bottom of the drive and I was peering out of the window to see if Mick was in the car with him, I wanted to check he was still going to turn up!  Eventually everybody left for the church and only my father and I were left in the house waiting for the wedding car to arrive.  My father seldom commented on any of the major events in my life but I do remember us standing together in our hall and he turned to me and said “Are you quite sure this is what you want to do?  This is your last chance to change your mind.” I was actually quite moved thinking how much he must have really cared for me. Of course I was sure, I was so looking forward to this next stage of my life!
All our friends and relations came to our wedding but sadly the photographer only took photos of the family, I would love to now look back on photos of our friends! One of the most memorable was Mick’s friend Geoff Garvey who arrived in the dining room at the hotel carrying a full size (real) stuffed bear! He had found him on the landing and decided it should attend our wedding breakfast! I don’t know of anyone else who had a stuffed bear attend their wedding!
After the meal I changed in one of the hotel rooms into my ‘going away’ outfit.  Something every bride used to do. I wore a matching dress and coat outfit in lime green with a navy blue stripe. I had a matching lime green hat. Clive and his wife Jo brought their car to the front door and everyone came to wave us off on our journey to London for the night, before going to London Airport the next day.  After the wedding I understand both families had their own parties in their own homes and our friends all went to the pub.




Just over a week later, after a wonderful honeymoon in Jersey we arrived at our first home together in Longhill Avenue. Mick’s mum Vera and Aunty Lil had decorated our dining/sitting room as a surprise and my mother had made up our new bed and everywhere was spotlessly clean.  I couldn’t have been happier!  We unpacked our wedding presents and were so delighted to have just enough of everything to start us off in our new life. We didn’t have a fridge, washing machine, TV or phone but none of that seemed to matter. I took the sheets and towels to the launderette each week on my walk to work and collected them on my way home.  I washed everything else by hand and boiled the real hankies we all used back then, in a large saucepan on top of the gas stove, hardly anyone used tissues, if they were even invented!  A few months later my Dad gave us enough money to make a deposit on a small TV and we had to make the remaining monthly payments. I bought some curtain material in Rochester market and made curtains on an extremely old singer sewing machine which had been left in the house. I hung them very proudly in the front room and in the front bedroom windows. Before our first Christmas together in our home, Mick came home from work with a Rayburn heater to be fitted in the dining room fireplace. I think a friend must have brought him home as we didn’t have a car! The dining room was the only room we used downstairs then as we only had two fireside chairs, a table and chairs and a beautiful sideboard which I still have. And, of course the television! The view from our bay dining room window was quite amazing as we were so high up, I loved it!  The Rayburn was duly fitted so we were able to keep a fire in all night and that really helped to heat the house, which was very cold during the winter. No one we knew had central heating back then. In December we bought a little ‘Esso Blue’ paraffin heater to keep alight in the hall try to heat upstairs too.
January 1968 was exciting, I was going to be twenty one, the age you could vote, and were looked on as an adult, able to get married without your parents consent and to receive the key of your front door!  Almost everyone had one by then anyway but the old music hall song was still sung! I was to have a small, family birthday party and I was lucky that my actual birthday fell on a Friday. It was a cold, wet January morning and I set off to walk to work feeling full of excited anticipation. I had booked an afternoon off work so I could get all the food ready for my party. I think about twenty people were invited from both our families.
I rushed home from work at lunch time, turning my key in the front door hoping there would be some birthday cards but, no, there were none and I was very disappointed. We had really nice, kind next door neighbours, a young couple in their early thirties called Win and Frank. They had three children. I knocked at their door and asked Win if the postman had been. She said he had and when I told her about my birthday cards she asked if I had looked in the side entrance. I quickly rushed to check and there they were! I then lit the fire in the front room which was very sparsely furnished although it did have the new curtains I had made and there was also an old, large, drop leaf oak table which had been left by the previous owners. I opened it out and proudly covered it with a new table cloth which had been saved in my bottom drawer. It was still a novelty to me to be able to use our own possessions, of which I was very proud!
We always made our own food then, hardly anyone bought ready-made meals.  The only things I remember as a treat were cakes from the bakers shop and very rarely fish and chips or a pie from the pie shop!  For my party I first made some sausage rolls then while the oven was on I made some fruit buns and a Victoria sandwich cake. A selection of sandwiches followed and the party food was ready! When Mick arrived home from work he got washed and changed and set up our small, Dansette record player and laid out some beer glasses for the men. I really don’t remember that the ladies even drank alcohol at home, or if they did I have no idea what we would have provided.  I do know it wasn’t wine and I do know I had set out all our cups and saucers for tea! The people who came were our immediate family, Mick’s aunts, uncles and cousins and my mum, dad and sister. I think they brought Aunty Bowsher too. So that was my twenty-first birthday!




By the time Easter came that year, I was fairly certain I was pregnant. There were no pregnancy tests then, you just waited until you thought you should go and ask a doctor and they sent off a urine sample to a laboratory to be tested on rats or mice I believe. You had to wait two weeks for the result. Well my test was positive. All the family were delighted especially Mick’s family as his cousin Malcolm’s wife Rita was also pregnant and her baby was due early that summer.
In the spring of 1968 Mick searched for a new job on a better wage. especially as I  had discovered that I was pregnant. Again, he went back to London in an admin supervisory role at Joe Lyons in Cadby Hall, their head office in Hammersmith. He really enjoyed his work there managing a team of mainly female staff. He sometimes caught a bus in the morning to Chatham railway station and he sometimes walked; it really depended on the weather.  I always walked to work at Crown House as it was a beautiful walk across the top of the Great Lines and down the footpath behind Chatham Town Hall. I loved the view and the wide open space. I have always loved a good view!
In May we went on holiday with our friends, Geoff and June Garvey. We went in Geoff’s car and drove up through Yorkshire spending a few days at Aysgarth Falls which was beautiful and then on to Blackpool spending a few nights at a guest house. It was my first experience of Blackpool, it was before the summer season had started and was rather cold. I was also three months pregnant and every evening I felt as if someone had hit me on the head I was so tired! After our week’s holiday we all went back to work.
My friend at work, Carol Pearce, had had her first baby just before Christmas the previous year and I had been a godmother to her son Michael. Michael had been a home birth and Carol was a mine of information advising me on things I should do.  I was determined to have a home birth too. I also started saving up and starting a layette for the baby which actually was the same idea as saving a bottom drawer to get married. Scans had not been invented then, so we had regular check ups with our designated midwife. I was still sitting at my desk in Crown House and we were all still sharing our cigarettes as we worked! I smoked Players Number 10 which were  a smaller cigarette but they had cigarette coupons to collect. Aunty Lil, Mick’s aunt, smoked Embassy filter cigarettes which also had coupons to collect. I know it sounds pretty awful now but many people collected cigarette coupons as well as Green Shield stamps, which you could collect from some grocery stores. To exchange for things that might be needed. We collected our cigarette coupons for a cot and a pushchair!  My friends and colleagues at work also had a collection for me before I left. Carol advised me that, with the collection money, I should suggest that they buy me a carrycot and then I could buy wheels for it so it could be used anywhere as a bed and we would be able to transport it too (not that we owned a car!) All I can remember from that summer was a feeling of great excitement and anticipation. I knew I would not be able to keep my job as there was no such thing then as paid maternity leave.  I left all that to think about another day!  We received a twenty two pound maternity grant from the government and again, on Carol’s advice, I spent it on buying a spin drier for all the Terry towelling nappies I would be washing, she was right about that too! There was also a maternity allowance which was paid for eighteen weeks.  I think it was £1/10/- a week. Most people took maternity allowance for six weeks before the baby was due and for twelve weeks afterwards but no job was held open for you, you were expected to be staying at home to look after your child.
It was a lovely summer of flower power and we wore flowers in our hair and around our necks. Mick and I went to my parents caravan on at least two occasions, once with our friends Geoff and June and once with Pete and Pat Last who had married in February that year. When we went to the caravan with Pete and Pat, we all went swimming from the sandy beach opposite the caravan park. I wore a two piece costume and took my baby bump into the water. We also enjoyed playing pitch and putt at East Cliff in Folkestone. We had previously enjoyed a few summers where the four of us had played tennis together in the evenings in the Medway Towns, sometimes on the Jackson Fields in Rochester and sometimes at The Strand in Gillingham.




Autumn came and I started waiting. I left work at the end of September. At the end of October Mick and I were going to a dance at Chatham Town Hall with our friends and I had nothing to wear!  All the pregnant ladies wore maternity dresses then and my mother had already made me two on her sewing machine.  I decided it was my turn to make one and I removed the train from my wedding dress and used the same, simple pattern my mother had used to make an A shaped, white satin dress, trimmed with the same gold braid, as a new dress. A week later I talked to the elderly neighbours on the other side of us who had a telephone line. They were very interested in having a new baby next door and had very kindly given us their front door key and told us we could use their phone to call the midwife when the time came.
That same night, on 7th November, I woke at three in the morning feeling rather damp and thought my waters must have broken. When I checked the bedding I discovered that the sheet was red with blood. I was shocked and frightened by what I found and I quickly woke Mick and asked him to go next door and use their phone to call the midwife. What I couldn’t work out was why I was not having contractions. It was something much worse, the placenta was coming away, although it took me serious thought to work that one out. 
In the meantime Mick was panicking, the key would not unlock the neighbour’s door, so he ran to the end of the road to use the telephone box. He then ran back home.  In that time, not only had I worked out what the problem was likely to be, I had also had realised that my baby might not have anything to keep them alive. I don’t know why I didn’t panic, I just didn’t.  The midwife arrived quickly, took one look and told Mick to run back to the phone box to call the doctor. She also berated me for the bedroom being too cold! I asked Mick to fetch our one and only, ancient, electric fire into the bedroom before he left for the phone box. After the call the doctor arrived quickly and I remember seeing his pyjama trousers sticking out of the bottom of his day trousers! He took one look and told Mick to knock the neighbours up and to use their phone to ring for an ambulance.
The cream painted ambulance arrived with a clanging bell and I was carried down the stairs on an ambulance chair and rushed to All Saints Hospital where my mother had worked, and which my primary school had been named after. It felt a bit like going home and I wasn’t frightened.  I understand that Mick then walked to his mother’s house and, as daylight came, went to yet another phone box to let my parents know.
The hospital night shift ended and the day shift began,  The nurse came regularly to check on me but I only developed very mild contractions.  At 10 in the morning, when my abdomen was listened to by the midwives using a stainless steel listening trumpet, they called in a senior nurse for help. She listened and said I had to be taken to theatre immediately. I did not have a pre-med but two nurses came to ask me to swallow a tube down into my stomach! I remember them saying they were amazed I was so calm, and I said ‘ what you are doing is to my body, I am in my head’. I was only twenty one years old and they said I was really young to think that way.  I have no idea why I said it but I do know that was how I felt.  I had to relax and let life take its course.
Robert George Ferguson was born by caesarian section at 11.15am. on Thursday 7th November. I had had a general anaesthetic, which I believe everyone did in those days, so it was quite a while before I knew what had happened. I do remember waking up back on the main ward and being offered a cup of tea by a ward orderly.  I was extremely thirsty, I downed the tea and was then promptly sick which brought a nurse running who told me I should not have had the tea! I was sore and didn’t really understand what had happened to me.  The maternity ward was always busy and even in those days, there was little support.  I could not even see my baby, I just felt sore und unwell from having a general anaesthetic. I think I must have then dozed intermittently until visiting time at 3pm. In those days visiting was only permitted twice a day, an hour in the afternoon and an hour in the evening.  Only two visitors were allowed and visitors were not allowed to sit on the bed. 
Mick came to see me at 3pm and I was so pleased to see him!  He told me we had a little boy, weighing in at 6lbs 8oz. Mick had been to see him, but he was in an incubator. What he didn’t tell me until several weeks later when it was obvious Robert was fine, was that the staff had told him that Robert was in an incubator as he was blue when born, because of loss of the placenta, and that he may have brain damage. What a burden for Mick to carry alone at that time.
The next three days were very strange. I was the only new mum on the ward of six that did not have a baby in a crib beside her. At that time the cribs were all removed and taken to the nursery overnight.  If a baby woke for a feed a nurse would wheel the baby back, then take it away again, as mothers were supposed to rest and sleep all night too! It was standard then, that when you had a baby by normal delivery, you stayed in hospital for ten days. If you had a cesarian section it was fourteen days.  It did seem like an eternity and especially for me as I had a home birth planned that had all gone straight out the window!  Robert stayed in the special baby unit in his incubator for three days. On the third day they took me to the unit to see him. I had started to feel most odd.  Obviously I knew I was in hospital and knew why but all I really wanted to do was go home and I don’t even think I had accepted that I had had a baby, just an operation. They took me to the unit in a wheelchair and when I got there I stood up and peered in the crib at this brand new baby who wasn’t blue at all! Unfortunately as I stood there the overwhelming heat of the unit hit me, I heard a rushing sound in my ears and fainted. They put me in the wheelchair again and took me back to the ward. Losing the placenta obviously meant I had lost a lot of blood, and I think I needed time to recover, quite apart from the shock at finding my young twenty one year old self in that predicament. 
On the morning of the fourth day I started to feel much brighter and was thrilled when the nurses brought a crib to me with Robert swaddled in his hospital night gown. That afternoon my mum and dad came to visit me and I was delighted to show them their first grandchild. I wanted to feed Robert but he was a ‘lazy’ baby according to the nurses and of course I had missed the first vital days of his life. We struggled on! On the tenth day it was discovered I had an infection, I never knew what, but I was moved to an isolation unit outside of the main hospital building. I think it was prefabricated and we each had our own room off a long corridor. I had bought a felt- toy making kit a few days before being taken in to hospital and Mick brought it in for me, plus a little matinée jacket I was knitting. I was pleased to have something to occupy me. There were no televisions or radios in the hospitals then and personal computers had not yet been invented, neither had mobile phones!
My fourteenth day arrived but I was not allowed home, it was a Thursday. In fact it wasn’t until Sunday 24th November that I was finally allowed to leave!  My parents were visiting that afternoon and had a car, and I already had a bag of my own belongings and clothes and a shawl for Robert. The nurse midwife gave me lots of instructions and off we went on the great adventure of the rest of our lives. I can remember asking the nurse what I should do if the baby wouldn’t stop crying and genuinely feeling a little bit worried that might happen. I know now that having a first baby is quite a scary situation. Fortunately babies are tough little humans and cope with having a mother who still has her L plates on !
When we got to our home in Longhill Avenue, the beautiful crib was as I had left it, in our bedroom ready for the new baby.  The new carrycot on its wheeled base was in our sitting room, as we only had the one room heated downstairs, and I carefully laid Robert in it. I then went upstairs to find Mick who did not know that we were coming home.  He had played Sunday League football that morning, and was sitting in very muddy looking bathwater, amazed to see me! I left him to sort himself out and came down stairs where I found my mother in the kitchen making a very welcome cup of tea, and my father very put out that Mick wasn’t waiting, clean and tidy for us to come home. I actually have no idea why he should think that, since we didn’t have a phone and we were not expected!




My mother was working full time as a district nurse, and she was very worried that I would not have any help.  Mick was commuting to London every day for work and there was certainly nothing like paternity leave then. On Monday morning mum called the home help service at the local council, indeed probably the same one I had worked for just a few years before. I was very lucky and received daily morning help Monday-Friday for the first two weeks. I can’t remember the lady’s name but she really was a godsend and a huge help.She gave me advice as well as boiling up the nappies in a pan on top of the stove, shopping, cooking, and cleaning, as I had been told I wasn’t to do any heavy lifting until I had my six week check-up. Robert did struggle with feeding and I had to supplement his feeds. He also had colic and would start crying about 11.0 pm every night. The good news was that he was passing the development tests and he was not brain damaged.  It was then that Mick told me what had been said to him in the baby unit. Oh my goodness, what a relief!
Christmas came very quickly. We put a small, real Christmas Tree on a little table in the corner of the room we used; I had recovered and was back to my usual self (apart from lack of sleep, I guess that’s why you have children when you are young enough to cope!). The only people I remember seeing that Christmas were our friends Carol and Brian who had their own small child Michael. They had a car and came and collected us on Boxing Day. We played Mahjong with them which they had been teaching us for a while.
I loved the beautiful navy blue and cream Silver Cross pram that I had bought secondhand from our friends Ron and Jean, whose own son had finished with it and was now in a pushchair. We kept the pram in our front room which still had very little furniture in it. I had to pull it up the short flight of steps from our front door to the footpath and it had a tray underneath for the shopping. I loved pushing the pram to the park or to the shops in Chatham High Street at the bottom of the very steep hill.  Of course that did mean I had to push it back up again with all the shopping too, no wonder I was always skinny! Life did get easier though, as my parents decided to give us their old Service washing machine and their old fridge which looked large but wasn’t inside. The washing machine had to be dragged up to the sink and an electric wringer put in place on the back of the machine so the washing could be scooped out of the tub and put through the wringer into the clean water in the sink.  You then put the lid back on the machine and reversed the process, putting the rinsed clothes back through the wringer to be hung out to dry.  That spin dryer I bought with my maternity allowance was worth its’ weight in gold!  Anyway, no more washing set to boil on the top of the cooker!
During the spring and summer I visited my mum in their bungalow on quite a few of her days off.  She often worked one day at the weekend and had a day off in the week.  I used to travel out to their home in Walderslade on the bus from the Luton Arches, using the same bus stops that I had once used to go to school.  I took Robert in the carrycot and folded down the wheels to go in the luggage rack.  I even took him to Mick’s office in Cadby Hall in London on the train on one occasion the same way.  That was quite hard work and I did have to ask strangers for help. I had decorated the small bedroom so that Robert could sleep in a cot instead of the crib, which by then he had grown out of ,and we had been using the carrycot on its stand for him. I bought a yellow wallpaper with a small stripe for three of the walls and found, in a reduced bin, two lovely rolls of Teddy Bear paper. I wallpapered all the walls, having watched and learned from my father during my childhood. Mick painted the door, skirting board and window sill white. I didn’t like painting so we worked well as a team. We built the cot we had purchased with embassy cigarette coupons and put in a small chest of drawers the shop gave us when we had purchased our new bed and wardrobe from them. I found a small green rug also left by the previous owners which I spread out on the Lino floor. It was lovely to have a special room for our little son.




I blame a cup of cocoa…




My doctor would not prescribe the contraceptive pill as he said it was too ‘new’ and just dismissed me. Later that summer Mick and I took Robert on holiday with our friends Carol and Brian with their son Michael.  We went by coach to a holiday park in Caister, Norfolk and had a lovely week even though the weather wasn’t very good. Carol and I entered our babies in the Bonnie Baby competition and Mick entered the knobbly knees contest. None of us won anything, but it was fun.  They also has a nightly baby patrol which meant that some evenings we went to the entertainment centre and bar. Every so often there would be an announcement “Baby crying in chalet 6”, “Baby crying in chalet 24”!  At the end of the week, on the way home in the coach, I had a funny feeling and I said to Mick, “I think I’m pregnant again”. He said “Don’t be silly, you can’t be” but I knew I could! That was early in September 1969 almost two years after we had married and a couple of months before Robert’s first birthday.
In November Mick’s brother Steve married Joy Hubbard, she had been his only girlfriend but they were alike in many ways and seemed very happy together. Joy and Steve had also grown up in the same road and knew each other’s families. Steve was a really nice guy, quietly spoken and very caring. He and Mick did not have a lot in common but they still cared about each other and had grown up sharing a double bed. Mick was Steve’s best man and I remember standing to one side and talking to Rita who was married to Mick’s cousin Malcolm. They had a little boy Sean who was a couple of months older than Robert and I told her I thought I might be pregnant again she replied she thought she was too and we were both waiting for the results of our pregnancy tests.
That autumn we saw quite a lot of our friends Pete and Pat Last.  They used to visit us in the evening and we would all play cards.  Sometimes we would walk down to Mick’s mother’s home in Dagmar Road on a Sunday afternoon. I remember that the next Christmas we had what would become our traditional Boxing Day party for the first time since Mick’s father had died seven years prior. Aunty Dot and Uncle Charlie would drive up from Sheerness with their two daughters Susan and Linda.  Vera, Mick’s mother, would often have her friend George there too. We ate cold turkey sandwiches with lovely home made picked onions Aunty Dot had made. We had crisps and sausage rolls and lots of beer and whiskey.  I probably drank Babycham, and I know I loved snowballs. Everyone got merry, George recited ‘There was a little sparrow, flew all the way to Spain’ and Uncle Charlie’s party piece was ‘Right in the Middle of the Road’.  That first year we left early to bring Robert home, but before we left we all had to process up the back path into the alley turning left into Clarence Road then left again at the end of the road back up Dagmar Road to re-enter the house by the front door. For some completely unknown reason, as we processed, we had to sing ‘Loyd George knew my father, my father knew Loyd George’!  Aunty Lil, who lived with her sister Vera never participated, but she did babysit for us. We were all packed into quite a small room with an open coal fire and most people smoked too. We didn’t realise then the effect it may have on our children.
New Years Eve 1969 arrived.  Mick had gone to work at Cadby Hall in London and I had lit the fire in the front room. My parents by then had given us their old red moquette three piece suite, which we had installed in our dining/living room with the large bay window and amazing views over the whole of Chatham and the river Medway.  We had put the two fireside chairs, a sideboard, a large gate leg table, and a small oak bookcase which had been left by the home’s previous owners in the front room. I had bought an off cut piece of carpet so, along with an old fashioned standard lamp, the room looked really quite cosy when the fire was lit. Aunty Lil had offered to come and babysit for us, so that Mick and I could celebrate the New Year with our friends. I think somehow I had learned from my father, and knew you could move the television into a different room if you could push the arial cable through the adjoining wall! It was cold and it had started snowing.  Aunty Lil and I were waiting for Mick to get home from work. It got well past his normal home time and I had started to get worried. Obviously without telephones we just had to wait. Eventually we heard Mick’s key in the front door.  He was damp and freezing cold. The trains had all been running late because of the weather and he didn’t feel very well. Obviously we didn’t celebrate the New Year as we had planned. Mick went to bed with a hot water bottle and I sat with Aunty Lil and we watched the New Year arrive courtesy of Andy Stewart on the television. The next morning we realised Mick had caught the flu which was doing its annual round that winter. 




Winter started to turn into spring and I decided it was time to get ready for the new baby who was due in May. I had read, and I read a lot of childcare books then especially Dr Spock which my friend Carol had given me, that when a new baby arrives the other children should already be in their own beds so they didn’t feel they had been ousted by the new baby. We were very lucky as our very good neighbours, Win and Frank, offered us bunk beds which their children had grown out of. That Easter we gratefully accepted the offer of the beds and put one up for Robert in his own bedroom to get used to sleeping in so we could use the cot for the new baby. The evenings were gradually getting lighter by then and when Robert was put to bed it was still light.  He thought it was great fun because he could get himself out bed!  We didn’t have stair gates then, in fact I didn’t know anyone who had one. Every evening, after our goodnight ritual, I used to sit on the top stair waiting for this little person to appear! And every time I would just calmly tell him to go back to bed, tuck him in and say goodnight.  Actually, that worked quite quickly and within a couple of weeks Robert had stopped getting up. Another hurdle over!
Another thing I became concerned about that spring was the steep flight of concrete steps down to the garden. I realised how dangerous they were particularly for small children, that they would not easily be able to go out to play, and certainly not without supervision. We decided, however wonderful the view was, that we would have to move house and immediately started looking. That Easter, we put our home with an estate agent, and as we did not have a car we walked everywhere with the pram, looking. There were a number of agents on the Top Road (actually that was the main A2, and was called Watling Street), it is an original Roman Road. It is also where the aforementioned Top Rank ten pin bowling alley was, where I spent so much time just a few years previously! We called in to one of the agents and asked for details of any houses that may be suitable. We were given details of one on Second Avenue in Gillingham and decided that it was not too far to go and have a look. I was of course seven months pregnant by then, but off we walked to have a look pushing Robert in his pram.  Few people had telephones then, so instead of ringing first you knocked at the door clutching the agents details. It was a Saturday and the owners were busy entertaining their family but they still very kindly let us in and showed us round. 
I remember going into the kitchen where two ladies were busy preparing food on the worktop, feeling a little embarrassed and apologising for disturbing them. I took up rather a lot of room with my baby bump and carrying an infant too!
We liked the fact that the kitchen was very modern with sixties, pale blue and white formica cupboards and top. The wall cupboards had sliding glass doors. It had a new white sink and drainer plus a more modern gas, multi-point water heater and gas stove. The dining room had French windows which opened on to a safe, enclosed back garden and both the dining room and living room had gas fires.  The living room had a modern one set in a wooden surround fire place.  There was nice, small front garden and it was end of Terrace. We decided to contact the agent , using the public telephone, and offer £3,800. We had been offered £3,750 for our home in Longhill Avenue. We were able to simply transfer our mortgage to the new property, which was something you were able to do once the new house had been surveyed.  It was all very different then. There was only one problem, my baby was due in five weeks!




When I discovered I was pregnant the second time, the same midwife who had told me off because the bedroom was too cold, told me crossly I should not have got pregnant again within the year of having a caesarian. Well, that information was a bit late coming, and I did wonder if I was suddenly going to ‘unzip’!  Fortunately not, but I did spend a week in hospital at the beginning of my ninth month going into a false labour, and apparently I needed to be watched!  My Mum took Robert home to their house, but she took him to my friend Carol to look after during the day as she was working as a district nurse.  Whereas Robert was a week early, Richard decided to be a week late. They let me come home from hospital because nothing was ‘happening’! I think I came home on a Thursday which was certainly good for my parents, as they had booked to go on holiday!  Dear Aunty Lil had agreed to come and live in our home and look after Robert and Mick when I went into hospital to have Richard. I was not allowed to chose a home birth this time.  On the Saturday I decided to walk, pushing the pram, down the very steep Institute Hill into Chatham High Street. I bought Mick a present of the 45 “Wandering Star” by Lee Marvin which had been very popular that spring. The England Football World Cup Squad were number one in the Hit Parade with Back Home, as the opening game of the World Cup was due to kick off in Mexico on Sunday 31st May. England had won the world cup in the previous tournament in 1966 and so were defending champions. As I walked home, pushing the pram back up the steep hill I thought I was feeling a bit under the weather. I gave Robert his tea and got him ready for bed then told Mick I thought I would go to bed early as I didn’t feel great.  As it was a Saturday night Mick walked down to meet his mother at the Elephant and Castle, on the corner of Luton Road and Castle Road, where she worked part-time as a barmaid. He wasn’t gone long, but when he came home I said he better go and ring for an ambulance as I was sure, this time, that I was having contractions. The ambulance arrived before midnight and off I went again, Mick stayed to look after Robert and arrange for Aunty Lil to come the following day.  He said he would then come to the hospital at visiting time. That was the way it was then, even husbands were not allowed to stay with their wives, they could only come at the set visiting times. I think because we all knew that was the way it was, we didn’t question it and felt very well looked after by the hospital.
On Sunday 31st May 1970 Mick came to visit me and I was by then in the labour ward. It was 3pm and the first match of the world cup was due to kick off at 3.30 pm UK time. As the defending champions England were playing the first game. It was very unusual then, but the midwives were having their tea break at visiting time so they left Mick in the labour ward alone with me. Before they left they handed him a buzzer and said ‘if she wants to push, press this’! Poor Mick, all I can remember him saying to me was “Shall I press this now?” repeatedly! Well eventually he did, and Richard David Ferguson arrived at 3.30 in the afternoon, just as the football started! Visiting time was over and Mick had disappeared as fast as his legs would carry him to his mother’s house to watch the football while I was stitched up and taken back to the main ward with my baby, this time, beside me in a crib! Mick came back to see me at visiting time in the evening and he peered in the crib at his new son. “What shall we call him?”, he said. Well he certainly wasn’t going to be called Helen, which was the name we had decided on, having chosen that when I was pregnant the first time! I remember we discussed several names but he didn’t like my suggestions, I know one of them was Alexander, but I can’t remember any others. When I said “Richard” Mick replied “Yes, that’s it!”, and so it was.  Nothing at all to do with the fact we already had a Robert.
Over the following ten days I enjoyed my baby and a good night’s sleep when the cribs were all taken to the nursery. I enjoyed the company of the other young mothers too and, because the weather was lovely, we would go out in our nighties to sit on the hospital lawn and have our cigarettes!  One afternoon Mick brought Robert to see us through the ward window, I felt homesick then, and desperate to get home to my family. Just as my ten days were up, my parents returned from holiday. This time just my mum came to collect me and take Richard and I home.  Aunty Lil had the carrycot ready in the front room, and dear little Robert was waiting there too. Mick and Aunty Lil had looked after him so well and had been telling him where Mummy was and that he was going to get a new baby brother. After Richard had been placed in the carrycot, Aunty Lil asked 17 month old Robert what was in the cot.  Robert peered inside then looked up at me and said “baby”! I was so thrilled! He had learned so many new words in the couple of weeks I had been away. This time, looking after a baby was a doddle for me and I felt very fit and healthy too. Of course the sun was shining and the house was warm.
I was very busy for the next few weeks with a new baby and a very young toddler.  I also was negotiating with our solicitor and organising moving house. Looking back, I’m not really sure how I did it.  There were no computers and we did not have a phone or a car.  Everything had to be written longhand too. Washing baby nappies, shopping, cooking and housework all had to be done. I was only twenty three years old.
In fact there was a moment when I did have quite an important memory lapse. One sunny afternoon about four weeks after giving birth, I decided to take my new baby for a walk in the beautiful Silver Cross pram so I could look in the shop windows in Chatham High Street. Window shopping was quite a common pastime then, I guess it has now been replaced by internet shopping and gazing at items on your mobile phone! I had a toddler seat across the top of the pram and carefully strapped eighteen month old Robert into the seat. I walked down the very steep Institute Hill holding on tight to the pram handle so the pram didn’t run away from me. I only weighed seven and a half stone then too! I walked very proudly along the High Street wearing my bib top hot pants which were just about still fashionable and stopped to look at the clothes in Dorothy Perkins shop window. I was such a small size then I could often find bargains and the summer sale had just begun. I parked the pram outside of the shop and applied the brake. I carefully lifted Robert from the seat and connected his reins to the harness which had held him safely on the pram seat. 
Everyone parked their prams outside of shops in those days, leaving the babies sound asleep. We knew if the baby became disturbed someone would run in to the shop asking whose baby was crying, rather like the evening baby patrol at the holiday camp. Often, in the daytime, prams were left on the street in front of your home, for the baby to sleep outside in the fresh air. I suppose it depended on where you lived how “fresh” the air was but it was certainly considered a very good thing for babies all year round!  Off I went into the shop holding Robert’s reins and searched through the tantalising racks looking for a bargain I could afford. I actually can’t remember if I did find my bargain, what I do remember was I had walked with Robert quite a distance from the shop when I remembered I had left my new baby behind in his pram! I picked Robert up and, with my heart in my mouth, made a mad dash back to the shop. The pram was still there and baby Richard was still asleep. Oh my goodness, what a relief! In future years I did lose Richard quite a few times as the eyes in the back of my head hadn’t obviously developed enough to cope with my escapee son!




On July 10th 1970 we moved to our new home, 110, Second Avenue, Gillingham Kent. We had managed to book a local removal van but Mick and I pushed our pram and walked to our new home. Fortunately it was a nice day but cloudy, with rain threatening. I had two little ones still wearing nappies then, so had packed spare nappies, milk, and food for the children in the shopping basket under the pram. The van was waiting outside of the front door for us and Mick and I had called at the estate agent on our way there to collect the keys. I assume they must have contacted the solicitor to check the sale had completed but it wasn’t something I really knew anything about then. I did know that the new mortgage was agreed!
The delivery men placed our furniture in the rooms we requested. We still didn’t own any new furniture apart from our bed and wardrobe which had been a wedding present. My parents had given us their old fridge and washing machine and their red moquette two seater settee and matching armchairs. We also had the two bunk beds that Win and Frank, our previous neighbours, had given us and of course, the baby cot and wooden high chair.  Every room was furnished and I was thrilled with my modern sixties kitchen. There was only one problem. The services had not yet been switched on by the Gas Board or South Eastern Electricity Board. All our public services then were nationalised and owned by the government. I was quite frantic as I needed to boil water for Richard’s lunch time bottle and we did not own an electric kettle or indeed any other ‘modern’ electric kitchen gadgets! We boiled the kettle on the top of the gas stove and made toast under the grill. In fact we carried on doing this for quite a few more years.  I had to make phone calls to get both services connected so I took my purse, put the children back in the pram and walked to the public phone box in Sturdee Avenue. The Electricity Board were really helpful and connected the power almost immediately.  Sadly the gas was another tale. They said the couldn’t connect the gas until someone came out to inspect the connection. I was frantic and told them I had two babies under two years of age that had to be fed and that meant having boiling water. I said I would hold them responsible if anything happened to either of my children as they hadn’t connected the gas supply as arranged. Eventually they did realise we had a problem and sent someone to our home to turn the gas on at the meter, which they had disconnected. The next day we began our life in our new home.
I loved being able to push the pram to Gillingham Park where we spent many sunny afternoons and Mick’s aunty and uncle, Rene and Jack lived close by. Aunty Rene was invariably home in the afternoon so I would call in to see her and have a cup of tea. Uncle Jack worked in Chatham Dockyard and finished work at four o’clock. If we were still there he always played with the children, tickling them and asking if they had ‘ants in their pants’ when they wiggled and squealed! I pushed the pram to Gillingham High Street occasionally to collect groceries from the Co-op, although there was a little Co-op in Sturdee Avenue and a small parade of shops where there was a butcher, a greengrocer and a post office. After Richard was born I was able to claim family allowance, which was only paid for the second and subsequent children born in the family, at a rate of eight shillings a week. I received my allowance book in the post and I had to take it to the post office each week to collect my allowance over the counter. This meant a lot to me. I was also told by the children’s health clinic that I might be eligible for free milk tokens for my children, which I applied for and received. Ninety-nine percent of people still had their milk delivered daily by their local milkman. He called weekly for his money and I was able to use the tokens towards my weekly bill.
I took the children to the clinic once a month, I always walked everywhere first with my pram and, as Richard got older, in the double pushchair where they sat one behind the other.  I had the children weighed and got my free orange juice.  The children also had their vaccinations at the appropriate time although not many were available then.  They both had their polio vaccination but the MMR had only become available in 1971 and there was a lot of mixed advice about it. Both my children suffered with eczema and it was decided they should not have this new vaccine, so they were given separate shots.




For our family holiday in 1970 we went to stay in my parents caravan between Hythe and Dymchurch. Aunty Lil came with us too and we all crammed into my mother’s Austin Morris Maxi, including the carrycot and push chair! We just held the babies on our laps, there were no seat belts or child seats then. They were both still wearing nappies too, that had to be washed, so I really can’t imagine how we all fitted in the car with our luggage and baby provisions but we did! During the week we spent there Robert enjoyed spending a lot of time playing in the sandpit which was only a few metres from our caravan door, with one of the adults helping fill his bucket with sand.  Robert always liked digging and had already spent weeks making mud pies in our back garden! Richard, of course was only a few months old but, unlike his brother who had colic for the first six months of his life following his traumatic entry into the world, Richard always slept well.  In fact, so well that he slept right through the night when he was only six weeks old and still in the crib in our bedroom.  I remember waking up before him one morning and panicking that he might be dead, leaping out of bed and rushing to look at him still sound asleep. I was astounded and so relieved!
In the caravan Robert slept on the bottom bunk and four-month-old Richard in his carrycot on the top bunk. We didn’t really do much that week as we had the seaside on the opposite side of the road to the small caravan park, and the site owners ran a well-stocked small shop. Aunty Lil did babysit for us on a couple of evenings and Mick and I walked to the local pub. One day, Aunty Dot and Uncle Charlie brought their daughters, Susan and Linda, to visit us. Dot stayed at the caravan with her sister Aunty Lil and baby Richard. Uncle Charlie took his two daughters and Mick and I with Robert to Folkestone where we went to the small funfair near the harbour.  Robert enjoyed a ride on the merry go round and we all enjoyed the old penny slot machines. We bought a lot of lovely fresh fish and chips and took them back to the caravan with us.
As autumn and then winter came we were all so much more comfortable in our new home which had gas fires in both reception rooms. The one in the dining room was several years old and still set in the original tiled fireplace. I had a big nursery guard around the fire which, all winter, was draped in Terry towelling nappies drying. The side board was in the alcove of the fire and that is where the children’s toys were stored, in fact, to this day, children’s toys for the grandchildren are still stored in that same sideboard. It was left in the first home we bought in Longhill Avenue, and is now an antique. I used to fill the baby bath and put it in the dining room to bathe baby Richard, it was so much warmer than upstairs, where there was no heating. One morning as I was bathing Richard, Robert was climbing up to stand on top of the sideboard. I told him to get down which he did, and put his foot inside a large toy train which then moved and tipped Robert against the wooden edge of the French windows. I had my hands full of a baby wrapped in a bath towel as I saw what was about to happen, of course all within a split second. I put Richard, still wrapped in his towel, on the armchair and rescued Robert who, when he stood up screaming, had blood pouring from his head and down his jumper. I put a tea towel around his head and told him to sit still as I grabbed the baby and rushed to our neighbours house for help. They were another lovely retired couple, and the husband was a first aid volunteer for the St John Ambulance. He came home with me, looked at Robert, and said we should go to the hospital as he would need stitches. I quickly got Richard’s nappy and clothes on and left him in the care of my neighbour while her husband drove us to the A&E department. Robert was duly stitched up and we returned home. Neighbours were so very important in those days, and I certainly don’t know how any of us would have coped in an emergency without each other.
The fashions changed again that autumn and the midi dress became fashionable. I had found one in a sale and was proud of my new frock, except I had nowhere to wear it. A few weeks before Christmas I had a lovely surprise, my friend Penny Gough from art college had succeeded in getting her art diploma and was working as a makeup artist for BBC Television. She wrote to me and said she was working on the Cliff Richard TV show and that she could get us free tickets if we would like to go! Well, Mick was never a Cliff fan but of course we would go, and I had somewhere to wear my new dress! Dear Aunty Lil once again came to babysit for us and we caught the train to Broadcasting House in Alexander Palace where the show was being recorded. It was the one and only time I have ever been part of a recorded programme and it did take ages, but was very exciting! First we all received our instructions from the set manager, then we were ‘warmed up’ by a comedian and given instructions when to clap or cheer. Eventually, we did get to be part of the whole show recording. Cliff talked to the audience and talked to his guests, I can’t remember who they were! The Shadows also performed their own 1963 hit, Dance On. What an exciting day!




Christmas Eve arrived and I was very excited looking forward to our first Christmas with all four of us together in our new home, Richard’s first Christmas. I had bought a good off-cut length of mustard coloured carpet to put down in the front room, as there were only bare boards when we moved in. Mick had painted the boards before we put the carpet down and with Mum and Dad’s old suite sitting on the carpet the room looked snug and warm with the modern gas fire alight. We bought a small, real Christmas tree and put it in the middle of the bay window. Strung with fairy lights from Woolworth’s, it looked cheerful and welcoming. The children were in bed and I wrapped all the presents, putting them under the tree. The children had also received presents from the family, which I had hidden, so I put those under tree too. Mick and I then used a pair of his black and white football socks to fill with little presents from Father Christmas and Mick, checking they were both fast asleep, but one in Richard’s cot and one on the bottom of Robert’s bed before we went to bed.
The next morning Robert came excitedly into our bedroom shouting ‘Look, look!, he’s been!’ I went downstairs and got us all a drink, and a bottle for Richard, before lifting him out of his cot and bringing him into the bedroom with us. I don’t think Richard had any idea at all what was happening, but he loved the crinkly paper, probably more than the contents, and Robert was beside himself with excitement!
Somehow we all got dressed and made our way downstairs to the dining room ,which was also the playroom, and had our breakfast.  Mick and I had half a grapefruit each, that was becoming our Christmas morning ritual, followed by toast and tea. 
After breakfast we flung open the front room door and lo and behold, Father Christmas had left presents under the tree.  Mick sat in his chair, he had a designated chair all his life, beside the fireplace, opposite the television, and lit a cigarette whilst I tried to sort out the presents from under the tree.  This proved very difficult because, although Richard was just less than seven months old, he had just learned how to drag himself across the floor rather like a caterpillar! I would put him on the floor at the furthest point from the tree and he would make a beeline for it attracted by the coloured paper he knew he liked!  Robert had a little blue and white tricycle as his main present, Richard had a teddy on wheels that you pushed, and my Mum and Dad came for afternoon tea. Of course on Boxing Day we all walked with the pram to Nanny Vere’s house where we had the traditional afternoon Christmas party.  Mick and I had to push the pram all the way up Chatham Hill to get home again, quite a long way!




The first major event of 1971 was my sister Patsy’s marriage to Roger Turner which was taking place at the end of February at St Stephen’s Church on the Maidstone Road in Chatham.  I was to be a Matron of Honour and cousin Julie who had been my bridesmaid in 1967 was to be her other bridesmaid too. Patsy had chosen a beautiful soft white velvet for her dress and dark green velvet for the bridesmaids. The pattern was simple and very similar to my dress, I guess because fashions had not changed very much in three years. Patsy also wore our mothers beautiful waxed flower tiara with her full length veil.
I prepared at our home in Gillingham and Uncle Charlie brought Aunty Dot, their two daughters Susan and Linda, and Aunty Lil to our house to babysit our two, very young sons. Mick and I were collected by minicab and taken to the church to wait for the bride to arrive.  It was a bright cold February day. The service went off without a hitch and photos were duly taken. The reception was held in the assembly room above the Ritz cinema in Chatham and we were all served with a roast lunch.  When Patsy and Roger left for their honeymoon Mick and I were given a lift back to our home in Gillingham by my mother. I was home in time to put the children to bed!
In my memory 1971 was a peculiar, grey year. We really were seriously short of money, having only one income, and there was very little left once Mick had paid for his season ticket to London to work and we had made our mortgage payment. I started to think about what future career I might have and I did really still want to be a teacher. To makes ends meet I became a Vanda sales person, which was really the same as an Avon lady, and walked door to door to leave my brochures, or to call back hoping for an order. I also had a Marshall Ward shopping catalogue which was really popular then. I guess they were the equivalent of internet shopping now. If you received orders from friends or family on your catalogue, you also received money off any of your own purchases. The goods could be paid for in weekly payments, so every week I had to take my paying in slip and the money to the Post Office. That was the way most of us bought our Christmas and birthday presents, it was extremely seldom that I treated us to new clothes.
We had decided to try to improve and decorate our home. When I went to the High Street I would look in the bins outside of the DIY shops at the leftover rolls of wallpaper.  My father had shown me years before how to count how many rolls of paper you needed dependant on the number of drops.  I had also watched him paste and hang wallpaper in my childhood home, and how to make and use a plumb line to ensure the paper was hanging straight. I had no idea that all these skills I was learning would stand me in good stead for our own business very many years later!  It was fashionable to have one wall in the room a different colour or pattern to the remaining walls.  We decided to decorate our front room where we sat in the evening and watched the television. We especially liked a series called Family at War, and Coronation Street was also only a few years old! All the TVs were only in black and white then. I bought a gold patterned wall paper for the chimney breast wall, and a very tiny patterned paper in the same colours for the remaining three walls. Mum and Dad saw we were trying to improve our home and had obviously noticed our very out of date bathroom when they had visited. It still had the original pull chain high cistern, as well as a cracked white sink. They knew our financial situation and very kindly offered to employ a plumber to put in a new white bathroom suite for us. Mick got very busy with his paint brush and we bought stick on blue plastic tiles to cover the old white tiles in the bathroom. I bought another dark blue carpet remnant for the floor in the bathroom and gold fibreglass material from the market to make new curtains for the front room. We were thrilled by our new look and we bought a jute carpet for our dining room which was also the play room, from my catalogue.



Time for dreams




That spring I decided to follow up on becoming a teacher, and applied to Sittingbourne Teacher Training College. What a silly dream that was when I did not have any child care, I just thought I would find some if I was accepted. I don’t know how I thought it would be paid for, I hadn’t made any enquiries! I did know that college courses were still free. I was duly invited for an interview as I did have sufficient, relevant exam passes from school.  My mother took me in the car with the children for my interview and she kept them entertained in the car whilst I was in the college.  The interview panel consisted of three people who were very kind. They did ask if I had childcare organised and I tried to reassure them it wouldn’t be a problem. I was ever an optimist!  A few weeks later I received a very nice letter from the college saying they would be happy to offer me a training place once both my children had started school. The grey world returned.
Unbeknown to me, my parents had also decided, after my sister left home, that they would redecorate and make some changes in their home in Dargets Road. They had a large lounge which they decided to decorate, and to purchase a new fitted carpet. We became the lucky recipients of their old carpet which was more than large enough to fit our newly decorated front room.  My father laid the carpet for us and also put two shelves in the wall beside the chimney breast. Mick won a gift token from work to use in Curry’s, and his brother Steve took him in his car to purchase a new record player and speakers replacing our faithful old Dansette record player which Mick had owned forever (well probably since 1962!)  Our new record player was installed on our new shelves with some LPs placed alongside it. The 45s were still kept in Mick’s well-loved travelling box which had been taken to various parties over the years. I believe this might now still be in Richard’s possession, along with the 45s! I had also been given some money for my birthday earlier in the year, and I spent that on Plumbs green stretch covers for the old moquette suite. I was so thrilled with the changes, we now had a ‘posh’ front room to sit in!
On one of my visits to Aunty Rene for an afternoon cup of tea I asked her about nurseries and she told me there was one in a neighbouring road.  The next day I walked there with both of the children to have a look, Robert was two and a half. I thought it looked quite nice, although I only had my own memory of nursery to guide me. I arranged for Robert to go two afternoons a week. What a disaster, I knew by the third day it was not for him.  When I went to collect him, there was a very distraught little boy with a snotty nose and wet trousers. I said no thank you he would not be coming again. On my next visit to Aunty Rene I told her what had happened and she told me that her daughter-in-law, Elizabeth, had taken her daughter to a private playgroup that someone ran in their own home. I made enquiries about it at the children’s clinic and was told you could run a group in your own home but it had to be registered by the Health Visitor.  There had been a lot in the press about illegal nurseries which people were running in their own homes, just for money, and how the children were at risk and not being cared for.  I asked if the Health Visitor could come and visit my home, and my life changed forever.
On a sunny summer day the Health Visitor arrived. We did have a safe home with an enclosed safe rear garden, an indoor toilet with a wash-hand basin, and suitable fireguards. I had to put a stair gate in the kitchen doorway to keep the children out  of there. I was told that I had enough floor space and one toilet for ten children under the age of five, and that it had to include any of my own. I could also only have five under five to one adult, and I could not care for under two year olds.  She also told me about the Pre-school Playgroups Association and the fact that there was a National Foundation Course which was finally approved in 1975.  It ran for one year, one evening a week, at our local adult education centre. She recommended I made enquiries and attended it when it started that autumn. I was so excited, the grey cloud lifted and I knew here was something I really wanted to do, and I could bring in a regular small income as well as know my children were still happy at home with me!  Joy!
There was just one more thing to do, work out how to earn some more money so that we were no longer literally managing hand to mouth. I could make a chicken last at least three days; roast on Sunday, cold or curry (made with available curry powder) on Monday and chicken broth I made in the pressure cooker on Tuesday. I had never bought baby food, the boys ate the same meal as us but it had been finely minced in the mouli mixer. We ate a lot of stewed fruit in season and I used the milk I got with tokens to make desserts; rice, sago or tapioca puddings, Angel delight as a Sunday treat, lots of custard and home made cakes, buns and scones. I used to listen to Jimmy Young on his morning radio show, and every day the announcer would ask “ What’s the recipe today Jim?” I’d grab my paper and pen to make a note of anything that I thought was suitable for me to make! I learned to make fishcakes with a large (cheap) tin of pilchards mixed with boiled mashed potatoes and shaped and fried in the frying pan served with a tin a baked beans. And I still have one of Jim’s recipes for banana cake, for using up overripe bananas!




Richard was fifteen months old, and one morning I went into his bedroom to find him hanging out of his cot by his arms. I decided it was time to use the second of the bunk beds we had been given, and that it would be a good idea to put both the boys in the same bedroom. This meant we then had an empty, small but comfortable, single bedroom. There was only one problem; at the age of three Robert decided that he could fly! He spent almost every day at home with an old curtain tied around his neck as a cape and would ‘fly’ off chairs, the sofa, and more!  I was seriously worried about the bedroom window which was a casement window in a wooden frame (they all were then). We solved that problem by purchasing a sunken bolt which could only be opened by an adult!
Because we then had a spare room, I thought that if we could let the room, it would solve all our financial problems. Problems which had actually got worse, as we had succumbed to doorstep selling and agreed to having electric central heating installed. We had to also pay for it monthly.  Electric fan heaters had been installed in the three bedrooms, the two living rooms and the hall, which connected to a timer switch under the stairs. We actually only ever used them manually to heat a room and then switch them off.  We couldn’t really afford the electricity either but it was nice to sometimes put them on first thing in the morning to heat the bedroom. I would press the switch when I got up to make everyone a drink.  I remember, after we had agreed to them, reading the small print and being terrified at what we had agreed on. I walked to the phone box to ring and say we couldn’t afford it, and that I wanted to cancel the order. We couldn’t, we had signed the agreement and there was no legal requirement for a cooling off period then. We had to wait until the next century for that! We had to bite the bullet, hence the need for a lodger.  I contacted the education department at Gillingham Borough Council and asked if they had any requests from trainee teachers for local accommodation. They took all our details and we were put on their register. A couple of weeks later I received a letter saying that a probationary teacher. Susan Hodgkinson, was looking for digs, as she had accepted a trainee placement at Barnsole Road school.  She lived with her parents in Canterbury and would like to lodge with us for four nights a week. I replied saying she was welcome to visit. She did, she was lovely, and she moved in for the September term. I started my playgroup course and Sue became my sounding board and a great friend allowing me to bounce ideas off her for my playgroup. Our financial situation improved tremendously.
Also in September, I started my playgroup three mornings a week initially from 9.0am to midday. Initially, I had three children plus my two, but I had put notices in local doors where I knew there were children and soon had a small waiting list. Of course Richard was only eighteen months old, so it was particularly hard work. Fortunately he still had a day time sleep so I would put him down about 11.30 am which gave us a quiet moment to finish with our singing circle, hand the children back to their mothers, tidy up, and prepare some lunch for the three of us before Richard woke up again. Children’s TV programmes started at 1.45 pm everyday so the three of us would have a bit of quiet time in the front room after our lunch (sometimes I had forty winks too!). In the afternoon I invariably still walked out with the pushchair, often to the shops to buy fresh meat and vegetables for our dinner, sometimes that also included a cup of tea with Aunty Rene, and occasionally I took the children to Gillingham Park. There was a high slide in the park and the play equipment in those days was placed on a plain tarmac surface, there was no safety cage at the top of the slide either. One summer, when Richard had just turned two and Robert was three and a half, I turned to see Richard steadily climbing the ladder of the slide. I helplessly stood at one side of the ladder as he climbed to the top, sat on the slide and successfully slid to the bottom where I was frantically waiting. There continued to be quite a few hair-raising moments throughout their childhood!
On an occasional Thursday my mum would manage to finish her patient round early and would arrive in her car, in her district nurse uniform. We were always very pleased to see her. She would take us into Gillingham and park the car near the children’s health clinic and bank. Often mum would take the two little ones to the bridge that crossed over the railway line beside the car park, and they would wave to the trains entering Gillingham station while I made a quick visit to the bank to withdraw money for groceries and other expenses.  That was the only way to get cash then, there were no ATMs and we all used cash to pay for everything.
Having started my playgroup I also started to attend the Playgroup Foundation Course one evening a week. The tutor’s name was Molly and she lived in a converted Oast house in one of the villages outside of Maidstone. It was a sixty hour course and ran for thirty evenings over three terms. The content of the course covered child development from two to five, different activities to use at playgroup and, of course, the business and legal aspects of running a group. I loved everything about it! I learned so much about how to offer the different activities; painting, junk play (which was sticking cardboard boxes, toilet rolls etc together  and painting them), sand and water play, playing with real clay, all the different tabletop activities and creating a book corner. We sang songs and made musical instruments too. I loved the creativity and understanding how children learned, that play was all about learning, they were one and the same thing. We very occasionally would watch a film or have a guest speaker to give us more understanding of children with different needs. In addition we also learned about the importance of the local community, how to organise community playgroups and offer support and help between the adults too. On the business side we learned about the Pre-School Playgroup Association which had started in 1961 and was set up by Belle Tutaev. It was an exciting time and ten years later, there were branches all over the country. Each county also had a county committee. I joined the Gillingham Branch of the association and started going to their meetings too.
I was so motivated by the course that I decided by 1972 to turn my playgroup into a community group. I was a bit concerned since it was in our home, but Molly our tutor said communities didn’t just happen in villages and as long as there were people, you could form a community, so I did! I had got to know quite a few local mothers once I started my group and I invited them all for a coffee evening, to tell them about my idea and ask what they thought.  I was very aware that they were paying me to provide for their children so I wondered what they thought about being involved. I needn’t have worried, they were all wonderful and keen to know how they could help.  We talked about making equipment and we made a bookcase using an old wooden folding clothes horse. We backed it with wood and used net curtain wires to hold the books, facing out to the children. It could then be folded to put away.  We made a wooden easel on an A frame so two children could use it at the same time with the paper held on by clothes pegs! In 1972, my best friend from school Linda Carr (by then Corley) came to help me.  She lived quite nearby and had continued to work for Barclays Bank.  She was desperate to have children, and her doctor had advised her to leave work as it was too stressful. With Linda helping me, we could then have ten children a day, four days week (including my two). Our little community group decided we would fundraise so we could buy more equipment, again, everyone was so kind and helpful. I had started keeping the playgroup equipment in the cupboard under the stairs, as I thought my children should play with their own toys and not the playgroup ones. With our fundraising money we did buy a climbing frame that my children did benefit from as it was permanently in our back garden! They liked to climb up it and, to my intense embarrassment, call over the large green hedge to our next door neighbour, ‘Hello Mrs Buttenshaw; have you got any sweets Mrs Buttenshaw?” Just once, in the front garden she had given them sweets, a treat they clearly never forgot! We also purchased a secondhand swing.
Our fundraising consisted of monthly coffee evenings and a raffle which we took it in turns to hold. Once we even had a huge successful Jumble Sale in the Foresters Hall on Sturdee Avenue. Soon after the jumble sale Linda discovered she was pregnant. I guess there must have been a connection somewhere, and she was delighted.




My course finished in June 1972 and I had managed to save a lot of my family allowance in my Post Office savings. I had saved enough for us to go on a real holiday! Before the school holidays started we went by train from Paddington Station to a caravan site near Looe in Cornwall. It was a long way and I’m not really sure how we made it without our own transport. Aunty Lil came with us and Mick’s young cousin from Sheerness, Linda. It was hard work with two young children but we really had a wonderful time.  One day we went to Talland Bay which was still a very quiet place.  I wanted to go and see it as I had stayed there in a cottage with my parents and sister when I was about twelve years old. It hadn’t changed a bit. The children played with their buckets and spades on the beach and then we went to the tea shop for a drink and cake.  The tea shop was on the ground floor of a very old cottage.  As the lady served us tea she turned and shouted up the stairs, to a person we could not see, “Don’t forget to bring the books down Raymond!” .Immediately three year old Robert piped up ‘Bring the books down Raymond!” We all looked at him quite shocked then burst into laughter. That phrase remained in our family forever!
Because I had started the playgroup we decided we really needed a telephone and we had one placed on the wall in the hall. I also decided, that summer, to carry on decorating and I went back to looking in the shop bins for wallpaper. I bought some fashionable rolls with large coloured circles on a white background for the ground floor hall wall and some Victoria Plum paint for the stair wall. I thought it might hide some of the finger marks! I also papered the chimney breast in the dining room/play room in paper that had tiny flowers on an orange background, and painted the other walls orange.
That autumn I received a phone call from Molly who was still tutoring the Playgroup Foundation Course.  She asked me, quite out of the blue, if I would be able to tutor two sessions for her on that year’s course.  One was to be on child development in painting and drawing  and one on junk play. I was astounded to be asked but also delighted. I decided I had better do some research and also look up the class notes I had taken the previous year. The dates were booked and I bought a secondhand children’s typewriter from a jumble sale! It was the best I could afford and although it was a very laborious process, I managed to type out a lesson plan. I found I enjoyed teaching adults very much, and I also enjoyed receiving a very nice payment for my work, the first proper pay I had, had for a few years. It was so exciting we went out and bought our first new furniture because, up until then, apart from our wedding present bedroom furniture, everything had been secondhand. We bought a very fashionable white, drop-leaf round table with four matching white ladder-back chairs with orange seat covers. It looked very nice in our newly decorated dining room.




In the spring of 1973 Mick and I were invited to our first ever dinner party.  Every time I see or hear of Mike Leigh’s play Abigail’s Party, especially the original with Alison Steadman, I think of that night! Mick had met a friend from school on the train coming home from work. He hadn’t seen him for a long time and they lived out at Park Wood, Rainham which was a desirable, modern area of new houses. They invited us to a dinner party. It was fashionable for the ladies to all wear long dresses in the evening then, especially the type favoured by Laura Ashley with small printed flowers (perhaps I should have worn my wallpaper?!). The Friday night before we were due to go on the Saturday, Mick said he was sure he had the flu’ so would I ring and tell them we wouldn’t be going. Although actually quite nervous, as I did not know them and I had never been to a dinner party before, I was very disappointed. I can’t remember the couple’s name, but I did ring and spoke to Mick’s friend’s wife. She had obviously been planning this to impress everyone and seemed genuinely disappointed. She said I must come on my own and proceeded to tell me what we were having.  Prawn cocktail, steak followed by black forest gateau,  the 1970s meal! Mick agreed I should go and his friend came to collect me. Well, the food was lovely, the house very modern with a through lounge-diner and black leather sofas.  We drank Black Tower wine, and I was brought home again, this was before any drink driving laws. A different world was unveiled to me, but to be honest it all felt rather contrived, and although I can still remember every detail I knew I was just there to be impressed. Maybe the hostess felt better impressing someone! Another life lesson learned by me. Actually, on reflection, I realised how happy I was with our own home, our family and the new, genuine, friends we had made.
During 1973 our little world continued to expand. We made very good friends with Christine and Wolf Babin and with Ann and Chris Goldthorpe.  We all had children of about the same age. Robert became very friendly with Wesley Goldthorpe and I’m really not sure how much mischief they got up to exploring the alleyways between our homes. Richard was good friends with Sasha Babin and they played together in each other’s gardens. Mick and I also made friends with Janet and Adrian Booker who lived opposite us in Second Avenue, they had a young daughter, Samantha who came to playgroup the last summer we were there. Occasionally Mick and I would go across the road to their house in the evening to play cards, we would take it in turns to cross to our house to make sure the children were still asleep and alright. I don’t think baby monitors had been invented then.
In late spring, Christine Babin told me about an advertisement she had found in the local paper, for families able to offer two week’s accommodation to French students who were coming from their schools to learn English that summer, offering a generous payment in return. Christine and I went to the advertised meeting. I knew we had room for the student to have their own room as we no longer had our lodger. That summer we had our first French student, the first of many! He was quite a young lad and I can’t remember his name. Every day they either attended lessons or were taken on outings. I do remember walking home from school, having collected Robert, to see socks and shoes balanced on his window sill in front of the open window. I wondered what on earth he was doing!
After Christmas we had looked at all the holiday advertisements in the Christmas edition of the Radio Times to see where we would like to spend our summer week. We chose to go to Thorness Bay holiday camp on the Isle of Wight. I’m not sure why but looking back, I think I was choosing places I had been to with my parents, although we never went to a holiday camp. That was something Mick and I thought would be fun, as we had stayed in them before we were married. Again I saved up my family allowance to pay for the holiday, and again we managed to get there using public transport.  I do remember we had one large suitcase which was very heavy and proved quite difficult for Mick to manage. We travelled by coach and over to the island on the Red Funnel ferry. I know we had a lovely holiday, we used to walk up the road from the holiday camp to the bus stop.  The bus stop was right beside a field of pigs and their styes. There was also a riding school and one afternoon the boys had their first riding lesson, Richard was only just three and Robert four.
Also in the summer of 1973 Mick decided he didn’t want to commute to London anymore, and looked in the paper for work nearer home.  He applied for a job as a work controller for Rank Xerox in Maidstone. It still meant he had to catch two buses, one from Gillingham to Military Road in Chatham and then one from there to Maidstone, but it was a much better job with prospects as well as being nearer  to home and the travel less expensive. He was successful and liked the work and the company. He got on very well with the regional sales managers who relied on Mick to make sure the customers orders were followed through efficiently and the photocopiers installed in a timely manner.
I received another call from Molly who told me she was giving up tutoring the course and asked me if I could take it over from her. We had meetings and discussed the course syllabus and she handed over her list of connections telling me how to order films and information. Life became incredibly busy and exciting.
That summer, just before Robert started school, we had decided to start a new playgroup in a church hall. I’m not sure why we choose Twydall Baptist church on Twydall Green, although I did know the area well as that is where my Granny had lived. I also knew that all the halls local to me already had users and Twydall was on a direct bus route from our home. During May and June, together with lovely willing helpers both from my group at home and also from the local Pre-School Playgroup Association, we canvassed the local parents in that area. We knocked on doors of houses that had visible signs of children such as toys in the garden or washing on lines. We set up a meeting in the church hall to see if there would be enough interest to start a community playgroup, with a committee who would then also employ the staff, including me as Playgroup Supervisor. Several meetings and fundraisers later, Twydall Baptist Playgroup was ready to open its doors to the first families. We purchased second hand infant school tables and chairs from the local education department, we bought books for a book corner and made a home corner from a three sided, covered, old clothes horse. I gave the group my blue wooden dolls cot together with my precious Janet doll and it was duly installed in the home corner together with a small table, a tea set, and a box of dressing up clothes. We had a painting easel and a baby bath on a stand full of sand, lots of jigsaws, tabletop toys, a box of mainly home made musical instruments and a junk play table. The only large equipment we had was a wooden slide. I did not agree with large wheeled toys inside, especially where so much else was going on.  All the playgroup equipment from my home group was donated. We were ready to open our doors with a daily rota of mothers helpers, so everyone was involved and it became a real community group. There were no such thing as DBS police checks then.
Before the playgroup opened its doors, I took Robert to his first day at school.  He joined Barnsole Road Infant School, where his playgroup friends Wesley and Karin had already completed one term. The junior school was on the opposite side of the road and was a red brick building. The infants, starting the term in which they were five, were housed in a portakabin. Us baby boomers of the late 1940s were all having our children around the same time, ensuring there was another mini boom in baby numbers! Robert went off very happily although, until they actually start, it does take some adjusting to being away from home all day. When Richard and I got home I left him with paper and paints and went into the kitchen.  When I returned to him a short time later he showed me a picture he had painted of three people of different sizes. Richard looked up at me and said “I’ve painted Mummy and Daddy and me, and Robert’s gone to school”. We never really know what is going on in a child’s mind, and that was so revealing.  Robert seemed very happy with his first week. One day, in his second week, he did exactly what I had done, although for different reasons, he decided to leave school in the middle of the day and come home! When I answered the knock at the front door there was a little boy who just said “Hello, I’ve come home”! I did exactly what my own mother had done, and we all walked back again!  It didn’t happen again, I think he just wanted to confirm for himself exactly where he was going every day, and feel a bit in control of the situation.
I thought it would be a good idea for Richard to also join a different playgroup and there was one in Foresters Hall in Sturdee Avenue, which I had helped another lady to start.  It was almost beside the school Robert was attending, and fortunately beside the bus stop where I caught the bus to Twydall Green. Richard attended for the first term but was not very happy, so after we had left Robert at school each day Richard would also come on the bus with me to the Twydall Playgroup which ran from 9.30 am to midday four days a week. On the fifth day of the week I ran a playgroup foundation course day class at the adult education centre in Gillingham and they had a very nice creche on site.  Richard was happy there. It was only a short day 10am to 3pm with an hour’s lunch time that we could spend together.  I also still ran an evening class one evening a week, busy times!
As a result of all these changes things began to improve financially too, now I also had paid work.  Mick was very happy working for Rank Xerox and only having his bus fares to pay (although he had also started to think about other ways of getting to work).  
We had a very happy Christmas that year and Mick and I spent New Year’s Eve with Christine and Wolf Babin and Ann and Chris Goldthorpe. We all went to the Babin’s home as that was midway between the three families and we all lived in the same road. We spent the evening taking it in turns again to check the children we had left in our respective homes. Everything remained peaceful and we had a fun evening sharing the left over Christmas drinks. In fact, Christmas was usually the only time we had alcohol in our cupboards at home. Needless to say with the variety of the leftovers there were a few hangovers the next morning!







1974 started off quite a damp, miserable year which made travelling between schools, playgroups and evening classes hard work, but I was pleased to be busy and to have so many new friends. We also made the exciting decision to buy our first new three-piece suite. It was a fashionable bouclé material in square shades of brown and cream.  Brown was the colour of the 1970s! Mick chose his chair which was a swivel rocker and I can still see him now, sitting beside the fireplace, cigarette in hand. There was another single fireside chair and a three-seater sofa. We were feeling very content with our home.
That Easter, the weather had actually got colder and it snowed!  We took a train to Sheerness and saw all the new born Romney lambs shivering in the fields. Fortunately, it did start getting warmer as spring arrived and Mick decided it was time to make changes to travelling by bus to Maidstone each day. He still had his motorcycle licence and one day he came home the proud owner of a motorbike. From then on, he rode the bike to the office in Maidstone every day. Motorcycle helmets had become compulsory in the previous year.
I was a very active member of the Medway branch of the Pre-school Playgroups Association and became a volunteer support worker giving advice to people who wanted to start their own groups. This fitted in well with my job as an adult education tutor too. I got to know more people in the association at a very exciting time in its development nationally. 
In 1974 Harold Wilson became prime minister for the second time but, politically,  it became a very difficult time and Chatham Dockyard was due to close for all but nuclear submarine refits, with people having to chose between transferring to Plymouth or taking redundancy. Obviously that also affected house prices in the Medway Towns and there certainly was a feeling of change, and uncertainty about the future.  It was a very different time to ten years prior; I was twenty seven, married with a mortgage and two children instead of a fun loving seventeen year old. Britain was no longer the exciting centre of the worlds of music, fashion, or even car production. The Union Flags were no longer on the roofs of the Mini Coopers leaving the Longbridge plant.
As the playgroup was no longer in our home it once again became an ordinary family home and we really enjoyed living there among friends with similarly aged children in our local community. We spent sunny afternoons in each other’s gardens before going to collect the older children from school. Richard played with Sacha Babin and Christian Goldthorpe and, at weekends, we still regularly went to Sheerness on the train. We also went to Gillingham park or the Strand where there was a paddling pool for the children and they could hire small paddle-boats or ride on the swing-boats or the little model train. Occasionally, at the weekend, Pete or Pat Last would call for us and take us to their new Wimpey home on Beechings Way in Gillingham. They also had two boys, Jamie who was just a few months older than Richard, and Christian who was a little baby then. Many new houses were being built in the Medway Towns with the GLC (Greater London Council) offering mortgages to people who would resettle out of London and become commuters.
I remembered that in the 1950s, when my parents were looking for holiday accommodation, my father had written a letter to be posted in the local paper in the area they wished to visit, under “Accommodation Wanted’, and he had had quite a success with several letters in reply.  I thought I would give it a go and wrote to the paper for the Bournemouth area, where I knew we had holidayed a few times when I was little. I have no recollection of how I found out where to write to, as there was no Google then! I was highly likely to have contacted directory enquiries or asked in the local library.  With my letter written and posted enclosing a postal order (we did not have cheque books then) for the advertising cost, I eagerly awaited a response.  We received a few replies from people in the area but the one we were most interested in was from a couple in Croydon who owned a holiday chalet at Naish Farm holiday village in Highcliffe, Dorset near to Bournemouth. The chalet had two bedrooms, a bathroom and an open plan kitchen and living area. It was in a safe area of the park which also had an outdoor swimming pool, children’s play area and tennis courts. It was situated above a sandy beach within easy waking distance and, most importantly of all, it was at a price we could afford. I replied, once again enclosing a postal order which I obtained from the post office which also held my savings account holiday money, and the holiday was booked!
In the warm, late summer 1974, off we went on the coach with our children and luggage to Dorset.  I remember the coach driver, very kindly, stopped at the main gate for us. The site was owned by Hoburne Holidays and was clean and smart with very good facilities. It was all self catering, but there was also a small restaurant we could use and a family entertainment area with a small stage and a bar. We had a wonderful holiday and took the bus to Bournemouth as well as using the beach below the site.  One evening we took the bus into Bournemouth to take the children to the theatre to see the David Niven magic show. I think that was probably their first ever visit to a theatre. I remember Richard was so tired coming back to our holiday site he fell asleep on the bus and had to be carried to our chalet.  That was the first of many visits we made to Naish Farm. The last visit we made there was in 1987 when we were living in Sharnbrook. Robert drove himself in his own VW Beetle with his friend Phil and Richard came with us with his friend Tom. We were still booking the same chalet, Richard and Tom shared the second bedroom and Rob and Phil managed on the convertible settee in the living area. We took so many happy memories with us from those holidays.
When we returned from our holiday in 1974, Mick decided to change his motorbike for a three-wheeled Robin Reliant car which he could drive on his motorbike licence. He had never had any driving lessons so certainly wasn’t able to buy a four-wheeled car. I think the thought of riding his motorbike to work everyday during the winter was just too much. He also had to wear a suit, shirt and tie to work so it was hardly appropriate motorbike gear! We were really excited to have a car even if it did only have three wheels! It was comfortable enough to fit two small children in the back and had more than enough leg room in the front foot wells for Mick’s six foot three frame. We drove into Chatham to visit Nanny Vere and Aunty Lil and out to Walderslade to visit Nanny and Grandad. Obviously Mick also drove it to work all through that winter.  There were a lot of hiccups, as with any old car, But it was old and cheap enough for us to afford it with the sale of the motorbike. 
In September I continued as a supervisor of Twydall Baptist Church playgroup and also tutored two playgroup courses. One was an evening course and the other a day time one. That year we did not need to have a lodger, although I did continue to have French students to live with us on occasion.
Christmas was fun and we took our car to Nanny Vere’s on Boxing Day for the annual party. We shared New Year’s Eve with our neighbours with little idea of what the New Year would bring.




Up sticks!




It turned out to be an amazing year in 1975. Our friends Chris and Ann Goldthorpe decided to emigrate to New Zealand. I really have no idea why, and it wasn’t a country I really knew anything about other than it was half way around the world and a neighbour of Australia.  They sold their home in Second Avenue and flew off with both their young sons, Robert’s friend Wesley and young Christian. 
Also in January Mick came home from work with some very exciting and, for us, life-changing news. He had been offered an excellent promotion from a work controller with Rank Xerox to a Regional Sales Administration Manager with a salary of over £1000 a year. We had always said that if your salary was over £1000 a year, you would be rich!  However, the job was based in Crawley, West Sussex and they wanted him to start work almost immediately.  Mick accepted the new job and agreed he would start in four weeks time which was mid Feb. His new boss was a lovely older man who lived near Maidstone, and he said that if Mick could come to his house each day, he would take him to Crawley. One of the area sales managers,  John Liddle,  also lived in Maidstone and offered Mick a lift so it was agreed that was how Mick would get to work until we could sell our house and find one in Crawley.  It was many years before remote working meant you could work from home!  The search for a new home began.
Mick’s promotion had not gone unnoticed by colleagues in Maidstone, especially the area sales managers who relied on Mick’s work to get their salesforce’s photocopiers to their purchasers, or to send out a repair team. He was very well liked and trusted. The area sales managers for the south-east region planned a wonderful leaving presentation evening at The Inn on the Lake, just off the A2 north of Gravesend for him and his wife! It was a very exciting evening.  We all sat down in the restaurant the men in their suits and ties and the ladies in their best frocks. Inside of the specially written menu was a lovely tribute to Mick wishing him well in his new job. He was also given a present of black leather briefcase. It was a very special, wonderful evening. Mick was 31 and I was 27. I think that evening was another changing point in our lives.
 Mick brought the Crawley local paper home with him and we looked at property in the Property for Sale section. This gave us an idea of how much house prices were in that area. Sadly they were a good deal higher than the Medway Towns where house prices had started to fall with the news of Chatham Dockyard closing. The good news was that, as Crawley was a New Town, the local council was giving mortgages to key workers. As Mick was moving house for work, he fell into this category. His new salary was also a great help, and we still had our original Provident Life Insurance mortgage guarantee to continue using. What an amazing investment that had turned out to be. We put our home in Second Avenue on the market with a local Estate agent
The first visit we made to house hunt was in our Robin Reliant, leaving our sons with Nanny and Grandad. We had arranged to view a house we had seen advertised in Blindley Heath, on the main A22 East Grinstead to Croydon Rd.  I’m not sure why we chose this, it was possibly because it was in our price range and looked interesting. I’m sure we had not taken account of how far Mick would have to travel to work. Over the years he had always made a journey to work, so it would not have appeared to be a problem. It was a semi-detached home which had originally been part of a very much larger house.  It was a really interesting building with a nice garden, set back from the main road.  It was also painted pink which I really liked!  I don’t remember us looking at any other properties and we drove home. We later discovered, because of it’s origins as part of another house, it was not, at that time, a suitable investment for a mortgage.
One Saturday, a few weeks later when I had been attending a playgroup training day, Mick had shown a couple over our home. At that time, mid-70s, you could still knock at the door of a house displaying a For Sale board and reasonably ask to be shown around. Goodness knows what state our home was in that day with Mick in charge of a five and four year old! Anyway, they liked it and said they would contact the agent to confirm they wished to buy it. They bought our home for £11,050. The following weekend we had to make a serious effort to find a new home in Crawley.  Aunty Lil once again came to babysit in our home and my Mum drove us to Crawley in her comfortable, reliable Austin Maxi. We left the Robin at home!  The new homes in Crawley were out of our financial means so we only looked at the details of properties we could afford and we had details from an agent about another detached property conversion in Southgate, Crawley. It was a ground floor flat with a garage and a garden. This conversion had been done to meet current building standards with all the evidence needed to obtain a mortgage from Crawley Borough Council. We were able to view it easily as the flat was currently empty. The first floor flat above it was occupied by a couple and had its own entrance accessed by an external staircase. The ground floor flat was very spacious and had retained some nice original features. There were two bedrooms, one at the front of the house with an ensuite bath and wash basin. The second bedroom was at the rear of the house, and also had an ensuite shower room. There was a cloakroom in the hall, a large, square kitchen-diner and a sitting room with french doors opening onto the garden. It was in a very small cul-de-sac in Malthouse Road, Southgate, which was one of the old original parts of Crawley before it became a new town.  This meant it was in easy walking distance of both the new modern town centre and the older railway station. Mick could, for the very first time, walk to work. We were very excited and offered the asking price of £11,250, which was accepted as obviously we were good purchasers having sold our own property, with a mortgage agreed, and ready to move. We moved home to Crawley at the beginning of the school Easter holidays in 1975.
Robert and Richard had to share a bedroom at the back of the flat and decided to make exploring very interesting. They discovered they could climb out of the bedroom window and drop down in to the back garden which was actually quite a drop. They then ran around the house, shrieking with laughter, and knocked on the front door. Just because they could! Having told them several times not to do this I eventually asked Mick to sort them out.  I don’t think I have been forgiven for that until this day!  I was very aware we had people living in the flat above us who did not have children.
Both the boys collected toy soldiers; Richard had little miniature ones and Robert a slightly larger version. We had only been living in our house a few days when Robert said he had ‘seen a boy’ living in the corner house beside us and asked  if he could take his soldiers to show him? I said yes that was fine and to come straight back home and off he went. What a wonderful way to make new friends, and to immediately feel settled in this completely new county. The boy Robert met was called Steven Dowse and he had a sister, Philippa, who was the same age as Richard, and was also about to start school after the Easter holidays. Their parents were Janet and Vince Dowse and they became very good friends. Janet took me to see Southgate First School where both the boys were due to start, Richard as a first-timer. After the Easter holidays I walked with Janet, her two children, and Robert and Richard, to the school and joined all the other mothers leaving their children at the school gates. Richard was to be five years old, six weeks after starting school. It was quite a new school because it was in an area of the new town; very different from the old school Robert had attended in Gillingham. I can’t remember there being any problems at all for either of them, starting off on their new adventure.  It was really lucky that they had both had a chance to make new friends before they started too. It felt quite strange to leave my youngest child at school and go home to an empty house in a new town and county, where I really didn’t know anyone other than my new friend Janet, who rapidly became my new best friend. Janet drove an old Morris Mini Traveller and introduced me to the area and the new town shopping centre, which was built around a very large open paved square. There was a fountain in the middle of the square, it all looked very modern with some quite big stores including a Tesco and Sainsbury’s.
Crawley also boasted a large indoor swimming pool and leisure centre. Janet and I enrolled our children for swimming lessons and we used to sit up in the balcony watching. In the school holidays we also enrolled them in some of the activities there, including gymnastics and trampolining. There were two large beautiful parks plus a number of other smaller ones. Janet drove me to see Tilgate Park which was on rolling parkland with a lovely big lake and a small animal and bird sanctuary. I told Mick about it and we often used to take the children there on Sunday afternoons to see the animals and to walk around the lake. The other large park was Southgate park with a nice sized outdoor paddling pool for the young children. Janet introduced me to some of her friends and to another new neighbour, Marion, who also had two young sons a year or so younger than mine. The eldest was a lovely little boy named Henry and was not much younger than Richard. That summer we often went to the park together or met in each other’s homes. I was so lucky, I didn’t have time to feel lonely. Janet also introduced me to a babysitting circle she belonged to. The members used coloured pieces of card cut in two sizes, one denoted an hour and the other half an hour and that was the currency for the amount of time you sat for someone. We were all initially given the same amount of currency. It was a wonderful idea as we all got to know each other and, providing you had enough currency, you could ask someone to sit for you.  If you didn’t have enough, you had to let it be known you were free to sit to earn some more! As a consequence, Mick and I were able to have an occasional night out!  Sometimes we met up with his friends from Rank Xerox,  usually on a Friday night, after Mick had had his end of week drinks with his colleagues in the pub The Sun, opposite the office in Stoner House. I often walked to the Sun and met up with Mick there.  I remember they had an arcade game called Space Invaders which I had never seen before, and being quite intrigued. Or we would meet our friends,Lindy and Richard Waller who lived in Caterham, at the Blue Anchor in Blindley Heath.. Occasionally we would have an Indian meal in the Taj Mahal restaurant, very near where we lived at the bottom of Malthouse Road by the railway crossing; we often met Janet and Vince there.
I remember we had not lived in Crawley very long when Mick said he wanted to take me to a Bistro he had been taken to for lunch, by one of the Sales Managers, as he thought I would like it. We had only really been to restaurants very few times indeed since we had been married, as we really couldn’t afford it and there was very little choice too. You only went to a pub to drink! Mick took me to Limes Bistro in Charlwood, a small village at the bottom of one of the Gatwick runways. I loved the experience! We were given the menu and I really didn’t know what to choose. I remember Mick suggesting I had corn on the cob as that was what he had eaten previously, and he thought I would like it.  I did and that was the first time I had eaten it and had to be shown how! I had a Chicken Maryland as my main dish, something else I had never eaten before, chicken with a banana! There were so many new things to learn.




I had only been in our new home for a couple of weeks when I received a phone call from June Spaul who worked for Crawley Social Services as an under fives coordinator. She said she had received a message from the Kent Pre-school Playgroups Association that I had moved to Crawley. To this day I have no idea who would have been in touch with her. She asked if she could come and visit me, as she had ideas she would like to discuss that I might be interested in. So we arranged a date.
June had been told that I was an adult education tutor for the Playgroup National Foundation Course and asked if I would consider running one at Crawley College in the autumn.  She also told me that Southgate playgroup in the Church Hall, very near to Southgate School, desperately needed a new supervisor as their old one had left. She asked if I would be interested, It was mornings only, term time. I loved both ideas!  I started working in the playgroup almost immediately and was very pleased to find it was a community group in which everyone was involved. We had a management committee of parents and a mothers' rota. I know that sounds politically incorrect now, but that is what it was called.  We always had a helper on duty to help the staff and it worked very well. So well, in fact, that the following year one of our mothers who had become the playgroup treasurer wrote an article for the local paper entitled “Lonely Mum so Happy Now”. Because Crawley was a new town incomers often had no family ties or local friends. With young children to look after, Mums often found getting involved in a playgroup a way of making new friends and, if they wished, somewhere their pre-baby skills could be useful and appreciated. 
During the summer holidays we drove, in our Robin Reliant, to another site in Hampshire called Oakdene Holiday Park. It was smaller than Naish Farm but we still had a lovely time and made the most of the facilities. It was the first time we had been able to go on holiday using our own transport.  Unfortunately it was not very reliable, so it was good job we were members of the AA!
Later that summer, we had a new addition to our family. Mick’s friend from Maidstone, John Liddle, who had been giving him lifts to work before we had moved house, brought a little little black and white kitten into the office. He asked Mick if he would like to keep him. When Mick returned from work that day he was carrying a cardboard box and the boys and I had no idea what was inside. Mick had a big grin on his face and asked us if we would like to see. He carefully placed the box on the floor then opened it up for us to see this gorgeous black and white little ball-of-fluff kitten, Charlie had come home to join us! 
In September, I started running the Pre-school Playgroups Association foundation course one evening a week at Crawley College, I also became a member of the Crawley PPA branch. The little extra money I earned also made me think that perhaps I could afford driving lessons.  Up until then I had been pushing my bike to the school each morning with the boys and then cycling on to the playgroup.  I enjoyed the ride home at the end of the session as it was all downhill! Mick also decided it was time to change our unreliable, green Robin. He found a dealer in Horley who sold Reliants as well as motorbikes, and he was able to trade up to a much newer white one. Fortunately it was also far more reliable and we felt confident enough to take it to visit friends and family. It was also surprisingly comfortable. Mick’s 6’ 3” frame fitted into the footwell to reach the pedals and there was room for two small boys in the back seat. It could even take a suitcase in the boot at the back, luxury!
So at this point, I decided I could afford to spend my playgroup earnings on driving lessons and signed up with the British School of Motoring. I couldn’t afford too many lessons, so asked Mick to teach me in the Reliant. Crawley, as a new town, had a confusing road network with a lot of roundabouts. I think it was only the second time I had driven the car and, at one of those roundabouts, I put the car into reverse instead of first gear.  As I released the handbrake of course I shot backwards into the car behind.  Reliants are made of fibreglass, so I did not do any damage to the other car, but I did take a piece out of our Reliant’s bumper.  Mick was furious and we had to go straight home. He refused to speak to me, so I sat in our bedroom until he could recover. Obviously no more lessons for me, but I don’t think teaching was for him either!




That November Robert was seven years old. How ridiculous now to have thought  that he was getting old!  I think I just realised that he was no longer a little boy, just a young one. We had a birthday party with Janet and Marion’s children in our garden, which was still the same tangle as when we had arrived earlier in the year,   so we were able to have a bonfire after attempting to clear some of the rubbish. We also had some fireworks and I cooked sausages for everyone to have hot dogs. It reminded me of some of the happy bonfire nights we had previously had, with our neighbours in Second Avenue.
Before Christmas, Southgate school put on a play, with just the older class doing a nativity. Janet, Marion and I went to the school hall in the morning and sat in the small children’s class seats. It was the first time I had been to watch a school activity, as parents had not been invited to any in Barnsole Road. The young group, that Richard was in, dressed the children to act to popular music of the time. Richard and another child appeared, splendidly dressed in tin foil suits and foil milk bottle tops, as astronauts. A wind-up child’s toy robot was set off across the stage, sparks flying from its chest and and Richard and his companion fired their toy guns at the robot. All the time the music of Space Oddity was playing over the PA.  I’m sure that wouldn’t be particularly acceptable now, especially as toy guns are banned from many play areas! As Robert was in the older year group, he was part of the Nativity. Another thing I remember about our first Christmas in Crawley was that all the playgroup children had a party and then sang appropriate songs to their parents, leaving for the end of term with a small present. Our sons went to the Christmas Party at Stoner House, that Xerox put on for the children of staff members. Of course Father Christmas was invited too.
We three neighbours got together for a party with our children in Marion’s house on Christmas Eve although we did also have to wait for our husbands to return from work after their staff drinks parties. I remember Marion’s husband (who always made us feel a little beneath him) arriving home rather cross and inebriated as he had especially ridden his bicycle to work instead of driving the car. He had been stopped by the police on his way home for being drunk in charge of a bicycle. Sadly for him, we all thought it was hilarious!
Father Christmas did manage to find us in our new home on Christmas Day much to everyone's relief, and on Boxing Day, we drove to Kent. We had dinner with Nanny and Grandad and went on to Nanny Vere’s house for the usual Boxing Day Party.




1976 and life keeps changing! As the New Year began, Mick and I realised that the flat we had bought was not really the right home for our family. When we had moved the previous year we had been very careful with our money and it had been difficult to find the right home in a short time. We were also worried that we were moving into a more expensive area and that the properties in the Medway Towns were falling in value due to the imminent closure of large parts of the dockyard. We re-evaluated our finances now that we had more knowledge about the prices in Crawley, and also a better idea of places to live. Having looked at the local paper for suitable homes on the market, we liked the area of Furnace Green as there was a good school, Robert May, and it was still fairly near the town centre, Sports Centre, and Swimming Pool. In January we went to view a town house in Aintree Road, Furnace Green. It was an end of terrace property with three storeys. On the ground floor was a nice size hallway leading to a utility room across the back of the house with a door to the garden, a cloakroom and an integral garage. A flight of stairs led to the kitchen, dining room and lounge and another flight of stairs led to three bedrooms and a bathroom plus an airing cupboard on the landing. The house was only ten years old and had been well looked after.  It was empty as the owners had already moved away, leaving their adult son living there until buyers were found. As we walked around I ran my hand over the beautifully carved , oak dining table and admired it. Their son told me it had been made for his parents as a wedding gift. The houses all had open plan front gardens with a driveway leading to the garage, and it was only a short walk to Robert May First and Middle schools. I can remember being thrilled, the most modern home we had ever had. I remembered how the cornflake boxes, when I first married in the sixties, used to have pictures of the ideal family in the ideal modern home on them. That became my dream and suddenly it was achievable. I was very excited, I was getting a Cornflake box house! I believe we were able, due to having increased income, to pay £16,050 for our new home. We were asked to pay the additional £50 and they would leave the oak table I had admired! When we moved in, we also discovered they had left two Royal Doulton plates hanging on the wall of an Admiral and a Mayor, they still hang on my wall to this day. 
Eventually all the paper work and mortgages were agreed and we moved in on 6th April. It was lovely to be able to furnish all the rooms and to have a separate cloakroom on the ground floor as well as a utility room which had its’ own sink and washing machine and an integral garage. There was also a white, wooden, tall set of shelves which the previous owners had left in the utility room and I used these for my playgroup supplies. The door to the fenced back garden was also in the utility room. 
I continued to  supervise Southgate playgroup during the summer term and to complete my first year of tutoring the evening PPA Playgroup course at Crawley college. My first driving test was on 6th April, I was asked to read the number plate of a parked car before we set off on the test. I managed to negotiate all the Crawley roundabouts successfully. The tester found a small hill (not many in a very flat Crawley!) for me to perform my hill start and another road to complete a three point turn without reversing into anyone’s driveway! As I continued to drive the examiner told me that, when he banged his file on the dashboard, I was to perform an emergency stop! A tip that my friend Janet had given me was to put the rear view mirror slightly to one side, to make it obvious to the examiner that I was using it. Bang! And I successfully stopped. After the driving part of the test, when we arrived back at the test centre, my examiner  asked me several questions about the Highway Code then announced I had passed!  My lovely driving instructor was waiting for my return at the test centre, as I had had to use the school car for my test. The three wheeled Robin was not a suitable vehicle.  I can remember being so very excited when I got home, so much so that Mick said he would have some proper lessons now and apply for his test. All this time he had, legally, been driving the Robin on his motorbike licence. I felt as if my whole world had opened up now I could drive instead of riding my bicycle everywhere, or relying on lifts from friends.  




That summer, during June, the weather became unbelievably hot, with temperatures as high as 35 centigrade. In some parts of the country water rationing was put in place and people had to queue at standpipes in the street to collect their water. We were lucky, our local reservoir did not run out of water.  The Wimbledon fortnight, last week of June and first week of July, was exceptionally hot and I had very bad hay fever. On one or two evenings, when Mick came home from work, I would have a picnic meal packed and we would drive to our nearest seaside, Shoreham by the Sea about forty minutes away and swim in the sea. Sometimes we had a picnic, and on special days we would have takeaway fish and chips.  I felt so lucky then, that we could just make the most of getting in the car and driving to the coast.In July, Mick took his driving test and was delighted to pass.  His boss at Xerox had a company car that he did not use, as he preferred to use his own.  He offered the car to Mick, a fairly new, light brown Ford Escort. We were so thrilled to have a ‘proper’ car to drive and went off on our annual week’s holiday to Blue Waters, Chesil Beach near Weymouth. It was a very nice holiday camp and we enjoyed exploring the local area, going out to Portland Bill and Chesil Beach. In the evening we all went to the club where we enjoyed the entertainment. They had a special fancy dress competition for children and Robert won first prize as a Zulu warrior. I had bought some crepe paper for his outfit and rubbed mud from around the beach area over him!  I wrapped Richard in white crepe paper and he went as a mummy!
While we were there we chatted with an older gentleman who told us he had to go to Weymouth hospital for an appointment but didn’t know how he was going to get there.  Mick, being so proud of his new car, said he would take him and off we went the following day. Sat-navs were completely unheard of so we drove into Weymouth, which was very busy with holiday traffic, following the signs for the hospital. Mick was feeling quite stressed and was desperately trying to find where to park the car. He accidentally drove down a narrow bending road into the hospital grounds and discovered he was going the wrong way. In his panic, he tried to turn the car around in the narrow space and hit the wall.  We did manage to get out of that road but he was very concerned about returning his car to the office after our holiday. As it turned out, he parked it in the car park and his boss later appeared in the office and said to Mick “Oh dear, someone has bumped into your car, I’m so sorry”. Mick replied “Really? Oh, what a shame” and it was duly repaired by the company and everyone was happy!




Previously, in 1975, following two lost elections by conservative Edward Heath, Margaret Thatcher challenged him for the Conservative Party leadership. Unaware of what was to come from that government, we were incredibly happy.  Mick’s job was going from strength to strength and he was very well thought of at work. Rank Xerox was flourishing as everyone wanted the new photocopiers in their offices, schools, and colleges. The old Roneo document copiers were quickly being replaced. I now gave my handwritten lesson plans to the Adult Education department secretaries to type out for me, and the required number of copies were quickly printed on the Xerox machine. No-one had to stand and turn the handle for the copies as they had to do on the old Roneos!                                     
Before the new school year commenced in 1976 I had been asked to become the Regional Field Worker for the South East Regional PPA. It was not salaried but I was paid a very reasonable honorarium, plus expenses. I had also been approached by East Grinstead's head of Adult Education, Mike Wright, who asked if I would tutor the PPA evening Foundation Course for them at Sackville school. That meant I would be teaching two evening classes a week. Things had really changed quite a lot for us since we had moved from our home in the Medway Towns!
In Furnace Green there was a wonderful adventure playground for the children which was provided by Crawley District Council and they employed a play leader on the site. It was a wonderful place for children to explore, building dens of old wood and even having supervised fires.  There were swing ropes and old tyres. It was wonderful that even quite young children had so much freedom whilst being supervised.
Everyone was busy that autumn and Christmas seemed to come very quickly, we enjoyed putting up the Christmas tree in our new house.  Father Christmas arrived, fortunately knowing we had moved house, and on Boxing Day we drove to Walderslade for dinner with Nanny and Grandad then on to Nanny Vere’s for the Boxing Day Party.  On that occasion we did stay the night in Chatham.
New Year arrived and it was 1977, I was going to be 30 years old in a few weeks and we had decided to have our first ever really big party at home. We had become friendly with a couple, Christine and Dave who lived a few doors along the road from us, they also had two young sons. The eldest was the same age as Richard and the other one, a little younger. Christine and Dave were invited to our party and we arranged with them that our two boys would stay with their sons overnight. We would then take turns to run up to check everyone was alright during the course of the evening. I know that would not be acceptable now, but then it was common practice amongst family and friends.




The night arrived, I had put a party spread on the table in the dining room and the kitchen was full of various beers, Martini and Cinzano (red and white!) which, in addition to avocado for snowballs, was the ladies’ favourite of the time. I’m sure other drinks were available depending on what was contributed!
In the living room the sofa and chairs were pushed back and the record turntable was ready to play.  You could only play one LP at a time, but we could still stack up ten 45 singles to drop onto the turntable.  Mick had bought me Abba Arrival LP as my birthday present and that was very popular at the time, along with groups like Boney M and Blondie. Of course, we had Mick’s famous box of records and other people always brought their favourites with them. Pete and Pat Last traveled from the Medway Towns and stayed the night with us. The friends and neighbours we had made in Crawley came, as did a lot of our friends from Rank Xerox. We were all having a wonderful evening when a knock came on the front door at about ten pm. It was the police who had received a complaint about noise from the only, older neighbour who had not been invited, Mr Tidy. Unfortunately Mr. Tidy was known by the locals as an unfriendly man. The two young policemen were great and just asked us to turn the music down. So on the party went. An hour and a half later the same police officers returned, Mr. Tidy had rung again. This time the officers came in, removed their caps and stayed for a while. One of them even made a date with a single friend who was there! Can you imagine that happening now in 2023? You wouldn’t even be able to make a phone call to a local station as there aren’t any, they’ve all been sold off! What a great start to the year!
I enjoyed being a Regional Field Worker for PPA. It was really an advisory role, but the South East region was also looking to open an office that would serve Kent, Sussex, and Surrey. Mike Wright, the Adult Education head, was also looking for somewhere to run classes in the daytime as, obviously, the schools we used for evening classes were not available during the day. Coincidentally, the nuns in East Grinstead were looking for community use for the school they had just closed. It is strange when sometimes everything comes together at just the right moment. I became the coordinator of this project in my Regional role, and also as a PPA tutor in East Grinstead. East Grinstead, in Sussex, was the ideal spot; Kent was only a few miles east on the A264 and Surrey a few miles north on the A22. Everyone became involved in the project, the old school hall was used for large meetings, the PPA South East regional office was opened in an old first-floor classroom and I helped establish a community playgroup on the ground floor. Mike was able to use some rooms for daytime classes, especially ones that would help people back into employment. And, of course, the nuns benefitted by charging everyone rent! I continued to run my evening classes and in the summer of that year became the Sussex PPA County Chairman.




Mick decided, thank goodness, that spring to change to Robin Reliant for a ‘real’ car. He took it back to the motorbike dealer he had bought it from in Horley, to see if he was interested in buying it back. As luck had it, the owner of the dealership told Mick he was selling his own blue Morris 1300, and he would do a straight exchange for the Robin. The deal was done and we had our own first proper car with four wheels! Fortunately Mick was still able to use his boss’s car so I was able to use the Morris. I started attending day classes once a month at Worthing Teacher Training Centre provided by the PPA for their tutors. I found it all very new and interesting. I enjoyed learning new things like Transactional Analysis and bought the recommended book “I”m OK, you’re OK’. The skills I was learning, I didn’t realise at the time, would be very useful throughout my future career. I also attended a two year monthly day course, for adult education Early Years tutors at Guildford University and really, being able to drive made all this possible. These courses were term time only so it fitted in with the boys schooling too.
That summer it was the Queen’s Silver Jubilee. Sadly, after the beautiful summer of the previous year, the weather was gloomy and wet. We had booked another week at the Blue Waters holiday camp in Weymouth as we had enjoyed it so much the previous year. There was an extended half term holiday at the end of May, beginning of June, to celebrate the Queen’s Jubilee. That was the week we had already booked for our holiday. Richard’s birthday was very often during the summer half term week so we would hide his presents and cards to take with us. That year we had bought Richard a ventriloquist dummy.  We were two miles from home in Three Bridges when I realised we had left it at home. I quietly asked Mick to return home and I made an excuse to the boys about having forgotten some luggage, or maybe food as we were self catering. 
In April, early May, Robert had contracted chickenpox at school.  After Easter is well known as being chicken pox season. Fortunately his scabs had all healed over so off we went in our Morris. We settled into our caravan on the Saturday we arrived, but on the Sunday Richard woke up feeling poorly. I made him a bed on the sofa but he went downhill quite quickly.  During the afternoon he obviously had a high temperature, his eyes rolled and he started talking gobbledegook. I realised he was delirious, and also realised I couldn’t go and ring my mother which I had always done with the children before, but I remembered she had told me aspirin brought down a fever. When I was young she would crush an aspirin between two teaspoons and mix it with sugar to spoon into us.  This is what I did for newly seven year old Richard. Thank goodness he survived! The spots developed immediately and I made sure he also wore sunglasses. 
The boys returned to school for the summer term, all signs of chickenpox having disappeared. The school was only at the end of the road so they went independently. One afternoon Robert came home but there was no sign of Richard and I became increasingly concerned. Robert said he had not seen him, so I walked along our street and also up Epsom Close, which was opposite our home. I couldn’t find him and none of his friends had seen him. An hour after he should  have been home I called the police telling them my seven year old had not come home from school. They told me to wait another hour until five o’clock and, if he had not come home, to call them again. At half past four, Richard wandered in the front door very unconcerned. As well as the enormous feeling of relief, I was very cross.  “Where have you been?” I asked (probably shouted!) “I was with my friend” he said, his friend lived in the next road along to ours. “We have been playing aeroplanes.” he said, “We were sitting in his Dad’s van, it was our aeroplane!” And that was why I couldn’t find him, because he was sitting in a large van!  That same summer I also had a problem with Robert and his imagination. Both the boys had been given Action Man uniforms the previous Christmas including a red army cap. Action Man was a very popular Mattel toy in the early 70’s and there was a stream of other Action Man items produced. One day Robert had worn the uniform to school (no uniforms for junior schools then) and had told another boy that he belonged to a junior army group in Crawley. The first I knew about it was the boy’s mother knocked at our door to ask me where the group met as her son wanted to go! I apologised and said it was just a game! That summer, Chris and Ann Goldthorpe returned from New Zealand and came to visit us.  They had had another baby boy whilst they were there, and named him Russell, it was lovely to see them again. Wolf and Christine Babin also visited us with Karen and Sacha. We always took our visitors to Tilgate Park, as it was such large interesting place to visit, and we could have a good walk around the park.
As the summer wore on, we continued our Sunday afternoon explorations. The boys loved to visit Warnham War Museum, near Horsham, and we also liked going to the Saturday market at Burgess Hill. I remember we once went to an historical house in Cuckfield on a guided tour, the boys seemed a little spooked by the stories of monks’ hiding places and ghosts the tour guide was telling. I actually can’t remember exactly the remark one of them made but the whole group of visitors laughed loudly!




One Sunday in September a week before our tenth wedding anniversary on the 30th of September, my sister Patsy, with Roger, came to visit us unexpectedly early in the evening. We decided to have an Indian takeaway which Mick drove into the town to collect. We enjoyed our meal with them and they later left to drive to their home in Keysoe Bedfordshire. They told us all about the extension they were adding to their home. After they had left I made us our late night drink and Mick complained about a pain he had in his chest.  We decided it must be indigestion from the Indian meal we had eaten and went to bed.   Mick seemed to sleep well, as far as I knew, although his breathing did sound bit odd.  I woke on the Monday morning and went downstairs to make tea for the boys and I, and coffee for Mick. I gave the boys their tea first and told them it was time to get up and get ready for school. I took Mick his coffee but he was complaining of being in a lot of pain and his breathing sounded awful too. I told hime to stay in bed while I got the breakfast and the boys off to school. It never occurred to either of us that it could have been his heart, I guess that didn’t enter our heads as we were so young, Mick was 34. Once the boys had gone to school I got in my car and drove to our doctor’s surgery, who was still in Southgate, as I knew there was no point in ringing on a Monday morning. When I explained the symptoms the doctor said it sounded like a collapsed lung and that I should take him to A&E at Crawley hospital immediately. I drove home, helped Mick dress and drove him straight into Crawley. He was examined and immediately taken to an isolation ward. They told me to leave and call later for more information. I couldn’t believe this was happening, and we had been making plans for our tenth wedding anniversary at the end of the week too! I didn’t know anything about collapsed lungs and it sounded very serious. I called in the early afternoon and it was confirmed that he had a spontaneous pneumothorax (collapsed lung). He had been admitted to an individual isolation ward, as he could not run the risk of being infected by anything else. The air, which had escaped from the lung into the rib cavity, had to be drained so that his lung would reinflate. I was told I could bring the boys in to see him, but they could only see him through the window and talk on the intercom.  I was allowed into the room, but I had to gown up first.
When the boys returned from school I explained where their father was and that we could go to see him.  It wasn’t our first visit to Crawley hospital as I had already had to take Richard to the A&E department for a minor problem to be sorted.
The situation at the hospital was just as had been explained to me. The hospital was modern with large glass windows everywhere so we could see Mick sitting up in bed. Unfortunately, it was before televisions were readily available in hospital wards, but he did have a radio and we had taken books for him to read. Personally I felt safe and reassured that Mick was going to be alright.  The doctor explained that this was a relatively common problem particularly for tall people, and Mick was six foot three. He explained they had a larger chest cavity and therefore the lung stretched further making it a little thinner. He said Mick would have to be careful in the future not to over exert himself. After five days, we were very relieved to have Mick back at home with us to rest. Little did I know then, this was just the beginning. Two weeks later Mick was back at work and normal life resumed. Celebrating our tenth wedding anniversary ceased to be important, we were so relieved, we thought then everything was over.




Autumn came, with both boys now in Robert May Junior School. Richard enjoyed acting, and was very happy to play the part of Dick Whittington in the Christmas play,, and Robert dressed in his Action Man outfit, hoping to find an army group to join! I returned to running more evening classes having been asked to also run one in Cuckfield as well as East Grinstead. 
That Christmas, my parents came to stay with us arriving on Christmas Eve. We had found Richard a small bike and I collected it Christmas Eve, to try to make sure it was surprise on Christmas morning. Neither of the boys then were sure that Father Christmas really existed, although they certainly were concerned that if they didn’t believe, there might not be any presents! Once they were tucked up in bed and we thought they were asleep we started frantically wrapping all the presents and put them around the Christmas tree, to be discovered the next morning. We still used Mick’s black-and-white-striped football socks as Christmas stockings, each of which were filled with small gifts and left at the foot of their beds by Father Christmas, when we went to bed. My parents were watching the black and white TV and Mick rescued the bike and brought it in the the living room to put by the tree. Whether the noise of him bringing the bike up to the living room in our town house disturbed Richard, I don’t know but, just as we had closed the door, Richard came downstairs and I panicked thinking he would see what we had been doing.  Father Christmas was about to be unmasked! I quickly tried to stand in the doorway so he couldn’t see much inside the room and told him to go back to bed, or there certainly would be no surprises the next morning.
New Year’s Eve was spent with our next-door neighbours, Pam and Brian. We had become good neighbours and were discussing with Brian about putting in a gas boiler and gas central heating so we could get rid of our large, old electric storage heaters. Charlie, our cat, had become so used to coming to find us in their house or reminding Pam he wanted to be fed when we were away that he had a permanent hard ridge on his nose, which he had developed by lifting the letter box to knock on the door with his nose! We didn’t have a cat flap, and we didn’t need one! He was a very clever boy. In the spring of 1978 our central heating system was fitted and I was thrilled to having a working airing cupboard on the landing for all the bed linen and towels. We also replaced the patio fire escape window, in the first floor living room, with a very nice, double glazed, tilt and turn window.  We were very happy to feel we were improving our home.
That spring, we also went to South Wales on holiday with Pete and Pat Last and their two boys, Jamie and Christian. We had adjoining chalets on a small self catering site on the Gower Peninsula. The scenery was spectacular and there were expansive, sandy beaches which the boys loved to play on. We went to Tenby for the day, and explored the Cambrian mountains and the Brecon Beacons. It was our first trip to Wales and little did we know then in future years we would return many times!




One day I received a phone call from Mick, who was at work.  He asked if I would like a temporary, part-time clerical job in his office. I thought that sounded brilliant, and the extra money would help to pay for all the things we were doing.  We were already discussing changing the car again! Mick told me it was just small clerical work like the daily post and filing.  He told me I had to go into Crawley and register with an Employment Agency, one which carried the Rank Xerox vacancies. I drove into Crawley town centre, went to the agency he had told me about, and enquired about the vacancy, telling them I had been told to register with them to apply. So there I was, back working in an office! I enjoyed meeting new people and was happy to sort the post and put all the envelopes through the franking machine. The filing was actually putting the written files onto microfiche to ensure that all the customer and product sales information could be stored in a much smaller space. This was before offices had computers installed. I worked there for a few months, until the schools broke up.




June 1978 was football world cup time again and, as always, it was an exciting time in our house as Mick and Rob were both huge fans. Mick decided we should get a colour TV.  I really didn’t think we needed a colour TV, I thought I could ‘see’ all the colours anyway on our black and white one! The mind does wonderful things! Anyway, my objections came to nothing and I remember Mick and I going shopping for a new one in the new Bewbush area of Crawley. We had not visited the new shopping precinct before, but found a very good electrical shop, chose our TV and placed an order making sure it would arrive before the World Cup began. It duly arrived a few days later and, as well as being a colour TV, it also had a remote control! That consisted of a long lead which you plugged in the front of the television connected to a controller, which you held in your hand wherever you were sitting. Such excitement! Everyone wanted to have a go at controlling the TV, changing channels, volume, and all in COLOUR! Of course, it did mean that the lead was permanently draped across the living-room room, and could be a trip hazard too!
Our other super exciting new purchase, after the TV, was to be a new car. We spent some time looking at different second hand cars and eventually settled on a blue Alfa Romeo Alfasud 1300. We were thrilled.  Eleven years married, our own home, two children and a car; we felt we had really come far. We had no savings, but loved what we had achieved.








All this was set to a backdrop of growing unrest though. In 1973 the IRA had expanded their attacks to sow terror in mainland Britain and reorganised themselves in 1977 into detached cells. The far right in Britain was also a growing force. Punk music and the Sex Pistols became very popular. Interestingly, against this background, our life was peaceful and happy.  The fact I was now able to contribute to our joint income, and was a good housekeeper did make a difference. We loved being a family and Mick and I also had wonderful Xerox staff events. At Christmas there were always dinner dances, usually at Gatwick Manor Hotel, and after one particularly successful event we were invited on a summer boat trip on the Thames.  Boney M was playing, the sun was shining and there was lots of food and drink! I think that must have been a weekend when the boys stayed with Nanny and Grandad!
After my work at Xerox I really started to feel I would like to return to a salaried job, and started looking in the local paper for vacancies, just as I had years before. I found a part-time, 18-hour-a-week, job advertised by Crawley Social Services for a home care organiser. I remembered my first full-time job had been for a home care organiser in Gillingham, and I also knew a little about people needing nursing care at home, from my mother being a District Nurse. It also stipulated that you would need to be able to drive. I liked the idea of the job and 18 hours a week was certainly manageable during term time, so I decided to apply. Much to my delight I was invited for interview. I can still remember what I wore for the interview and, to be honest, with hindsight I think I should have worn something smarter than my bright green, long sleeved blouse and my denim skirt especially as I wore flip flops too! The interview was with the Crawley social services male head and Pat, the home care manager. For some completely unknown reason, I got the job! The manager of social services had attended the West Sussex PPA AGM which I had chaired that summer in Worthing, so that might have helped. Pat told me she thought it would be nice to have youngster in the team, well thank you Pat for giving me that opportunity! We agreed I could start at the beginning of September, after the school summer holidays.
That summer we decided, instead of our usual self-catering caravan or chalet holiday, we would go and stay in a family hotel in Ilfracombe. Although it was very comfortable and it was lovely being catered for, we discovered it was not really the holiday for us as we had to share any available space with others, and had just our bedroom for ourselves.  We did enjoy our holiday as we went out everyday visiting different places, including Lynton and Lynmouth at the river Lynne estuary, a place where thirty-four people had lost their lives in the 1952 flood disaster. We visited the museum. There is also the world’s highest and steepest, completely water powered, funicular Cliff Railway. A lovely area, we always enjoyed our annual week’s family holiday and I have wonderful memories.
To begin my new job with Crawley social services I needed to find myself a car.  The manager that had let Mick use his company car had retired, so Mick now used the family car for travelling to and from work. I checked out second-hand cars in the local paper, where everything could be found in those pre-internet days. I found a private seller advertising a Hillman Minx for £150, which was as much as I could afford. I remembered my Uncle Billy having a Hillman during my childhood and he always said they were good cars.  I really didn’t know very much, but I was always an optimist!  I rang the phone number and arranged to go and see the car.  It really was quite old, with a bench seat in the front  and the gears on the steering column. It was roadworthy and had a year’s MOT. I don’t think it did many miles to the gallon but we were not so concerned about that then. I paid cash for it, collected the paperwork and took it home to Furnace Green. I was really quite pleased to have my own not so little car!
September came and the new term started. I continued teaching evening classes and I also started my new job. I was given two areas, Ifield and Gossops Green in Crawley, where I would be responsible for interviewing any referrals made to us for help and for assessing how much and what type of help they needed.  A finance officer from the council would then also visit to assess how much the care would cost; quite a large proportion of clients were funded by the local council in those days but all the help was means-tested. I also had a team of home helps in each area and was responsible for matching them with clients, taking account of their workload and travelling distance to each person’s home. At the end of each week, I had to create and post out the following week’s time-sheet to the home helps and at the beginning of each week I received the completed time sheets, which I had to check, sign, and pass through to the finance department for payment. The home helps were lovely, friendly, cheerful ladies and we really loved seeing them call in to the office. We also had occasional social functions, and I remember we organised a wonderful coach outing to go to London to see the King And I. It was 1978, and Yul Brynner was playing the part of the King. It happened to be the last production he appeared in, and he was quite magnificent.
I enjoyed my work and learned about respecting elderly people, who I had not really had reason to come in contact with before, other than our family and the wonderful Aunty Bowsher. I remember visiting an elderly gentleman who lived in Ifield Manor, he had been widowed and needed help to live alone. Ifield had originally been a village with the Manor House, when Crawley New Town was built. Any land it had owned had been bought up and developed. I remember, when I visited, he was sitting in a high backed chair by the old inglenook fireplace.  It was a huge room, full of beautiful antiques although very obviously dusty and untouched, just like I had always imagined Satis House in Great Expectations.  The house that Aunty Bowsher told me about in The Vines, Rochester. I can’t remember the gentleman’s name now but he had a lovely aged face, and as we chatted about his needs he told me his wife had recently died, he was obviously lonely and he really didn’t know how to cope. He also told me he had been a Tiger Moth pilot in the First World War. I knew a little about the planes, as they had been built in Rochester and there was one displayed outside of the aptly named local pub. They were very flimsy especially compared to the fighters of World War Two. What an amazing life he had led!
The five staff in the Home Care social services team occupied a small area in the open plan office of Crawley Social Services ,so I was in regular contact with all the other social workers. There was one person who was responsible for organising Family Aids for people who needed additional support at home. The Family Aids were often used as a support for families with young children and teenagers who had problems, often teaching skills such as budget-management and cooking skills. The NSPCC local inspector often visited our office, and would refer people for additional support. I knew his wife Margaret, who ran a special group for disadvantaged children and was a member of PPA. June Spaull, the under-fives co-ordinator that I knew, was also based there. I loved being part of a team that helped others.I found the part-time work fitted in well with the school day. I was also on the road, just as my mother had been as a District Nurse, so that if the children were at home I always let my neighbour know and I was able to call in and check on them. Christmas school holidays were short and we also had a lot of bank holidays so, together with taking some annual leave, I was able to cope. It was the same at Easter and Mick would help with taking some of his leave for child care too.  The longer summer holidays were more difficult but I would take some annual leave, as did Mick, and we overlapped when we were going away on our family holiday. It all seemed to work out.




The autumn term started again and I still continued with my evening classes in East Grinstead, teaching just one evening a week. I also continued to be a member of the babysitting circle, so we could find baby sitters if we wanted a night out together. Occasionally Mick would feel a pain starting in his chest but he knew to rest and be careful until it had passed.
Christmas 1978, and again we had Christmas Day at home and visited the family on Boxing Day ending with the traditional party at Nanny Vere’s house. We all had a wonderful time and the boys enjoyed all the sandwiches, jelly and cream, washed down with lots of lemonade, with Robert eating his fair share of cheese sandwiches. We didn’t stay the night as I had offered to drive the Alfasud home. We were only a few miles from home, near Lingfield racecourse, when Robert suddenly announced from the back that he felt sick. Before I could find somewhere to pull in, off the narrow road, he threw up between the two front seats and all over the carpet covering the gearbox! Poor car, it never recovered.  Robert though, felt much better! A few months into the new year of 1979 and we knew we could not go on a long journey in that car again. As soon as the gearbox got hot, the smell became unbearable. Mick and I went to Shaws garage in the middle of Crawley. They were keen to have our Alfa (little did they know!) and we exchanged it for a gun metal grey Renault 19.
We heard at the beginning of the new term, that unfortunately changes were planned for Robert May school, it was to be combined with the Tilgate school and the children would move on to Tilgate Upper School, instead of the one at Three Bridges which we had been expecting. Mick and I talked about moving home again. I told him about some of the villages I had found near East Grinstead where I had been teaching. He felt they were near enough to his work in Crawley and said perhaps we should look. We had already visited Ashurst and Forest Row where we had previously taken the boys on our exploring trips. I bought the local paper for the East Grinstead area and we started our search.


This time we’ve found home




One day, I found a house advertised for sale in Crawley Down.  It was £24,000 and we thought we could just about afford to increase our mortgage once again, to meet the repayments. One afternoon, I decided to drive to Crawley Down and just have a look at the area, the village, and to look at the outside of the house, which was in Squires Close. As I approached the 1960s semi detached house, I thought it looked empty. I parked the car, walked up the drive and peered through the large picture windows (and the letterbox)! Goodness knows what the neighbours thought but, as no-one approached me, I felt I had not been seen! Through the letterbox I could see down the hall into the kitchen, which was painted bright red. I could also see something of the garden behind out through the kitchen window.  I worked out that the garden backed onto the playing fields behind, King George V Recreation Ground. I just knew this was the right home for us!  I liked the village, which had a lovely village green, some shops and a pub. The village school was exactly the kind of school I wanted our sons to go to.  That evening I reported it all to Mick and he told me to call the agent and ask for the details, which I did the following day. Mick and I arranged a viewing while the boys were at school. The rooms all had radiators but they were fired by a solid fuel stove in the kitchen and the open fireplace in the lounge diner also had a wet back for domestic hot water. I was happy about that, as I had grown up with a solid fuel boiler in the kitchen of my childhood home. I always had the feeling that fires you looked after yourself were living things! There were three bedrooms and a separate bathroom and toilet upstairs. A conservatory (of sorts) had been added, but it had a leaking corrugated plastic roof! The garden was mainly lawn, boxed in by hedges giving us plenty of privacy and a wonderful outlook over King George’s field.  There was a detached garage (thinking back now, I suspect it was made with an asbestos roof!) and coal bunkers behind the garage. There was a weeping willow tree in the corner of the front garden and a large corkscrew one in the far corner of the back garden.  We loved it!
That evening, back at home in Furnace Green, we told the boys all about it and arranged to take them for a look at the weekend. Mick and I then sat at talked about the finances, which we could only just afford, and of course it would also depend on how much we could sell our current home for. We were very pleased that we had made several good improvements to the house within the few short years we had been there, especially the gas central heating. We knew we would also have to pay off our Crawley Town Council mortgage, as we were moving out of their area, and apply for a new mortgage with a new lender.
Our home in Furnace Green sold easily, and we paid £24,000 for our new home in Squires Close in Crawley Down. We moved there during the Easter holidays of April 1979. The move was not without its problems! We had bought the house through an Estate Agent in Crawley Down village. All our possessions were packed up in the removal van and Mick and I both had cars. In my car I had Charlie, our cat, in his cat box and Robert in the front seat balancing a goldfish bowl which he eventually had to put between his feet as the water kept slopping out! Mick had his precious record collection, an electric kettle, and Richard in his car. We drove to the Estate Agent to collect the key, it was just before lunch time. We were told that the money had not arrived from our mortgage company, so our solicitors could not exchange and we could not move in! After a slight panic the agent reassured us that this was quite common and suggested we went to the village pub, which was opposite, for lunch. They agreed to look after Charlie for us, plus the goldfish who had his water replenished! It was just the start of making so many wonderful friends in the village and we had a very nice lunch in the pub which was called the Prizefighters.  I understand it has now, sadly, closed.
After 2pm, we were told all was well and we could have the keys for our new home.  We quickly drove to meet up with the removal lorry outside of our house. Very efficiently, everything was moved into the house and, most importantly, the beds were installed in the right rooms. We had already moved several times and in Furnace Green Richard had the bigger bedroom at the back of the house, so this time Robert had that bedroom and Richard the smallest room at the front.
I found the box marked bed linen and made up the beds. Next, having already filled the kettle Mick had, we found sufficient crockery, tea and milk to make everyone a cup of tea! With all our possessions safely inside the house the removal men left and we were ready to start the next chapter of our lives.
The white table and chairs, of which I had been so proud when we could afford to buy them, were put in the conservatory as were the white shelves that had been so useful in the utility room in Furnace Green. My parents had, some time before, given us their two Remploy fireside chairs and we put them in the conservatory too.  In the dining area of the lounge diner we put the beautiful oak gate leg table which we had brought from Furnace Green and we had four old dining chairs and a carver chair to match, which we had bought from an antique fair.  We also had the lovely, previously mentioned, antique sideboard from our very first home which also matched our table and chairs.  I was very proud of the way our dining area looked, ready for all the dinner parties and social events we had planned! We also had a floor standing record rack for LPs and 45s. On top of this we placed the record player we had brought with us from our Gillingham home.
The lounge area had the three-piece suite, including Mick’s swivel char, which we had also bought new in Gillingham and now placed next to the very nice open fireplace. The television with the remote control was on the far side of the fireplace, with Mick’s swivel chair in prime position! I think we all immediately felt at home in that house.




Before we had moved in, I had telephoned the Crawley Down junior CofE school to arrange for the boys to start there after the Easter holidays. It was a wonderful old building next door to the church and had been the only village school for very many years until a new infants school had had to be built in Burleigh Way, as so many new homes had been built in the village. The junior school was also busting at the seams, and there were several additional, (not so) temporary classroom huts in the school playing field.
We had only been in our new home a couple of days and were very busy unpacking.  I had taken some leave from work, but Mick was still going into Crawley every day to Rank Xerox. Once the boys had finished unpacking their things in their rooms, they went out to explore the local area. When they returned home Robert said he thought he had seen a boy in the house opposite and could he go and ask him if he wanted to come and play.  Of course I said yes, and Jonathon Clements duly arrived in our home! They were playing happily when some time later a lady appeared at our back door asking if her son was with us! I asked her in and that was the start of a friendship, which continues to this day, with Steve (Stephanie) and David Clements. Jonathon turned out to be the same age as Richard, and their daughter Katherine (Kate) was a few years younger and still at the infants school. After the Easter holidays, the boys set off happily with new friend Jonathon to their new school. They were becoming dab hands at changing schools and making new friends.  I was very proud of them. 
A few days later Steve came to visit, to invite me to a Tupperware party she was having, and I very happily said yes as I had returned to work and had not had a chance to meet anyone else. They were happy days, when the party plan sales were working.  It was just a very good way for local people to meet up. With hindsight I feel a little sorry for some of the sales people, although mostly they were mums trying to earn some money in their spare evenings, when they could leave their children with their partners and not pay a babysitter. The various parties I went to over the years, Tupperware, Pippa Dee, jewellery parties, pottery parties et al, none of us really spent a lot of money because few of us had any to spare!  We all came home with little free gifts and someone always booked another party to hold in their house. We enjoyed the chat and coffee and biscuits! Steve had quite a number of neighbours at her party that night and I remember perching on the arm of the settee and talking to the lady beside me. We introduced ourselves to one another, and I explained we had just moved into the close into the house opposite. I told her my name was Eileen and I had two boys who had just started at the local school.  She said her name was Jane and she had three girls, the middle one was in the junior school, the eldest had just started at Imberhorne School in East Grinstead and youngest was still at the infant school where Kate was. “Do you work?” Jane  asked, ‘Yes’ I replied, “I work as a home care organiser for Crawley Social Services. ‘Oh” Jane said “I work as an EWO (Education Welfare Officer) for Crawley Social Services but we are based at an office on Southgate Road”. I was based in the office in the town centre. Jane also lived in Squires Close next door but one to Steve and Dave. I loved Jane, we quickly became best friends and discovered we had so much in common. I soon met other people too. Carol and Bernard lived next door to Jane and her husband John, before they completed their move to the neighbouring Vicarage Road, and Roger and Trish lived on the other side.  They had two very young children Daniel and a little girl, Samantha. Next door to us lived Miss Violet Emy who was a retired school headmistress. She was a very nice lady and an interesting character. Often on a Sunday morning whilst we were still in bed drinking our tea (well I was, Mick only drank coffee!) We would hear Miss Emy playing her piano which was obviously placed by the adjoining wall under our bedroom! I had visions of her playing for the morning assembly in her school and all the girls lining up to walk in. I was sure it had to be a girls school, as I was reminded of my own school days. We were very quickly and happily settled in our new home. The boys once again started at the village school for the summer term and, by then, already knew the local children from our road.


We had only been in our new home for a few weeks when we had a shocking surprise. Margaret Thatcher had very recently been voted in as the conservative prime minister and decided that the way to control inflation was to raise interest rates. We had coped with various rises in interest rates since we had first taken out a mortgage in 1967. In 1971 the rate was 7.5% and had changed very little since we had bought our first home. By 1974 it was 10.03% but then briefly dipped to 8.0%. In 1979 we received a letter notifying us the rate was rising to 12.9% just after we had increased the amount we had borrowed to buy our new house. Things were going to be tough, and by October 1981 they even reached 18.6%! To help us cope, I visited our bank which was then still the Trustee Savings Bank where I had first started banking all those years ago, paying in my card of government savings stamps and, later on, my Woolworths Saturday job money when I had anything left over. They advised me to set up a budget account so that I always knew there was an account where I put money to cover all the essentials. This worked for a while, Mick had a good stable job with Rank Xerox and I still worked part time for Crawley Social Services, as well as running two evening classes. We did have to be careful with money especially as we also each had a car to run. It was shortly after this we decided to have a credit card each for the first time.  It was almost as good as magic! Credit cards were first introduced in Britain in 1966, but only the very rich used them, it was only later in the seventies that they were more widely advertised. Debit cards only became available in 1987. I was careful to make sure there was enough money in our budget account to make a monthly payment but it was rare for us to be able to clear the whole amount. We only really used the credit card for exceptional things like buying a new three piece suite or the occasional meal out, which was often still on a Friday night with Janet and Vince at the Taj Mahal Indian restaurant in Crawley. Janet also took me to a wonderful preloved clothes shop and I looked through my wardrobe for clothes I could leave to sell there, and often replaced some of my own wardrobe from the same place. I remember getting the boys their school rugby shirts there too, as they were too expensive to buy new.
A few months after we had moved to our new home we decided to have a summer housewarming party. We invited all our new neighbour friends, friends from Rank Xerox, as well as Janet and Vince and some neighbours we were friends with from our previous home. It was great fun and I remember Ra-Ra frilled skirts had become popular. Our small group of ladies had also invented a dance called the pocket dance which I think was just a version of dancing around the handbags which we had all done in our younger days! All a bit silly I suppose but I just remember us dancing in a little group, hands in pockets and doing a bit of head banging if we were listening to Status Quo! We just all had great fun and there was a lot of laughter especially when Roger, Trisha’s husband appeared from the kitchen wearing my yellow Marigolds and joined in the dancing!
I had prepared a cold buffet and the food was put in the conservatory on my old white table. Our new neighbours Jane and John came, John was always ridiculous and funny company at parties. He had worked as a waiter on the pullman trains out of London when he left school and remained a committed steam train enthusiast all his life. His party piece was to serve everyone drinks whilst pretending he was a waiter on a moving train (and he was also rather inebriated too). It was like taking part in a live TV comedy sketch; so funny. A great time was had by all but unfortunately Jane and John moved away to Wiltshire a couple of months later. The following summer we went to visit them in their new home in the Wylie Valley with Robert and Richard and we all took a large picnic to Longleat House and ate our picnic and played rounders on the lawn. Jane and John remained our best friends for the remainder of their lives.




During the half term week at the end of May we returned once again to the same chalet at Naish Farm, Highcliffe.  It felt like home from home! One day we visited the model town of Tucktonia near Christchurch. We were all fascinated by the tiny, miniature detail of the various model properties. We spent some lovely days on the beautiful Bournemouth sandy beach and, one day, drove inland to visit Marwell Zoo near Winchester. We always so enjoyed our family holidays all together and exploring new places.
For our summer holiday that year, having already paid for an earlier holiday, were allowed the use of a caravan on Hayling Island off Portsmouth. My work with Crawley Social Services and the Pre School Play Group Association had put me in contact with some lovely, like minded colleagues including the local Inspector of the NSPCC David Baker and his wife Margaret. They owned a caravan on the island and asked very kindly if we would like to use it during the school holidays. It sounded a lovely idea and somewhere I not even heard of!  The weather was lovely and warm; the boys enjoyed putting all their pennies in the slot machines and we spent time sitting on the local beach.  We also took a day trip to the Isle of Wight again on the Red Funnel ferry and visited Blackgang Chine.




In the autumn of that year we received some very sad news from Mick’s brother Steve. He and Joy had brought Vera and Aunty Lil to visit us one Sunday shortly after we had moved in and Vera had not really seemed herself. Steve now told us that she had been admitted to St Barts hospital in Rochester. We visited that coming weekend and left Robert and Richard with my parents while we went to the hospital. It was obvious Vera was seriously ill. The doctor asked Steve and Mick into the office whilst Joy and I waited outside. They were told Vera was in the last stages of Oesophageal cancer. All I can really remember was that it was a very somber time. It was the first time I had experienced an impending family death. I had realised when they had visited us earlier in the year that Vera had alcohol problems, and Aunty Lil had told me how worried she was about her sister. Vera was also a heavy smoker. Mick managed to visit his mother on a few occasions in the following two weeks, driving to the hospital in Rochester after finishing work.  Thank goodness we had our own reliable transport then. Shortly after that we received the inevitable phone call.
The funeral seemed strange to me although, with hindsight, I think it was quite possible it was the first one I had ever attended. The immediate family all had to sit together whilst Joy and I had to sit in the row behind. I was quite upset having to sit behind Mick when I thought he needed comfort. Anyway, like everything in life, it ended and I really can’t remember anything about a wake although I’m sure there would have been one. Vera’s three remaining sisters would have organised something.




Christmas that year came and went, obviously, without the annual Boxing Day party, in fact there were no more in Chatham but lots of happy memories remained.

Just after Christmas, Mick and I decided we could just about afford a family holiday  abroad for the first time. We talked about where we could go as Mick would find it difficult to have a family holiday somewhere too sunny. In that quiet week between Christmas and New Year we decided to go to the travel agents in East Grinstead and see if they had any ideas of what might be suitable for us. We had to be careful with expense and wanted to self cater. We went to the travel agent on New Years Eve. It was a very exciting proposition for us. We looked at some brochures and then sat and talked with one of the advisors.  We had not thought of it but when Scandinavia was mentioned we became very interested! It was explained we would take the car to Felixstowe and catch a Tor Line ferry overnight to Gothenburg. We would then drive ourselves to Sommarviks Fritidscentre in Varmland, Sweden during the May half term holiday. There we would hire a four berth, wooden chalet. I still can’t believe how all this happened and how excited I was! And it was very different to the holidays our friends had planned!

On New Years Eve Steve and Dave held a New Years Eve party all the usual friends and neighbours were there and, of course, I couldn’t wait to tell people our exciting news, I think I must have been floating on a cloud or something! Needless to say it was a wonderful party and we all had a great time. As their house was opposite ours we could see into our living room to keep an eye on the boys and they knew exactly where we were if they needed us. 





The 1980 Spring term began and the boys returned to Crawley down Junior School, it was Robert’s last two terms at the school. I had joined the PTA ( Parent Teacher Association), as Jane had suggested I might like to fill her vacancy when they moved away.  All the parents were so friendly and we were very involved in raising funds for the school.  There was a lovely head teacher named Gwyneth Stevens and she made it clear to the parents the funds would only be used for ‘extras’ for the children as the state should provide all the necessary teaching materials and building maintenance. 

We organised some social evening events which often the children could come to as well.  I remember we held a Teddy Drive which was organised the same way as a Beetle Drive when you collected the correctly numbered part on the throw of a dice: head, body, arms, and legs. We played in teams of four and the winning team was the one who completed all four Teddies first. We also had a disco evening and the PTA organised the summer sports day which was held on the school field beside the school. I offered to help with the candy floss stall as one of the mums knew where to hire a candy floss machine. You can imagine how difficult was to add sugar and food colouring as the machine wizzed away, making the ‘floss’. In addition you spent most of the time batting the wasps away!





Mick’s brother Steve had been appointed as executor of their mother’s will and he rang us to let us know that their family home in Dagmar Road had been sold.  Aunty Lil had found a ground floor apartment which suited her in Lords’s Wood near where Steve and Joy lived. The money from the house sale (I believe it was £11,000) was to be shared by the two brothers.  This seemed amazing to me, I had never received such a very large sum ever before! We realised we could pay off our Barclaycard which we had been so excited about opening and still have money to buy a small new car, our first ever! Whenever this happens, there is always a feeling of guilt and sadness as to how this money came to us. 

When the money was in the bank we visited Crawley Down garage.  It was a British Leyland garage and we chose a brand new, blue, Austin Allegro, trading in our  old Renault 6. This would be the car we would take on our road trip to Sweden!





At the beginning of May the school held the traditional village May Day celebration and a maypole was erected in the school playground. The girls danced around holding the coloured ribbons. A May Queen had been chosen in school for the ceremony and a traditional Tipteerers Mummer’s play was performed by the schoolchildren. That year Richard played the part of Jack in the Green. It was wonderful to see these traditions still being upheld in a village in West Sussex.

Also in May that year was the village stoolball competition.  Stoolball originated in Sussex in the 15th century.  It is a cross between cricket and rounders. I don’t know if it still continues in Crawley Down, but each organisation in the village that year entered a team in the competition. Mick and I played for the Junior School PTA. Even the headmistress played and another couple of staff members. It was great fun and very competitive! Our team were runners up.

Half term arrived at the end of May and we set off on great Swedish adventure. With the car safely stowed below deck we set off to find our cabin.  It had two sets of bunk beds. We then went to the cafeteria and it was the first time we had seen a smorgasbord. What I really remember was that it was the first time we had a portion of chips on our plate with mayonnaise to dip them in. Delicious!

After we docked in Gothenburg we set off following the directions we had been given to find Varmland.  There were no Satnavs in those days so we used our roadmap of Sweden and the instructions we had been given.  I knew there was a good reason for getting my map reader badge when I was in the Girl Guides!










About the Author




Eileen Ferguson Smith is a woman who has seen it all! Not content with being born in the forties, she was also a child in the fifties, a teenager in the sixties and a mother of two in the seventies. Eileen danced her way through the eighties and started all over again in the nineties. Marvellous!
